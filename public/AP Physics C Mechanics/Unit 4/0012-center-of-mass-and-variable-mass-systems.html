Center of Mass and Variable-Mass Systems

Center of mass becomes an integral for continuous objects. And a system whose own mass changes — a rocket burning fuel — needs the product rule to explain why it accelerates at all.

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Center of Mass, Discrete and Continuous For discrete point masses: xcm = Σmixi / Σmi, exactly as in Physics 1. For a continuous mass distribution, the sum becomes an integral over infinitesimal mass elements dm: xcm = (1/M) ∫x dm, where the total mass M = ∫dm. This is the same "sum becomes integral" move Unit 5 will use again for moment of inertia.
Example: center of mass of a non-uniform rod A rod of length L = 2 m has linear density λ(x) = 3x kg/m (heavier toward the far end), measured from x = 0. Total mass: M = ∫₀² 3x dx = 1.5x²|₀² = 6 kg. Center of mass: xcm = (1/6)∫₀² x(3x)dx = (1/6)∫₀² 3x² dx = (1/6)(x³|₀²) = 8/6 = 1.33 m — past the geometric midpoint (1 m), since more mass sits toward the heavier end.
Variable-Mass Systems: A Rocket, Qualitatively A rocket ejects burned fuel at exhaust speed ve relative to the rocket, continuously losing mass. Because p = mv and m itself changes, the product rule gives dp/dt = m(dv/dt) + v(dm/dt) — an extra term beyond ordinary F = ma. That extra term produces a thrust force, magnitude ve|dm/dt|, even with zero external force. Working through the full separable differential equation this sets up gives the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, Δv = ve ln(m₀/mf) — presented here qualitatively, as the payoff of the reasoning rather than a derivation to reproduce.
Why F = ma alone can't describe a rocket F = ma silently assumes m is constant — exactly the assumption Lesson 10 flagged as the limitation of that form of the second law. A rocket accelerates by throwing mass backward, converting the system's own momentum bookkeeping (not an external push) into forward motion. F = dp/dt, applied carefully with the product rule, is the only form of the second law equipped to handle it.

1. For a continuous object, the discrete sum xcm = Σmixi/Σmi generalizes to which integral?

Each infinitesimal mass element dm at position x replaces a discrete m_i x_i term, and the sum becomes an integral divided by the total mass M = ∫dm.

2. Why does a rocket accelerate even though no external force pushes it forward (in deep space, far from any gravity)?

Rockets work by momentum exchange with their own ejected exhaust, not by pushing against an external medium — which is exactly why they function in the vacuum of space.

3. In the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation Δv = ve ln(m₀/mf), what does m₀/mf represent?

m₀ is the rocket's total starting mass (structure plus fuel) and m_f is what remains after burning fuel — a larger ratio (more fuel burned relative to what's left) means more Δv.

4. Fill in the blank: expanding d(mv)/dt into m(dv/dt) + v(dm/dt) uses the ___, since both m and v can depend on time.

The product rule from AP Calc Unit 2 is exactly what's needed to differentiate p = mv when neither factor is held constant.
