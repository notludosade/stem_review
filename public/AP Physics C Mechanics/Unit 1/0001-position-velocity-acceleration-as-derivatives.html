Position, Velocity, and Acceleration as Derivatives

AP Physics 1 gave you four kinematic equations to memorize. This course gives you one idea instead: velocity and acceleration are nothing but derivatives of position, taken once and twice.

Contents Lesson 2 → Glossary →

In AP Physics 1, average velocity was Δx/Δt and instantaneous velocity was "the value the average approaches as Δt shrinks to zero." That description was exact — it just didn't have a name yet. AP Calculus BC gave it one: a limit of a difference quotient as the interval shrinks to zero is a derivative. Everything in this unit is that idea, applied to motion.

Sign Convention (used for the rest of this course) Unless a problem says otherwise, positive x points right (for horizontal motion) or up (for vertical motion), matching standard Cartesian axes. Position, velocity, and acceleration are all signed quantities — a negative value means "in the negative direction," not "smaller." This convention is applied consistently in every later unit, including rotation (Unit 5) and gravitation (Unit 7).
Velocity and Acceleration, Defined via Derivatives For position x(t), velocity is v(t) = dx/dt — the instantaneous rate of change of position. Acceleration is a(t) = dv/dt = d²x/dt² — the instantaneous rate of change of velocity, equivalently the second derivative of position. Both are exact at every single instant t, not averaged over an interval.
Why this replaces the kinematic equations The four constant-acceleration equations from AP Physics 1 (v = v₀ + at, Δx = v₀t + ½at², and so on) are not new physics — they're what you get from solving dx/dt = v and dv/dt = a in the one special case where a is constant. This course drops the "constant acceleration" restriction entirely: the derivative definitions above work for any x(t), whether acceleration is constant, changing, or a mess.
Example A particle's position is x(t) = 3t² − 2t + 1 (meters, t in seconds). By the power rule (AP Calc Unit 2, Lesson 4): v(t) = dx/dt = 6t − 2 m/s, and a(t) = dv/dt = 6 m/s² (constant — this is one of those special cases). At t = 2 s: v(2) = 6(2) − 2 = 10 m/s, a(2) = 6 m/s².
Example: velocity that changes sign x(t) = t³ − 6t² + 9t (meters). v(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3). The particle is momentarily at rest (v = 0) at t = 1 s and t = 3 s — its velocity is positive before t = 1, negative between t = 1 and t = 3, and positive again after t = 3. No kinematic equation from Physics 1 could describe this motion at all, since acceleration a(t) = 6t − 12 is not constant.

Higher-order derivatives, briefly

Nothing stops the chain from continuing: the derivative of acceleration, da/dt = d³x/dt³, is called jerk — the rate at which acceleration itself changes (why a smoothly-accelerating elevator feels comfortable and a jerky one doesn't). This course rarely needs it, but it's worth knowing the pattern extends as far as a function stays differentiable.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. If x(t) = 4t³ − t, what is v(t)?

Power rule term by term: d/dt(4t³) = 12t², d/dt(−t) = −1. So v(t) = 12t² − 1.

2. A particle's velocity is v(t) = 2t − 8. At what time is the particle's acceleration equal to zero?

a(t) = dv/dt = 2 m/s², a constant. It's never zero at any time — the mistake is confusing "when is velocity zero" (t = 4 s) with "when is acceleration zero."

3. For x(t) = t³ − 6t² + 9t (the second example above), what is the particle's acceleration at t = 1 s, the moment it's momentarily at rest?

a(t) = 6t − 12. At t = 1: a(1) = 6 − 12 = −6 m/s². Velocity and acceleration are independent quantities — an object can be momentarily at rest while still accelerating, exactly like a ball thrown straight up at its peak.

4. Fill in the blank: acceleration a(t) = d²x/dt² is the ___ derivative of position with respect to time.

Velocity is the first derivative of position; acceleration is the derivative of velocity, making it the second derivative of position.
