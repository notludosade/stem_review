Gravitational Potential Energy and Escape Velocity

U = mgh only works near Earth's surface. The true, general gravitational potential energy comes from integrating F(r) out to infinity — an improper integral, straight out of AP Calc Unit 6.

← Lesson 20 Unit 8, Lesson 22 → Contents Glossary →

Building U(r), with U(∞) = 0 as the Reference U(r) − U(∞) = −∫∞r F(r′) dr′ = −∫∞r (−GMm/r′²) dr′. Evaluating this improper integral (AP Calc Unit 6, Lesson 9) and setting U(∞) = 0: U(r) = −GMm/r — always negative, approaching zero only as r → ∞.
Why U is negative Choosing U(∞) = 0 as the reference means every finite separation has less potential energy than infinite separation — because gravity did positive work pulling the masses together from infinitely far apart. A negative U(r) is the bookkeeping signature of a bound, attractive interaction; it's not a sign error.
Escape Velocity Total mechanical energy E = ½mv² − GMm/r is conserved. An object "just barely" escapes to r → ∞ (arriving there with zero leftover speed) when E = 0: ½mvesc² − GMm/R = 0 → vesc = √(2GM/R). If E < 0, the object is gravitationally bound (falls back or orbits); if E > 0, it escapes with speed to spare.

1. Why is gravitational potential energy U(r) = −GMm/r always negative (with U(∞) = 0)?

With the reference point at infinity, every closer separation is "downhill" in potential energy, making U negative everywhere finite.

2. An object has total mechanical energy E = 0 relative to a planet. What does this mean?

E = 0 is exactly the escape-velocity boundary case: KE and the (negative) potential energy exactly cancel, so the object reaches infinity with no kinetic energy left over.

3. A planet has mass M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg and radius R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m (G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²). What is its escape velocity, to the nearest km/s?

v_esc = √(2GM/R) = √(2(6.674e-11)(6e24)/6.4e6) ≈ √(1.252e8) ≈ 11,190 m/s ≈ 11 km/s — Earth's actual escape velocity.

4. Fill in the blank: building U(r) by integrating force out to r = ∞ is an example of an ___ integral, since one bound is infinite.

An improper integral (AP Calc BC Unit 6, Lesson 9) is exactly what's needed whenever a bound of integration is infinite, evaluated as a limit.
