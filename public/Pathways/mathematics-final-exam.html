1. What does the completeness axiom guarantee about ℝ that ℚ lacks?

ℚ fails completeness — e.g. {x ∈ ℚ : x² < 2} is bounded above in ℚ but has no rational supremum, since √2 is irrational.

2. Fill in the blank: {x ∈ ℚ : x² < 2} has no supremum within ℚ because ___ is irrational.

The least upper bound of this set would have to be √2, which isn't rational — this is the classic example of ℚ's incompleteness.

3. A sequence (aₙ) converges to L means:

The quantifier order matters: ε comes first (any tolerance), then N is found in response to it — reversing the order gives a different, weaker statement.

4. Fill in the blank: every convergent sequence is ___ (a property that convergence implies, but does not imply convergence back).

Convergent ⟹ bounded, but not conversely — e.g. (−1)ⁿ is bounded but doesn't converge.

5. In ℝ with the standard metric, which of these sets is clopen (both open and closed)?

In the standard topology on ℝ, only ∅ and ℝ itself are clopen — (0,1) is open only, [0,1] and {0} are closed only.

6. By the Heine-Borel theorem, a subset of ℝ is compact if and only if it is:

Heine-Borel is specific to ℝⁿ — closed and bounded together are exactly equivalent to compact there (this equivalence fails in general metric spaces).

7. f is continuous at c means:

All three conditions are required together — either one alone is not sufficient for continuity.

8. The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that a continuous function on a closed interval [a, b]:

Both closedness and boundedness of the interval matter here — the theorem can fail on an open interval like (a, b).

9. Which statement is TRUE?

Differentiability is the strictly stronger condition — it always implies continuity, but the converse fails.

10. Which of these functions is continuous everywhere but NOT differentiable at x = 0?

The absolute value function has a sharp corner at x = 0 — the left and right derivatives disagree (−1 vs. 1) — despite being perfectly continuous there.

11. A bounded function f is Riemann integrable on [a, b] if and only if:

This is the Riemann/Darboux criterion — continuity is sufficient but not necessary for integrability, since integrable functions can have discontinuities (as long as they're not too severe).

12. Fill in the blank: every continuous function on [a, b] is ___.

Continuity on a closed bounded interval is sufficient to guarantee Riemann integrability.

13. Which statement is TRUE about uniform vs. pointwise convergence of a sequence of functions?

Uniform convergence is the strictly stronger condition — the classic counterexample fₙ(x) = xⁿ on [0,1] converges pointwise but not uniformly.

14. Fill in the blank: if fₙ → f uniformly and every fₙ is continuous, then f is ___.

Uniform convergence preserves continuity — this can fail under merely pointwise convergence.

15. Within its radius of convergence R (i.e., |x − c| < R), a power series converges:

Absolute convergence holds everywhere strictly inside the radius of convergence — the boundary |x − c| = R is where behavior gets ambiguous.

16. Fill in the blank: at the endpoints of the interval of convergence (|x − c| = R), a power series may converge or may ___ — it must be checked separately at each endpoint.

Convergence at the two endpoints is independent and not guaranteed by the radius alone.

17. The triangle inequality for a metric d states:

Going directly from x to z is never longer than any detour through y — this, plus non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, and symmetry, define a metric.

18. Fill in the blank: in any metric space, every convergent sequence is also ___ (though the converse requires completeness of the space).

Convergent implies Cauchy always; Cauchy implies convergent only in a complete metric space.

19. Clairaut's theorem guarantees that the mixed partial derivatives ∂²f/∂x∂y and ∂²f/∂y∂x are equal, PROVIDED:

Clairaut's theorem has a real hypothesis — without continuity of the mixed partials nearby, equality can actually fail.

20. Fill in the blank: the total derivative of f: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ at a point, if it exists, is represented by the ___ matrix.

The Jacobian collects all the first-order partial derivatives into one matrix, generalizing the single-variable derivative.

21. Which set has Lebesgue measure zero?

Every countable set has Lebesgue measure zero, and ℚ is countable — this includes ℚ even though it's dense in ℝ.

22. Fill in the blank: the Cantor set has Lebesgue measure zero but is ___ in cardinality.

Measure zero and cardinality are independent notions of "size" — the Cantor set is a classic example of a measure-zero set that's still uncountable.

23. Compared to the Riemann integral, the Lebesgue integral can integrate a strictly larger class of functions, such as:

The Dirichlet function is discontinuous everywhere, which defeats Riemann integration, but it's Lebesgue integrable (with integral 0, since ℚ has measure zero).

24. Fill in the blank: the Lebesgue integral is built by partitioning the ___ of the function, rather than the domain the way the Riemann integral does.

Slicing the range (values) instead of the domain (input) is exactly what lets Lebesgue integration handle wilder, more discontinuous functions.
