Coupling, Cohesion, and Separation of Concerns

The two properties that predict how painful a codebase is to change, and the principle that keeps both in check.

← Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 15 promised that Lesson 16 would explain what makes a monolith "well-structured" rather than just one large tangle of code. Two properties — coupling and cohesion — largely determine that, regardless of whether a system is a monolith or split into microservices.

Coupling: how entangled two components are

Why low coupling is the goal Coupling measures how much one component depends on the internal details of another. High coupling means changing one component frequently forces changes in another, unrelated-seeming one too — exactly the kind of hidden dependency Unit 4's Singleton discussion warned about. Low coupling means components can be changed, tested, and understood largely on their own.
Tightly coupled 
class OrderProcessor:
    def process(self, order):
        db = MySQLDatabase("prod-server", "orders_db")
        db.execute(f"UPDATE inventory SET qty=qty-1 WHERE id={order.item_id}")
        # OrderProcessor now knows MySQL syntax, the server address,
        # and the exact schema of the inventory table
Loosely coupled 
class OrderProcessor:
    def __init__(self, inventory):
        self._inventory = inventory
    def process(self, order):
        self._inventory.decrement(order.item_id)
        # OrderProcessor only knows "inventory can decrement an item" —
        # nothing about how, or what database is behind it
Why the second version is better Changing the database technology, or testing OrderProcessor with a fake inventory (Unit 3's testability discussion again), no longer requires touching OrderProcessor at all — it only knows about a small, stable interface, not the implementation behind it.

Cohesion: whether a component does one clear job

Why high cohesion is the goal Cohesion measures how closely the responsibilities inside a single component relate to each other. A highly cohesive class does one clear job, with all its pieces working toward that same purpose. A low-cohesion class bundles unrelated responsibilities together, which makes it harder to name, harder to understand, and more likely that an unrelated change forces you to touch it anyway.
Example A UserManager class that handles authentication, sends marketing emails, and generates PDF invoices has low cohesion — those three responsibilities have nothing to do with each other, and a change to invoice formatting risks breaking authentication code that happens to live in the same class. Splitting it into Authenticator, EmailSender, and InvoiceGenerator gives each class one clear, cohesive job.

Separation of concerns: the principle behind both

Why this is the unifying idea Separation of concerns is the broader design principle that low coupling and high cohesion both serve: each part of a system should be responsible for one distinct concern, with clear boundaries between concerns. Coupling and cohesion are the two measurable signals that tell you whether separation of concerns is actually being achieved — high cohesion means each piece has one job, low coupling means the pieces don't secretly depend on each other's internals to do it.

Why this connects back to Unit 5's whole thread

This is what "well-structured" means at every scale A monolith with high coupling and low cohesion is genuinely painful to work in, regardless of it technically being "just one codebase" — while a well-separated monolith, with clear internal boundaries, can stay maintainable far longer than Lesson 15 might suggest, and is also what makes splitting it into microservices later (if a real constraint ever demands it) tractable instead of a total rewrite.
