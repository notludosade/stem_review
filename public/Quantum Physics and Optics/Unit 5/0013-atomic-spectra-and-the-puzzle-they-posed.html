Atomic Spectra and the Puzzle They Posed

Heat a gas until it glows and look at its light through a prism: instead of a smooth rainbow, you get a handful of sharp, isolated colored lines — a fingerprint no classical model of the atom could explain.

← Unit 4, Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Emission and Absorption Spectra An emission spectrum is the set of bright lines a heated, excited gas emits against a dark background. An absorption spectrum is the set of dark lines missing from a continuous background after light passes through a cooler gas. For a given element, both spectra show lines at exactly the same wavelengths — and every element's set of lines is unique, like a fingerprint.
Why this stumped classical physics completely A classical model of the atom — a negative electron orbiting a positive nucleus, held by Coulomb attraction, the way a planet orbits the sun — predicts disaster on two fronts. First, an orbiting (and therefore accelerating) charge should continuously radiate electromagnetic energy at whatever frequency it circles the nucleus, causing it to spiral inward and collide with the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second — atoms, on this picture, should not be stable at all. Second, as the electron spirals in, its orbital frequency should change continuously, so the radiated light should form a smooth, continuous smear of every frequency, not a handful of sharp, discrete lines. Neither prediction survives contact with even the simplest experiment.
Example: an equation that worked before anyone knew why Decades before any explanation existed, the empirical Rydberg formula correctly predicted hydrogen's spectral line wavelengths: 1/λ = R(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²), with R ≈ 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹. It fit the data essentially perfectly — but nobody could say why it worked until Bohr (next lesson).

1. What does a classical (planetary) model of the atom predict that directly contradicts atoms being stable at all?

Classical electromagnetism requires any accelerating charge to radiate energy continuously, which would cause an orbiting electron to spiral inward almost immediately — atoms should collapse, but they don't.

2. A classical model also predicts what kind of emission spectrum, in contradiction with what's actually observed?

As the electron's orbital frequency continuously changes while spiraling in, classical theory predicts continuously varying radiated frequency — a smear, not the sharp discrete lines actually observed.

3. An element's emission and absorption spectra, measured separately, show bright lines and dark lines respectively. How do their wavelengths compare?

The same set of wavelengths appears as bright lines in emission and dark lines in absorption — both come from the same underlying transitions.

4. Fill in the blank: the empirical equation that correctly predicted hydrogen's spectral line wavelengths, decades before anyone could explain why it worked, is called the ___.

The Rydberg formula fit hydrogen's spectrum precisely, but had no theoretical justification until Bohr's model.
