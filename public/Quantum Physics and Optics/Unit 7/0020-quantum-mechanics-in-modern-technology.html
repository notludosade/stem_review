Where Quantum Mechanics Shows Up in Modern Technology

None of this stayed confined to physics classrooms — quantized energy levels, tunneling, and wave-particle duality are the working principles behind lasers, semiconductors, MRI machines, and the transistors in every phone.

← Lesson 19 Lesson 21 → Contents Glossary →

Quantum Mechanics, Already in Your Pocket Semiconductors and transistors rely on quantized electron energy bands in solids (an extension of Lesson 17's discrete-levels idea) to control conductivity. Lasers use stimulated emission between quantized atomic energy levels (Unit 5). LEDs emit photons from electrons crossing a semiconductor's quantized band gap. MRI machines exploit quantized nuclear spin states in a magnetic field. Scanning tunneling microscopes use Lesson 19's tunneling current for atomic-scale imaging. Electron microscopes use Unit 4's electron de Broglie wavelength — thousands of times shorter than visible light — for far higher resolution than any light microscope could achieve.
Why this closing survey matters Every device listed above depends on a genuinely quantum effect with no classical explanation — this isn't a coincidental list of trivia, it's the practical payoff of this course's entire arc, from Unit 1's wave optics through the Schrödinger equation, now grounded in devices already sitting in most students' pockets and homes.
Example: STM resolution from tunneling's sensitivity Because tunneling current depends exponentially on tip-to-surface distance, even a change of a fraction of a nanometer produces a large, easily measured change in current — which is exactly what lets an STM map out individual atoms on a surface.

1. An electron microscope achieves far higher resolution than a light microscope mainly because:

Since resolution is limited by wavelength, and electrons' de Broglie wavelength is far shorter than visible light's, electron microscopes resolve much finer detail.

2. A scanning tunneling microscope images individual atoms by measuring how a tunneling current changes with:

Tunneling current's exponential sensitivity to barrier width (Lesson 19) means tiny distance changes produce large, measurable current changes.

3. A laser's light comes from electrons undergoing:

Lasers rely on the same quantized-energy-level transitions introduced with the Bohr model in Unit 5, now driven by stimulated emission.

4. Fill in the blank: the quantum effect a scanning tunneling microscope's imaging technique is directly named after is ___.

The "T" in STM stands for tunneling — the same exponential barrier-crossing effect from Lesson 19.
