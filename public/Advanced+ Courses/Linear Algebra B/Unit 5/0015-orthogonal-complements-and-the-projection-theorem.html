Orthogonal Complements and the Projection Theorem

Every subspace splits V into two orthogonal pieces, and every vector's closest point in a subspace turns out to be exactly the piece that lands inside it. This lesson proves both, using Gram-Schmidt as the construction tool.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

1. The orthogonal complement

Definition — Orthogonal Complement For a subspace W of V, the orthogonal complement is W⊥ = {v ∈ V : ⟨v,w⟩ = 0 for every w ∈ W}.

W⊥ is itself a subspace (0 ∈ W⊥ trivially, and closure under addition/scalar multiplication follows directly from bilinearity of the inner product — the same style of check as every subspace test so far).

2. V splits exactly into W and its complement

Theorem — V = W ⊕ W⊥ For a finite-dimensional inner product space V and subspace W: W ∩ W⊥ = {0}, and W + W⊥ = V. Equivalently, every v ∈ V has a unique decomposition v = w + u with w ∈ W, u ∈ W⊥.
Proof Take a basis of W and run Gram-Schmidt (Lesson 14) to get an orthogonal basis {w₁,…,wm} of W. Extend to a basis of all of V and run Gram-Schmidt on the full extended list — since w₁,…,wm are already orthogonal, they pass through Gram-Schmidt unchanged, producing an orthogonal basis {w₁,…,wm,z₁,…,zn−m} of V. By construction, each zi is orthogonal to every vector processed before it — including all of w₁,…,wm — so zi ∈ W⊥ for every i.

W⊥ = span{z₁,…,zn−m}: the ⊇ direction is immediate from the previous paragraph. For ⊆, take any v ∈ W⊥ and write it in the full orthogonal basis: v = a₁w₁+⋯+amwm+b₁z₁+⋯+bn−mzn−m. Take the inner product with wk: orthogonality kills every term except ak⟨wk,wk⟩, so ⟨v,wk⟩ = ak⟨wk,wk⟩. But v ∈ W⊥ and wk ∈ W means ⟨v,wk⟩ = 0, forcing ak = 0 for every k. So v = b₁z₁+⋯+bn−mzn−m ∈ span{z's}.

Since W = span{w's} and W⊥ = span{z's} together span V (the full extended basis), W+W⊥ = V. And if v ∈ W ∩ W⊥, then v ∈ W⊥ forces ⟨v,w⟩=0 for every w ∈ W — in particular for w=v itself (since v ∈ W too) — giving ⟨v,v⟩=0, which by positive-definiteness means v=0. So W ∩ W⊥ = {0}. ∎

3. The Projection Theorem

Theorem — Projection Onto a Subspace For v ∈ V and subspace W, write v = w + u (the unique decomposition from above, w ∈ W, u ∈ W⊥). Then w is the unique closest point to v inside W: ‖v−w‖ ≤ ‖v−w′‖ for every w′ ∈ W, with equality only when w′=w.
Proof For any w′ ∈ W: v−w′ = (w−w′) + u, where w−w′ ∈ W (subspace, closed under subtraction) and u ∈ W⊥. Since w−w′ and u are orthogonal, ‖v−w′‖² = ⟨(w−w′)+u, (w−w′)+u⟩ = ‖w−w′‖² + 2⟨w−w′,u⟩ + ‖u‖² = ‖w−w′‖² + ‖u‖² (the cross term vanishes by orthogonality). This is minimized exactly when ‖w−w′‖² = 0, i.e. w′ = w, giving the minimum value ‖u‖². Any other w′ gives a strictly larger ‖v−w′‖² (since ‖w−w′‖²>0 whenever w′≠w). ∎
Why "closest point" and "orthogonal remainder" are the same fact The proof shows the minimizing w′ is characterized by making v−w′ orthogonal to all of W — not by any calculus-style derivative argument. This is the exact fact behind least-squares regression: the "error" vector between data and the best-fit prediction is always orthogonal to the subspace of predictions being searched over.
Worked Example — Projecting onto a Plane in ℝ³ Let W = span{u₁,u₂} with u₁=(1,1,0), u₂=(1/2,−1/2,1) (Lesson 14's orthogonal basis), and v=(2,0,0). The projection formula for an orthogonal basis is projW(v) = (⟨v,u₁⟩/⟨u₁,u₁⟩)u₁ + (⟨v,u₂⟩/⟨u₂,u₂⟩)u₂. ⟨v,u₁⟩=2, ⟨u₁,u₁⟩=2, so the first coefficient is 1. ⟨v,u₂⟩=1, ⟨u₂,u₂⟩=3/2, so the second coefficient is 2/3. projW(v) = 1·(1,1,0) + (2/3)(1/2,−1/2,1) = (4/3, 2/3, 2/3).

Check: v − projW(v) = (2/3, −2/3, −2/3). Its inner product with u₁ is 2/3−2/3+0=0, and with u₂ is 1/3+1/3−2/3=0 — orthogonal to all of W, exactly as the Projection Theorem requires.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof that W⊥ = span{z₁,…,zₙ₋ₘ}, why does taking the inner product of v ∈ W⊥ with wₖ force aₖ = 0?

Every cross term in ⟨v,wₖ⟩ vanishes except aₖ⟨wₖ,wₖ⟩; since v∈W⊥ forces this to be 0, and wₖ≠0 makes ⟨wₖ,wₖ⟩≠0, aₖ itself must be 0.

2. In the Projection Theorem's proof, why does ‖v−w′‖² = ‖w−w′‖² + ‖u‖² have no cross term?

w−w′ lies in W (closed under subtraction) while u lies in W⊥ by construction — orthogonality between the two subspaces is exactly what kills the cross term.

3. Fill in the blank: ‖v−w′‖² is minimized exactly when ‖w−w′‖² = ___, i.e. w′ = w.

Since ‖w−w′‖² ≥ 0 always, the smallest possible value of ‖v−w′‖² = ‖w−w′‖²+‖u‖² occurs when the ‖w−w′‖² term drops to exactly 0.

4. In the worked example, what is the coefficient on u₂ in projW(v) for v=(2,0,0)?

⟨v,u₂⟩=1 and ⟨u₂,u₂⟩=3/2, so the coefficient is 1÷(3/2) = 2/3.

5. In the worked example, projW(v) = (___, 2/3, 2/3). Fill in the first coordinate (as a fraction like 4/3).

1·(1,1,0) + (2/3)(1/2,−1/2,1) = (1,1,0)+(1/3,−1/3,2/3) = (4/3, 2/3, 2/3).
