Dimension Formulas for Sums and Intersections

Two subspaces of the same space can be combined two ways — added together, or intersected. Their dimensions turn out to obey an exact formula, proved by building one careful basis.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Given two subspaces U and W of the same vector space V, two new subspaces come for free: their intersection U ∩ W, and their sum U + W = {u + w : u ∈ U, w ∈ W} — every vector reachable by adding one vector from each. This lesson proves both really are subspaces, then proves the dimension formula tying all four numbers — dim(U), dim(W), dim(U∩W), dim(U+W) — together exactly.

1. U ∩ W and U + W are subspaces

Fact — Both Are Subspaces For subspaces U, W of V, both U ∩ W and U + W are subspaces of V.
Proof U ∩ W: 0 ∈ U and 0 ∈ W (both subspaces), so 0 ∈ U ∩ W. If u₁, u₂ ∈ U ∩ W, then u₁, u₂ ∈ U gives u₁+u₂ ∈ U (U is closed), and likewise u₁+u₂ ∈ W — so u₁+u₂ ∈ U ∩ W. The scalar case is identical. All three subspace-test conditions hold.

U + W: 0 = 0 + 0 ∈ U + W. Given (u₁+w₁) and (u₂+w₂) in U+W, their sum is (u₁+u₂) + (w₁+w₂) — a sum of a U-vector and a W-vector, since U, W are each closed under addition — so it's in U+W. Scaling: c(u+w) = cu + cw, again a U-vector plus a W-vector. All three conditions hold. ∎

2. The dimension formula

Theorem — Dimension of a Sum For finite-dimensional subspaces U, W of V: dim(U + W) = dim(U) + dim(W) − dim(U ∩ W).
Proof Let {x₁,…,xk} be a basis for U ∩ W (dim(U∩W) = k). Extend it (Lesson 3's Extension Theorem) to a basis {x₁,…,xk, u₁,…,up} of U (so dim(U) = k+p), and separately to a basis {x₁,…,xk, w₁,…,wq} of W (dim(W) = k+q). Claim: {x₁,…,xk, u₁,…,up, w₁,…,wq} — k+p+q vectors — is a basis for U+W.

Spans U+W: any element of U+W is u+w for some u ∈ U, w ∈ W. Write u using U's basis and w using W's basis; adding the two expressions writes u+w as a combination of all k+p+q vectors together.

Independent: suppose a₁x₁+⋯+akxk + b₁u₁+⋯+bpup + c₁w₁+⋯+cqwq = 0. Let v = a₁x₁+⋯+akxk+b₁u₁+⋯+bpup — this is in U (a combination of U's basis vectors). The equation says v = −(c₁w₁+⋯+cqwq), which is in W. So v ∈ U ∩ W, meaning v is also expressible using {x₁,…,xk} alone. But v already has a representation in terms of U's basis {x₁,…,xk,u₁,…,up} — and since U's basis representation is unique (Lesson 3), comparing the two forces every bi = 0. With the b's gone, the original equation reduces to a₁x₁+⋯+akxk + c₁w₁+⋯+cqwq = 0 — a dependency among W's own basis {x₁,…,xk,w₁,…,wq}, which is independent, so every ai = 0 and every cj = 0 too. Every coefficient is 0 — the combined set is independent.

So dim(U+W) = k+p+q = (k+p) + (k+q) − k = dim(U) + dim(W) − dim(U∩W). ∎
Why the shared basis vectors x₁,…,xk are the whole trick Starting the construction from a basis of the overlap U ∩ W, rather than building U's and W's bases independently, is what prevents double-counting. Every vector genuinely shared between U and W gets counted once (among the x's); the u's and w's cover exactly what's left over in each subspace separately. That's the entire content of the "−dim(U∩W)" correction term.
Worked Example — Two Planes Through the Origin In ℝ³, let U = span{(1,0,0), (0,1,0)} (the xy-plane) and W = span{(0,1,0), (0,0,1)} (the yz-plane). U ∩ W = span{(0,1,0)} (any vector in both planes has first and third coordinates 0), so dim(U∩W) = 1. dim(U) = dim(W) = 2. U + W contains (1,0,0), (0,1,0), and (0,0,1) — the full standard basis of ℝ³ — so U+W = ℝ³ and dim(U+W) = 3. Checking the formula: dim(U)+dim(W)−dim(U∩W) = 2+2−1 = 3. ✓.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does U + W need the subspace test proof to check "sum of two elements of U+W is again in U+W" rather than something more specific?

Elements of U+W are exactly u+w pairs, so proving closure means showing (u₁+w₁)+(u₂+w₂) can be regrouped into a valid U-vector plus a valid W-vector — which works precisely because U and W are each individually closed.

2. In the dimension formula's proof, why does the construction start from a basis of U ∩ W rather than starting separately with bases of U and W?

Building outward from a shared basis of the overlap is exactly what makes the "−dim(U∩W)" correction term appear naturally — every overlap vector is counted exactly once.

3. Fill in the blank: the independence half of the proof relies on Lesson 3's theorem that every basis representation is ___, to force the b coefficients to 0.

Comparing v's two representations — one via U's basis, one via {x₁,…,xₖ} alone — only forces the b coefficients to vanish because U's basis representation is guaranteed unique.

4. In the worked example (U = xy-plane, W = yz-plane in ℝ³), what is dim(U ∩ W)?

A vector is in both planes exactly when its first and third coordinates are both 0 — that's the y-axis, a 1-dimensional subspace.

5. Using the formula, if dim(U) = 4, dim(W) = 3, and dim(U∩W) = 2, what is dim(U+W)?

dim(U+W) = dim(U) + dim(W) − dim(U∩W) = 4 + 3 − 2 = 5.
