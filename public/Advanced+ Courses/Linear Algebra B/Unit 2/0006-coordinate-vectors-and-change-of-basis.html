Coordinate Vectors and Change of Basis

Every n-dimensional vector space, no matter how abstract, turns into ordinary Fⁿ once a basis is chosen. This lesson proves that translation is a genuine isomorphism, then finds the matrix that translates between two different choices of basis.

← Lesson 5 Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 3 proved every vector in V has a unique representation relative to a basis B. That representation has a name — the coordinate vector — and this lesson proves it does far more than just organize a computation: it sets up an exact correspondence between V and Fⁿ, valid for polynomials and matrices exactly as much as for ℝⁿ.

1. The coordinate map

Definition — Coordinate Vector For a basis B = {v₁,…,vn} of V and a vector v = c₁v₁+⋯+cnvn, the coordinate vector of v relative to B is [v]B = (c₁,…,cn) ∈ Fⁿ. Lesson 3 guarantees this is well-defined — exactly one tuple works for each v.
Theorem — The Coordinate Map Is an Isomorphism The map φB: V → Fⁿ, φB(v) = [v]B, is linear and bijective.
Proof Linear: write u = a₁v₁+⋯+anvn and w = b₁v₁+⋯+bnvn, so [u]B = (a₁,…,an) and [w]B = (b₁,…,bn). Then u+w = (a₁+b₁)v₁+⋯+(an+bn)vn, so [u+w]B = (a₁+b₁,…,an+bn) = [u]B+[w]B. Similarly [cu]B = c[u]B, directly from cu = ca₁v₁+⋯+canvn.

Bijective: define ψ: Fⁿ → V by ψ(c₁,…,cn) = c₁v₁+⋯+cnvn. By construction φB(ψ(c₁,…,cn)) = (c₁,…,cn) (existence half of Lesson 3's theorem), and ψ(φB(v)) = v (rebuilding v from its own coordinates). So ψ is a two-sided inverse of φB, making φB bijective. ∎
Why "isomorphism" is the right word A linear bijection is exactly what it means for two vector spaces to be structurally identical — every vector space fact (spanning, independence, subspaces) translates back and forth through φB without loss. This is the precise sense in which "P₂(ℝ) is basically ℝ³" or "the 2×2 matrices are basically ℝ⁴": pick a basis, and φB makes the resemblance exact, not just a vague analogy.

2. Changing which basis you're using

A vector's coordinate vector depends entirely on which basis was chosen — the same v gets a different tuple relative to a different basis. The change-of-basis matrix converts one basis's coordinates directly into another's, without ever going back through v itself.

Definition — Change-of-Basis Matrix For bases B = {v₁,…,vn} and B′ = {v₁′,…,vn′} of V, the change-of-basis matrix PB←B′ is the n×n matrix whose j-th column is [vj′]B — the coordinates of B′'s j-th vector, expressed in B.
Theorem — What PB←B′ Does For every v ∈ V, [v]B = PB←B′ [v]B′.
Proof Let [v]B′ = (c₁,…,cn), i.e. v = c₁v₁′+⋯+cnvn′. Apply φB and use its linearity (proved above): [v]B = [c₁v₁′+⋯+cnvn′]B = c₁[v₁′]B + ⋯ + cn[vn′]B. The right-hand side is exactly c₁ times column 1 of PB←B′, plus ⋯ plus cn times column n — which is precisely the matrix-vector product PB←B′(c₁,…,cn) = PB←B′[v]B′. ∎
Worked Example — Converting Standard Coordinates into a Tilted Basis Let B = {(1,1), (1,−1)} and B′ = {(1,0), (0,1)} (the standard basis) in ℝ². Build PB←B′: column 1 is [(1,0)]B, found by solving (1,0) = a(1,1)+b(1,−1) → a+b=1, a−b=0 → a=b=1/2. Column 2 is [(0,1)]B, from (0,1)=a(1,1)+b(1,−1) → a+b=0, a−b=1 → a=1/2, b=−1/2. So PB←B′ = [[1/2, 1/2], [1/2, −1/2]].

For v = (3, 5), [v]B′ = (3, 5) (B′ is the standard basis, so coordinates match components exactly). Then [v]B = PB←B′(3,5) = (1/2·3 + 1/2·5, 1/2·3 − 1/2·5) = (4, −1). Check directly: 4(1,1) + (−1)(1,−1) = (4,4) + (−1,1) = (3,5) = v. ✓.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof that φB is bijective, what role does the map ψ(c₁,…,cₙ) = c₁v₁+⋯+cₙvₙ play?

Showing ψ∘φB and φB∘ψ are both identity maps is precisely what it means for φB to have an inverse — and having a two-sided inverse is equivalent to being a bijection.

2. What goes in column j of the change-of-basis matrix PB←B′?

Each column records where one of B′'s basis vectors lands when expressed in B's coordinates — that's exactly what the proof's linear combination c₁[v₁′]B+⋯+cₙ[vₙ′]B needs to match a matrix-vector product.

3. Fill in the blank: the proof that [v]B = PB←B′[v]B′ relies directly on the ___ of the coordinate map φB, applied to v = c₁v₁′+⋯+cₙvₙ′.

Linearity is exactly what turns [c₁v₁′+⋯+cₙvₙ′]B into c₁[v₁′]B+⋯+cₙ[vₙ′]B, the linear combination of P's columns.

4. In the worked example, why does [v]B′ for v=(3,5) simply equal (3,5) itself?

Only for the standard basis do coordinates and components coincide automatically — (3,5) = 3(1,0) + 5(0,1) is trivially true. For any other basis (like B here) this shortcut fails, which is exactly why P is needed to convert.

5. Fill in the blank: in the worked example, [v]B for v=(3,5) was computed as (4, ___).

PB←B′(3,5) = (1/2·3+1/2·5, 1/2·3−1/2·5) = (4, −1), confirmed by 4(1,1) + (−1)(1,−1) = (3,5).
