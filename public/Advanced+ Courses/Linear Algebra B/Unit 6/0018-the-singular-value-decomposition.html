The Singular Value Decomposition

The Spectral Theorem needs a square, self-adjoint operator. Most matrices — including every non-square one — are neither. The SVD gets a diagonalization-like decomposition anyway, by applying the Spectral Theorem one step removed.

← Lesson 17 Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

Linear Algebra A's Lesson 21 used PCA's covariance eigenvectors without connecting them to a general-purpose decomposition. The Singular Value Decomposition is that decomposition — it applies to any m×n matrix, built directly from the Spectral Theorem applied to AᵀA.

The theorem

Theorem — Existence of the SVD Every m×n matrix A factors as A = UΣVᵀ, where U is an m×m orthogonal matrix, V is an n×n orthogonal matrix, and Σ is an m×n matrix that is 0 off its main diagonal, with nonnegative entries σ₁≥σ₂≥⋯≥0 (the singular values) down that diagonal.
Proof (Construction) AᵀA is an n×n matrix, and it's symmetric: (AᵀA)ᵀ = Aᵀ(Aᵀ)ᵀ = AᵀA. By the Spectral Theorem (Lesson 17), AᵀA has an orthonormal eigenbasis {v₁,…,vn} with real eigenvalues λ₁≥⋯≥λn. These eigenvalues are automatically nonnegative: for eigenvector vi, λi = λi⟨vi,vi⟩ = ⟨AᵀAvi,vi⟩ = ⟨Avi,Avi⟩ = ‖Avi‖² ≥ 0. Define σi = √λi ≥ 0.

For each i with σi > 0 (say there are r of them, r = rank(A)), define ui = Avi/σi. These are orthonormal: ⟨ui,uj⟩ = ⟨Avi,Avj⟩/(σiσj) = ⟨AᵀAvi,vj⟩/(σiσj) = λi⟨vi,vj⟩/(σiσj), which is 0 for i≠j (orthonormality of the v's) and σi²/σi² = 1 for i=j. Extend {u₁,…,ur} to a full orthonormal basis {u₁,…,um} of ℝᵐ using the Extension Theorem and Gram-Schmidt (Lessons 3, 14) if r < m.

By construction, Avi = σiui for i ≤ r. For i > r, λi=0 means ‖Avi‖²=λi=0, so Avi=0 too — consistent with a 0 entry in Σ. Assembling U = [u₁ ⋯ um], V = [v₁ ⋯ vn], and Σ with σ₁,…,σr on the diagonal (0 elsewhere): AV = UΣ (column by column, this is exactly Avi=σiui or Avi=0). Since V is orthogonal (Vᵀ=V⁻¹), multiplying both sides by Vᵀ gives A = UΣVᵀ. ∎
Why AᵀA, specifically A itself need not be square, so it has no eigenvalues of its own to appeal to. AᵀA is always square and always symmetric, regardless of A's shape — which is exactly what makes the Spectral Theorem applicable at all. Every step of the SVD after that point is just unpacking what AᵀA's eigen-decomposition implies about A.
Worked Example — SVD of a 3×2 Matrix A = [[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]]. AᵀA = [[1,0,1],[0,1,1]]·[[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]] = [[2,1],[1,2]]. Characteristic polynomial: (2−λ)²−1 = λ²−4λ+3 = (λ−3)(λ−1), eigenvalues 3 and 1 — so σ₁=√3, σ₂=1. Eigenvectors: v₁=(1/√2,1/√2) for λ=3, v₂=(1/√2,−1/√2) for λ=1 (both check directly against AᵀA, exactly as in Lesson 17's style).

u₁ = Av₁/σ₁ = (1/√2,1/√2,√2)/√3 = (1/√6, 1/√6, 2/√6). u₂ = Av₂/σ₂ = (1/√2,−1/√2,0)/1 = (1/√2,−1/√2,0). Extending {u₁,u₂} to an orthonormal basis of ℝ³ gives u₃=(1/√3,1/√3,−1/√3) (orthogonal to both, found via Gram-Schmidt). So A = UΣVᵀ with U = [u₁ u₂ u₃], Σ = [[√3,0],[0,1],[0,0]], V = [v₁ v₂].

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does the SVD construction start from AᵀA rather than A itself?

A itself may not even be square, so "eigenvalues of A" may not make sense — AᵀA is square and symmetric no matter what, making it the natural object the Spectral Theorem can act on.

2. In the proof, why is λᵢ = ‖Avᵢ‖² for each eigenvalue of AᵀA?

Moving one factor of A across the inner product (⟨AᵀAv,v⟩=⟨Av,Av⟩) is exactly the adjoint relationship, and combined with the unit-norm eigenvector this pins λᵢ down as a squared norm — automatically nonnegative.

3. Fill in the blank: for i > r (where r = rank(A)), λᵢ=0 forces ‖Avᵢ‖²=0, meaning Avᵢ = ___.

A norm of 0 forces the vector itself to be the zero vector — matching the 0 entries Σ has past the r-th diagonal position.

4. For A=[[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]], what are the singular values?

AᵀA=[[2,1],[1,2]] has eigenvalues 3 and 1 — the singular values are the square roots, √3 and 1.

5. Fill in the blank: Σ for this example is a 3×2 matrix; the diagonal entry in row 1 is √___ (as an integer under the radical).

σ₁ = √λ₁ = √3, the larger of AᵀA's two eigenvalues.
