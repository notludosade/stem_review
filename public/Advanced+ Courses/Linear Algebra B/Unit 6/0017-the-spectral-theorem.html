The Spectral Theorem

Lesson 11 proved diagonalizability can fail. This lesson proves it never fails for a self-adjoint operator — not only is a full eigenbasis guaranteed, it can always be chosen orthonormal.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 11's defective matrix — a repeated eigenvalue with too few eigenvectors — is impossible for a self-adjoint operator. This lesson proves why, by building an orthonormal eigenbasis one dimension at a time, peeling off an invariant orthogonal complement at each step.

The theorem

Theorem — The Spectral Theorem If T is a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space V, then V has an orthonormal basis consisting entirely of eigenvectors of T. Equivalently, in matrix form: every real symmetric matrix A factors as A = QDQᵀ, with Q orthogonal (Qᵀ=Q⁻¹, columns an orthonormal eigenbasis) and D diagonal (real eigenvalues on the diagonal).
Proof Sketch (induction on dim(V)) Base case, dim(V)=1: any nonzero vector, normalized, is trivially both an eigenvector (every operator on a 1-dimensional space is multiplication by some scalar) and an orthonormal basis.

Inductive step: assume the theorem holds for every self-adjoint operator on a space of dimension less than n; let T be self-adjoint on n-dimensional V. T's characteristic polynomial has a root (every polynomial of degree ≥1 has a root over ℂ), and Lesson 16 proved that root — call it λ₁ — is real; a corresponding eigenvector can be taken to have real entries as well, so normalize it to unit length: e₁, with T(e₁)=λ₁e₁, ‖e₁‖=1.

Let W = span{e₁}⊥ (dimension n−1, Lesson 15). W is T-invariant: for w ∈ W, ⟨T(w),e₁⟩ = ⟨w,T(e₁)⟩ (self-adjointness) = ⟨w,λ₁e₁⟩ = λ₁⟨w,e₁⟩ = λ₁·0 = 0 (since w ∈ e₁⊥). So T(w) ∈ e₁⊥ = W too.

T restricted to W is still self-adjoint (the defining property ⟨T(v),w⟩=⟨v,T(w)⟩ holds for all v,w ∈ V, hence in particular for v,w ∈ W), and W has dimension n−1 — so the inductive hypothesis gives an orthonormal eigenbasis {e₂,…,en} of T restricted to W. Since W is T-invariant, these are genuine eigenvectors of T on all of V too.

{e₁,e₂,…,en} is orthonormal (e₁ ⊥ W by construction, and {e₂,…,en} is orthonormal within W) and consists entirely of eigenvectors of T — an orthonormal eigenbasis of V. ∎
Why this proof structurally cannot hit Lesson 11's defective case At every step, the theorem peels off one confirmed eigenvector and continues on a strictly smaller invariant subspace — it never needs to ask whether enough eigenvectors exist within a single eigenspace, the exact question that broke down for the defective matrix. Self-adjointness guarantees the peeled-off complement is always T-invariant, which is what makes the induction possible at all; that guarantee (Lesson 16's orthogonality theorem) fails for a general operator.
Worked Example — Full Spectral Decomposition Continuing Lesson 16's A=[[5,2],[2,5]], with eigenvalues 7, 3 and eigenvectors (1,1), (1,−1): normalize to e₁=(1/√2, 1/√2), e₂=(1/√2, −1/√2). Then Q = [[1/√2, 1/√2], [1/√2, −1/√2]] and D = [[7,0],[0,3]]. Check Q is orthogonal: QᵀQ = [[1/√2,1/√2],[1/√2,−1/√2]]·[[1/√2,1/√2],[1/√2,−1/√2]] = [[1/2+1/2, 1/2−1/2],[1/2−1/2, 1/2+1/2]] = [[1,0],[0,1]] = I. ✓ A = QDQᵀ reconstructs the original symmetric matrix exactly.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof's inductive step, why is W = span{e₁}⊥ T-invariant?

Self-adjointness is exactly what lets ⟨T(w),e₁⟩ be rewritten in terms of ⟨w,e₁⟩=0, proving T(w) never leaves W.

2. Why can the inductive hypothesis be applied to T restricted to W?

W's dimension is strictly smaller (n−1), and self-adjointness restricts to any T-invariant subspace automatically, satisfying exactly the induction's requirements.

3. Fill in the blank: the Spectral Theorem guarantees not just a basis of eigenvectors, but an ___ one.

Orthonormality — not just linear independence — is the theorem's stronger guarantee, coming directly from Lesson 16's orthogonal-eigenvectors theorem plus the induction's use of an orthogonal complement.

4. In the worked example, what is Q for A=[[5,2],[2,5]]?

Q's columns are the normalized (unit-length) eigenvectors — dividing (1,1) and (1,−1) each by √2.

5. Fill in the blank: checking QᵀQ = I confirms Q is ___ (orthogonal), where I is the identity matrix.

QᵀQ=I is exactly the defining property of an orthogonal matrix — its columns form an orthonormal set. (If the blank confused you, the point is: this equals the identity matrix, confirmed entrywise as 1's on the diagonal.)
