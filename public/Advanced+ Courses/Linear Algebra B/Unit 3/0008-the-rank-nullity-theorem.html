The Rank-Nullity Theorem

Kernel and image trade off against each other exactly — every dimension that collapses into the kernel is a dimension the image can no longer reach. This lesson proves the exact accounting.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Linear Algebra A's Lesson 9 tied rank to pivots and free variables, but never connected it back to the kernel and image as subspaces in their own right. This lesson proves the identity underneath all of it, for any linear map between finite-dimensional spaces — not just matrix row-reduction.

1. Rank and nullity, defined

Definition — Rank and Nullity For a linear map T: V → W with V finite-dimensional, the rank of T is rank(T) = dim(im(T)), and the nullity of T is nullity(T) = dim(ker(T)).

2. The theorem

Theorem — Rank-Nullity For a linear map T: V → W with V finite-dimensional: dim(V) = rank(T) + nullity(T).
Proof Let {u₁,…,uk} be a basis for ker(T) (so nullity(T) = k). Extend it (Lesson 3's Extension Theorem) to a basis {u₁,…,uk, v₁,…,vp} of V, so dim(V) = k+p. The claim is that {T(v₁),…,T(vp)} is a basis for im(T) — proving rank(T) = p, and hence dim(V) = k+p = nullity(T) + rank(T).

Spans im(T): any w ∈ im(T) is w = T(v) for some v ∈ V. Write v = a₁u₁+⋯+akuk+b₁v₁+⋯+bpvp using the extended basis. Apply T: w = T(v) = a₁T(u₁)+⋯+akT(uk)+b₁T(v₁)+⋯+bpT(vp). Since each uᵢ ∈ ker(T), every T(uᵢ) = 0, leaving w = b₁T(v₁)+⋯+bpT(vp) — a combination of T(v₁),…,T(vp) alone.

Independent: suppose c₁T(v₁)+⋯+cpT(vp) = 0. By linearity, T(c₁v₁+⋯+cpvp) = 0, so c₁v₁+⋯+cpvp ∈ ker(T) — expressible as d₁u₁+⋯+dkuk for some scalars di. Rearranged: c₁v₁+⋯+cpvp − d₁u₁−⋯−dkuk = 0 — a dependency among {u₁,…,uk,v₁,…,vp}, the basis of V, which is independent. So every ci = 0 (and every dj = 0). {T(v₁),…,T(vp)} is independent. ∎
Why extending from a kernel basis is exactly the right move Splitting V's basis into "kernel part" (the u's) and "the rest" (the v's) is what makes the accounting exact: the u's contribute nothing to the image (they all vanish under T), while the v's map to a genuine basis of the image — one-to-one, no collisions, by the independence argument above. Every dimension of V ends up counted on exactly one side of the ledger.
Worked Example — T: ℝ³ → ℝ², T(x,y,z) = (x+y, y+z) Kernel: solve x+y=0 and y+z=0, giving y=−x and z=−y=x. So ker(T) = {(x,−x,x) : x ∈ ℝ} = span{(1,−1,1)}, a 1-dimensional subspace — nullity(T) = 1. By Rank-Nullity, rank(T) = dim(ℝ³) − nullity(T) = 3 − 1 = 2. Since im(T) ⊆ ℝ² and rank(T) = 2 = dim(ℝ²), im(T) must be all of ℝ² — confirmed directly, since T(1,0,0)=(1,0) and T(0,0,1)=(0,1) already give a spanning set for ℝ².

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof's "spans im(T)" half, why does every T(uᵢ) term vanish when T is applied to v = a₁u₁+⋯+aₖuₖ+b₁v₁+⋯+bₚvₚ?

The u's were specifically chosen as a basis for ker(T), so by the very definition of kernel, T sends each one to 0 — regardless of the coefficients aᵢ.

2. In the "independent" half of the proof, what happens after c₁v₁+⋯+cₚvₚ is shown to lie in ker(T)?

Rewriting c₁v₁+⋯+cₚvₚ in terms of the kernel's own basis produces an equation entirely in terms of V's full basis — and since that basis is independent, every coefficient (both the c's and the d's) must be 0.

3. Fill in the blank: dim(V) = ___(T) + nullity(T), the Rank-Nullity Theorem.

rank(T) = dim(im(T)) is the other term in the identity, alongside nullity(T) = dim(ker(T)).

4. For T: ℝ³ → ℝ², T(x,y,z) = (x+y, y+z), what is nullity(T)?

Solving x+y=0, y+z=0 gives a 1-parameter family (x,−x,x), a 1-dimensional kernel.

5. Using Rank-Nullity, if dim(V) = 5 and nullity(T) = 3, what is rank(T)?

rank(T) = dim(V) − nullity(T) = 5 − 3 = 2.
