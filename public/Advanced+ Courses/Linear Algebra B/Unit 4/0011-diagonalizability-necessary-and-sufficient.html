Diagonalizability — Necessary and Sufficient

Linear Algebra A's diagonalization recipe worked when it worked, with no explanation of why some matrices resist it entirely. This lesson proves the exact condition — geometric multiplicity must match algebraic multiplicity, for every eigenvalue.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Linear Algebra A's Lesson 18 defined A = PDP⁻¹ and worked examples where P had a full set of independent eigenvectors, without ever addressing what happens when it doesn't. This lesson names the two competing counts — algebraic and geometric multiplicity — and proves exactly when they line up.

1. Two multiplicities, and an inequality between them

Definition — Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicity For eigenvalue λ of T, its algebraic multiplicity is λ's multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial; its geometric multiplicity is dim(Eλ).
Theorem — Geometric ≤ Algebraic For any eigenvalue λ: 1 ≤ geometric multiplicity(λ) ≤ algebraic multiplicity(λ).
Proof Geometric multiplicity ≥ 1 since λ being an eigenvalue means Eλ ≠ {0}. For the upper bound: let m = dim(Eλ), pick a basis {v₁,…,vm} of Eλ, and extend it to a basis {v₁,…,vm,u₁,…,un−m} of V. Since T(vᵢ) = λvᵢ for i ≤ m, the matrix [T] relative to this basis has block form [[λIm, B], [0, C]] — the first m columns are λ times the standard basis vectors, with 0 in every row below m. For a block upper-triangular matrix, det([T]−xI) = det(λIm−xIm)·det(C−xIn−m) = (λ−x)m·det(C−xIn−m) — so (x−λ)m divides the characteristic polynomial. That means the algebraic multiplicity of λ is at least m = geometric multiplicity. ∎

2. When eigenspaces from different eigenvalues never overlap

Lemma — Eigenspace Sums Are Direct If λ₁,…,λk are distinct eigenvalues of T and w₁+⋯+wk = 0 with each wi ∈ Eλᵢ, then every wi = 0.
Proof Suppose not every wi is 0. Discard the zero terms and relabel the remaining nonzero ones v₁,…,vp — each is a genuine eigenvector for a distinct eigenvalue among λ₁,…,λk, and v₁+⋯+vp = 0 is a nontrivial dependency (every coefficient is 1) among eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues — contradicting Lesson 10's independence theorem. So every wi = 0. ∎

Consequence: combining a basis from each of several eigenspaces (for distinct eigenvalues) always produces a linearly independent set — group any dependency by eigenspace, apply the Lemma to force each group's contribution to 0, then use each eigenspace's own basis independence to force every individual coefficient to 0.

3. The diagonalizability criterion

Theorem — Diagonalizability Criterion Suppose T's characteristic polynomial splits into linear factors over F, with distinct eigenvalues λ₁,…,λk and algebraic multiplicities a₁,…,ak (a₁+⋯+ak = n = dim(V)). Then T is diagonalizable if and only if dim(Eλᵢ) = ai for every i.
Proof (⟸) If dim(Eλᵢ) = ai for every i, combine a basis from each Eλᵢ: total size Σaᵢ = n, and by the Lemma's consequence this combined set is independent. n independent vectors in an n-dimensional space form a basis (Lesson 4) — a basis entirely of eigenvectors, so [T] relative to it is diagonal.

(⟹) If T is diagonalizable, some basis B of V consists entirely of eigenvectors. Group B's vectors by eigenvalue: the vectors in B belonging to Eλᵢ are independent (a subset of the independent B) and lie in Eλᵢ, so their count is ≤ dim(Eλᵢ). Summing over all k eigenvalues, n = |B| ≤ Σdim(Eλᵢ) ≤ Σaᵢ = n (the last inequality from the previous theorem). Equality must hold all the way through, which forces dim(Eλᵢ) = aᵢ for every single i — any strict inequality anywhere would make the sum fall short of n. ∎
Worked Example — A Defective Matrix A = [[2,1,0],[0,2,0],[0,0,3]]. Characteristic polynomial: (2−λ)²(3−λ), so λ=2 has algebraic multiplicity 2. Solving (A−2I)v=0: [[0,1,0],[0,0,0],[0,0,1]]v=0 forces v₂=0, v₃=0, leaving v₁ free — dim(E₂) = 1. Since 1 ≠ 2, the criterion fails: A is not diagonalizable. Contrast this with B = [[2,0,0],[0,2,0],[0,0,3]] (already diagonal): here dim(E₂) = 2 = algebraic multiplicity, and B trivially satisfies the criterion (it's its own diagonalization).

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof of Geometric ≤ Algebraic, why does the matrix [T] have block form [[λIm, B], [0, C]] relative to the extended basis?

T(vᵢ)=λvᵢ for the first m (eigenvector) basis vectors is exactly what produces the λIm block and the zero block beneath it — no assumption about diagonalizability is needed.

2. In the Lemma's proof, why does discarding the zero wᵢ terms produce a contradiction with Lesson 10's independence theorem?

After discarding zeros, the surviving vectors are genuine nonzero eigenvectors for pairwise distinct eigenvalues, and their sum being 0 is exactly the nontrivial dependency Lesson 10's theorem rules out.

3. Fill in the blank: for the defective matrix A=[[2,1,0],[0,2,0],[0,0,3]], dim(E₂) = ___, while the algebraic multiplicity of λ=2 is 2.

Solving (A−2I)v=0 leaves only v₁ free, giving a 1-dimensional eigenspace — strictly less than the algebraic multiplicity 2.

4. In the (⟹) direction of the Diagonalizability Criterion's proof, what forces dim(Eλᵢ) = aᵢ for every i, rather than just Σdim(Eλᵢ) = n overall?

A sum of nonnegative "slack" terms (aᵢ − dim(Eλᵢ) ≥ 0) that must total exactly 0 forces every individual slack term to be 0.

5. Fill in the blank: if n independent eigenvectors are combined into a basis of V, the matrix [T] relative to that basis is exactly ___, with the eigenvalues down the diagonal.

Each basis vector being an eigenvector sends column i of [T] to λᵢ times the i-th standard basis vector — exactly a diagonal matrix.
