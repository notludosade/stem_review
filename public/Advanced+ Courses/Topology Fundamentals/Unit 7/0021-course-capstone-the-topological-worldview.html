Course Capstone — The Topological Worldview

Twenty lessons ago, "open" needed a distance to even be stated. It doesn't anymore — and that single removal turned out to carry almost everything else with it.

← Lesson 20 Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 opened this course with a question: how much of what Real Analysis A proved about ℝ actually needed distance, versus just needing a handful of closure axioms about open sets? The honest answer, twenty lessons later, is: almost none of it. Continuity, connectedness, compactness, the Intermediate Value Theorem, the Extreme Value Theorem — every one of them turned out to be a statement about open sets in disguise, and every one of them now applies to spaces Real Analysis A had no vocabulary to even describe.

One last worked example, tying three units together

Worked Example — Distinguishing the Torus from the Sphere Let T² be the torus (Unit 5, Lesson 15: a square with both pairs of opposite edges identified, matching direction), and let S² be the sphere {(x,y,z) ∈ ℝ³ : x² + y² + z² = 1}. Both are compact (Unit 4: T² is a continuous image of the compact square [0,1]²; S² is closed and bounded in ℝ³, so Heine-Borel applies directly). Both are connected, in fact path-connected. Every tool from Units 3–4 alone says these two surfaces look the same. But π₁(S²) is trivial (any loop on a sphere can be slid off to one side and contracted — informally, the sphere has "no hole" for a loop to catch on), while π₁(T²) ≅ ℤ × ℤ (a loop can wind around the torus's "donut hole" independently in two different directions — around the tube, and through the hole — stated here without proof). Different fundamental groups mean T² and S² cannot be homeomorphic, settling a question connectedness and compactness alone were structurally unable to answer.
Why this example is the right note to end on It uses Unit 5's quotient construction, Unit 4's compactness tools, and Unit 7's fundamental group together in a single argument — the entire course, working as one system, to answer a question about two everyday shapes that "looks obvious" but has no proof at all without exactly this machinery.

The full dependency chain

The Course, Traced as One Chain Topology axioms (L1), abstracted from ε-ball open sets, generate a basis (L2) that defines closed sets and closure (L3). Continuity is redefined via preimages (L4), matching ε-δ continuity exactly on ℝ, and used to define homeomorphism (L5) and the subspace topology (L6). Connectedness (L7) and path-connectedness (L8) combine with continuity to reprove the Intermediate Value Theorem (L9). Compactness via open covers (L10) reproves Heine-Borel (L11) and, combined with continuity again, the Extreme Value Theorem (L12). Products (L13) and quotients (L14) build new spaces from old ones, including the circle, torus, and Möbius strip (L15). Separation axioms (L16–17) explain when limits are unique and when a space is well-behaved enough to be metrizable (L18). The fundamental group (L19) supplies the first invariant able to distinguish spaces that every earlier tool in this course saw as identical — closing with the exact torus-versus-sphere question above.
Looking Ahead The next course in this sequence, Differential Geometry, picks up exactly here — but adds structure back in, deliberately. Its central objects, smooth manifolds, are topological spaces exactly like the ones built in this course (in fact, every space in Unit 5 — the circle, the torus, the sphere — is a manifold), except equipped with an additional smooth structure that makes calculus possible on them: derivatives, curvature, geodesics. Where this course asked "which of Real Analysis A's results survive with no distance at all," Differential Geometry asks the opposite question: what does calculus look like once "distance" is put back, not as the flat |x−y| of ℝ, but as something that can genuinely curve?
