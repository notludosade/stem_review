Connectedness

"No gaps to jump across," rebuilt with open sets alone — and the separation-based proof technique every connectedness argument in this course will reuse.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 18 defined a set E ⊆ ℝ as connected if it can't be written as a union of two nonempty separated sets, where "separated" used closures (Ā ∩ B = ∅ and A ∩ B̄ = ∅). This lesson gives an equivalent, purely open-set formulation that generalizes immediately to any topological space, and reproves the key machinery — continuous images stay connected — needed for Lesson 9's topological Intermediate Value Theorem.

The definition

Definition — Separation, Connected Space A separation of a topological space X is a pair of nonempty, disjoint, open sets U, V ⊆ X with U ∪ V = X. X is connected if no separation of X exists.
Why this agrees with Real Analysis A's closure-based definition On a subspace E ⊆ ℝ, a separation E = U ∪ V (U, V open in the subspace topology, disjoint, nonempty) exists exactly when Real Analysis A's separated sets A = U, B = V exist: if U, V are open in E and disjoint, then each is also closed in E (being the complement of the other within E), so Ū ∩ E = U and V̄ ∩ E = V, forcing Ū ∩ V = U ∩ V = ∅ and U ∩ V̄ = ∅ — Real Analysis A's separation condition. The reverse direction runs the same argument backward. The two definitions single out exactly the same connected sets; this lesson's version simply doesn't need an ambient metric to state the closures in.

An equivalent test: clopen sets

Theorem — Connected ⟺ No Nontrivial Clopen Sets X is connected if and only if the only subsets of X that are both open and closed ("clopen") are ∅ and X itself.
Proof (⇒) Suppose X is connected, and let C ⊆ X be clopen with C ≠ ∅, X. Then U = C and V = X \ C are both open (C is open by assumption; X \ C is open since C is closed), disjoint, nonempty, and U ∪ V = X — a separation, contradicting connectedness. (⇐) Suppose the only clopen sets are ∅ and X, and suppose toward contradiction X = U ∪ V is a separation. Then U is open by definition, and U = X \ V is closed (V is open, so its complement is closed) — so U is clopen. Since U is nonempty (separation requires it) and U ≠ X (V is nonempty too), this contradicts the hypothesis. ∎

The technique: continuous images of connected spaces

Theorem — Continuous Image of Connected Is Connected If f : X → Y is continuous, X is connected, and f is surjective, then Y is connected.
Proof Suppose toward contradiction Y = U ∪ V is a separation. Since f is continuous, f⁻¹(U) and f⁻¹(V) are open in X. They are disjoint (U, V are disjoint, and preimages of disjoint sets under a function are disjoint), and their union is f⁻¹(U) ∪ f⁻¹(V) = f⁻¹(U ∪ V) = f⁻¹(Y) = X. Both are nonempty: since f is surjective and U, V are nonempty, some point of X maps into each. So f⁻¹(U), f⁻¹(V) form a separation of X — contradicting that X is connected. ∎
Why this is the single most-used theorem in this unit This is the abstract engine behind "you can't tear a connected space into two pieces with a continuous map" — and it's exactly what Lesson 9 needs to rebuild the Intermediate Value Theorem from nothing but this fact plus one more about which subsets of ℝ are connected.

Intervals in ℝ are connected — the same proof, reused

Theorem (restated from Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 18) A subset E ⊆ ℝ with at least two points is connected (in this lesson's open-set sense) if and only if E is an interval.
Why no new proof is needed Real Analysis A's proof of this fact (the sup-based case-check ruling out a, b ∈ E with a < c < b, c ∉ E) only ever used the order structure of ℝ and the completeness axiom to locate a supremum — it never actually depended on distance beyond what "separated" already captured. Since this lesson's separation definition agrees with Real Analysis A's exactly (the why-box above), the identical proof transfers over unchanged, word for word, as a fact about the topological space (ℝ, τstd).

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is a separation of a topological space X?

A separation requires both pieces to be open, disjoint, nonempty, and together covering all of X — connectedness is defined by the absence of such a pair.

2. According to the clopen-set theorem, what characterizes a connected space X?

A nontrivial clopen set would immediately supply a separation (itself and its complement), so ruling out nontrivial clopen sets is exactly equivalent to connectedness.

3. In the proof that continuous images of connected spaces are connected, why must f⁻¹(U) and f⁻¹(V) be nonempty?

Surjectivity of f is exactly what guarantees every nonempty subset of Y has a nonempty preimage, which is needed for f⁻¹(U), f⁻¹(V) to form a genuine separation of X.

4. Fill in the blank: a subset that is simultaneously open and closed is called ___, and a nontrivial one is exactly what a separation supplies.

Clopen — open and closed simultaneously — is the term this lesson's equivalent characterization of connectedness relies on.

5. Fill in the blank: the restated theorem shows connected subsets of ℝ with two or more points are exactly the ___s — reusing Real Analysis A's proof unchanged, since the two separation definitions agree.

Interval is the exact characterization this course inherits unchanged from Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 18.
