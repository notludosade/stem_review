Compactness via Open Covers

Real Analysis A's sequential compactness needed sequences, which need countability. This definition needs neither — and it's the one that actually survives outside metric spaces.

← Unit 3, Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 16 defined a set K ⊆ ℝ as compact if every sequence in K has a subsequence converging to a point of K. That definition is genuinely useful on ℝ, but it leans on sequences — and in a general topological space, a sequence might not carry enough information to detect every open set (this becomes a real issue once spaces stop having a countable basis, a subtlety Unit 6 returns to). The open-cover definition below is the one that generalizes cleanly to any topological space at all.

The definition

Definition — Open Cover, Subcover, Compact An open cover of a topological space X is a collection 𝒰 = {Uα} of open subsets of X with ⋃Uα = X. A subcover is a subcollection of 𝒰 that still covers X. X is compact if every open cover of X has a finite subcover.
Why "finite" is the entire content of the definition Every space has an open cover with a finite subcover trivially if you allow the cover itself to already be finite (or to include X as one of its own members). The definition only bites when the cover is potentially infinite: compactness says that no matter how an infinite (even uncountable) open cover is chosen, some finite piece of it was already enough.

A first example: (0, 1) is not compact

Example Let 𝒰 = {(1/n, 1) : n = 2, 3, 4, …}. Each set is open, and ⋃𝒰 = (0, 1) (any x ∈ (0,1) lies in (1/n, 1) once 1/n < x). But no finite subcollection covers (0, 1): given finitely many sets (1/n₁, 1), …, (1/nk, 1), let N = max(n₁, …, nk); then any point in (0, 1/N] is missed by every one of the finitely many chosen sets. So this cover has no finite subcover, and (0, 1) is not compact.

Closed subsets of compact spaces are compact

Theorem If X is compact and F ⊆ X is closed, then F (with the subspace topology) is compact.
Proof Let 𝒰 = {Uα} be an open cover of F (each Uα open in F, hence of the form Vα ∩ F for Vα open in X). Then 𝒰 ∪ {X \ F} is an open cover of X: X \ F is open (F is closed), and together with the Vα's it covers all of X (every point is either in F, hence in some Vα, or outside F, hence in X \ F). Since X is compact, some finite subcollection Vα₁, …, Vαk, X \ F covers X. Discarding X \ F (which contributes nothing to covering F) and intersecting the rest with F shows Uα₁, …, Uαk is a finite subcover of F. ∎
Why this theorem earns its keep in Unit 5 This is the single most reused fact about compactness in the rest of this course: rather than proving a space compact from scratch, it is very often enough to exhibit it as a closed subset of something already known to be compact — exactly the strategy Lesson 11 uses to extend compactness from intervals of ℝ to all closed, bounded subsets of ℝⁿ.

Relating back to Real Analysis A

Why sequential and open-cover compactness agree on ℝⁿ For subsets of ℝⁿ (and more generally, any metric space), the open-cover definition given here and Real Analysis A's sequential definition (Unit 3, Lesson 16) turn out to characterize exactly the same sets — a genuine theorem, though its proof is beyond this course's scope. What matters here is which definition generalizes: sequential compactness is provably equivalent to open-cover compactness only when the space has enough of a "countable local structure" (formally, is first-countable); the open-cover definition needs no such assumption and is the one used to define compactness in a general topological space.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does it mean for a space X to be compact?

Compactness allows the original cover to be infinite — the requirement is that a finite piece of it was always already enough.

2. Why does the cover {(1/n, 1) : n = 2, 3, 4, …} show (0, 1) is not compact?

Taking a maximum N among finitely many chosen indices always leaves a gap (0, 1/N] uncovered, which is exactly why no finite subcover can exist.

3. In the proof that closed subsets of compact spaces are compact, what role does adding X \ F to the cover of F play?

Adding the open complement X \ F patches the cover of just F into a cover of the whole compact space X, letting X's compactness supply a finite subcover that (after discarding X\F) restricts to a finite subcover of F.

4. Fill in the blank: the entire content of the compactness definition is the requirement that some ___ subcollection of any open cover already covers the space.

Finite is the crucial qualifier — every cover trivially has itself as a subcover, so the definition only has teeth once "finite" is demanded.

5. Fill in the blank: a subset of a compact space is guaranteed to be compact itself, provided it is ___.

Closedness is exactly the extra hypothesis needed — this theorem will be reused throughout Unit 5 to establish compactness without starting from scratch.
