1. Which of the following is NOT one of the three axioms a topology τ on X must satisfy?

Only finite intersections are guaranteed to stay open — arbitrary intersections of open sets can collapse to a non-open set, as ⋂(−1/n, 1/n) = {0} showed in Real Analysis A.

2. On a set X with at least two points, what distinguishes the discrete topology from the indiscrete topology?

Discrete and indiscrete are the two extremes: maximum possible open sets versus minimum possible open sets, both satisfying the topology axioms trivially.

3. Fill in the blank: basis axiom (B2) requires that for x ∈ B₁ ∩ B₂, there exists a basis element ___ with x ∈ B₃ ⊆ B₁ ∩ B₂.

B₃ is exactly the basis element (B2) guarantees exists, and it's what makes the intersection of two basis-generated open sets provably open too.

4. Which collection of subsets of ℝ is a valid basis that generates the standard topology τstd?

Open intervals satisfy both basis axioms and generate exactly τstd, matching the ε-ball construction. Singletons would generate the discrete topology instead, and closed intervals fail (B2) at shared endpoints.

5. Fill in the blank: a set F is closed exactly when its complement X \ F is ___ — there is no independent definition of closed.

Closed is defined purely in terms of the complement, exactly as in Real Analysis A, now for any topological space.

6. Real Analysis A proved "finite union of closed sets is closed" for ℝ using ε-balls. Answer both parts about this course's general proof:

(a) What tool converts the open-set axiom (T3), finite intersections open, into the closed-set fact, finite unions closed?

(b) Fill in the blank: De Morgan converts the complement of a finite union of closed sets into a finite ___ of open sets, which (T3) guarantees is open.

(a) De Morgan's Law is the mechanical engine that flips union statements about complements into intersection statements, and vice versa. (b) The complement of a finite union of closed sets becomes a finite intersection of open sets, open by (T3).

7. Let A = (2, 5] in (ℝ, τstd). Answer both parts:

(a) What is cl(A)?

(b) Fill in the blank: the point ___ has to be added to A to reach its closure, since every ε-ball around it intersects A without that point itself belonging to A.

(a) A already contains 5, but not 2 — every closed set containing (2,5] must also contain 2, so cl(A) = [2,5]. (b) The single missing endpoint is 2.

8. Let A = [−3, 1) in (ℝ, τstd). Answer both parts:

(a) What is int(A)?

(b) Fill in the blank: the interior of A is defined as the ___ open set contained in A — the union of every open subset of A.

(a) −3 has no ε-ball staying inside A (it always spills below −3), so it's excluded from the interior, giving (−3, 1). (b) Largest — interior is dual to closure's "smallest closed superset."

9. Let A = [−3, 1) in (ℝ, τstd), continuing from Question 8. Answer both parts:

(a) Using cl(A) = [−3, 1] and int(A) = (−3, 1), what is ∂A = cl(A) \ int(A)?

(b) Fill in the blank: even though A = [−3, 1) contains −3 but not 1, ___ endpoints belong to the boundary ∂A regardless of which are actually in A.

(a) Subtracting the open interior from the closed closure leaves exactly the two boundary endpoints. (b) Boundary membership doesn't depend on whether the point is in A itself — both −3 and 1 sit on the boundary regardless.

10. Real Analysis A proved arbitrary unions and finite intersections of ε-ball open sets stay open. Answer both parts about how Lesson 1 uses this:

(a) What does Lesson 1's theorem conclude from that earlier result?

(b) Fill in the blank: the proof is short because Real Analysis A's ___ theorem for ε-ball open sets already established exactly what axioms (T2) and (T3) require.

(a) The abstract topology axioms are reverse-engineered from exactly what Real Analysis A already proved about ℝ's open sets. (b) The union/intersection theorem from Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 14 does essentially all the work.
