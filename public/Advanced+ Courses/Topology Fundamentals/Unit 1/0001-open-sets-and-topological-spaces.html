Open Sets and Topological Spaces

Generalizing "open" from ε-balls to a short list of closure axioms — the definition that lets everything Real Analysis A proved about ℝ get proved again, with no notion of distance anywhere in sight.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 14 defined an open set O ⊆ ℝ as one where every point has an ε-ball (x − ε, x + ε) entirely inside O. That definition needs distance: it needs |x − y| to be measurable before "ε-ball" even makes sense. This course asks a more radical question: how much of the machinery built on top of "open" — continuity, connectedness, compactness — actually needs distance at all, versus just needing sets called "open" to obey a short list of closure rules?

Why strip out distance on purpose Plenty of important mathematical objects have no natural distance function at all — the set of all possible outcomes of an infinite coin flip, the space of continuous functions with only "closeness in the limit" and no single number measuring it, a set with only finitely many points. Real Analysis A's ε-ball definition can't even be written down in those settings. What can be salvaged is the two closure properties Real Analysis A already proved about ℝ's open sets — arbitrary unions of opens are open, finite intersections of opens are open (Unit 3, Lesson 14) — and simply declare those properties to be the definition, wherever "open" is meant to apply.

The definition

Definition — Topology, Topological Space Let X be a set. A topology τ on X is a collection of subsets of X (called the open sets) satisfying:
(T1) ∅ ∈ τ and X ∈ τ.
(T2) If {Oα} is any collection of sets in τ (finite, countable, or uncountable), then ⋃Oα ∈ τ.
(T3) If O₁, O₂ ∈ τ, then O₁ ∩ O₂ ∈ τ.
The pair (X, τ) is a topological space. By induction, (T3) extends to any finite intersection of open sets.

Notice exactly what's missing: no mention of ε, no mention of distance, no mention of numbers at all. X can be any set whatsoever. A topology is nothing but a chosen collection of subsets, tagged "open," that happens to close up under arbitrary unions and finite intersections and includes ∅ and X.

The standard topology on ℝ satisfies these axioms

Theorem — The ε-Ball Opens of ℝ Form a Topology Let τstd be the collection of all subsets O ⊆ ℝ such that for every x ∈ O, there is ε > 0 with (x − ε, x + ε) ⊆ O (Real Analysis A's definition of open). Then τstd is a topology on ℝ, called the standard topology.
Proof (T1): ∅ satisfies the condition vacuously (there is no point to check), and ℝ satisfies it trivially (every ε-ball around every point already lies in ℝ). (T2) and (T3): this is exactly Real Analysis A's Unit 3, Lesson 14 theorem — "an arbitrary union of open sets is open; a finite intersection of open sets is open" — proved there directly from the ε-ball definition, with no changes needed here. The three topology axioms are precisely the properties that lesson already established. ∎
Why this proof is so short It's short because the theorem was already proved, a course ago — this lesson's real content is recognizing that Real Analysis A's union/intersection theorem is the topology axioms, just not yet named that. The axiomatic definition above is reverse-engineered from what ℝ's open sets were already known to do.

Two more topologies on any set

Example — Discrete and Indiscrete Topologies For any set X at all (no algebraic or metric structure required): the discrete topology τ = 𝒫(X) (every subset is open) satisfies (T1)–(T3) trivially, since any union or intersection of subsets of X is again a subset of X. The indiscrete topology τ = {∅, X} (only the empty set and X itself are open) also satisfies all three axioms — unions and intersections of {∅, X}'s members only ever produce ∅ or X back. These are the two extremes: discrete has the most possible open sets, indiscrete has the fewest, and every topology on X sits somewhere between them.

Unit 3, Lesson 16 of Real Analysis A distinguished open and closed as "not opposites" for ℝ specifically. That fact is now a special case: since the axioms above put no restriction preventing a set from being both open (in τ) and having an open complement (making it closed too), the same "not opposites, and sometimes both at once" behavior is automatic in every topological space, not just ℝ — X and ∅ are open by (T1) in every topology, and their complements (∅ and X, respectively) are therefore also open, making both X and ∅ closed as well.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a topology τ on a set X consist of, according to this lesson's definition?

The topology axioms (T1)–(T3) never mention distance — only which subsets are declared open, and the two closure properties they must obey.

2. Why does the theorem that τstd (ε-ball opens) is a topology on ℝ have such a short proof in this lesson?

This lesson's axioms are a direct abstraction of the exact theorem Real Analysis A already proved about ε-ball opens — so the proof here is really just pointing back to that earlier result.

3. In the discrete topology on a set X, which subsets of X are open?

The discrete topology declares every possible subset open — it's the topology with the maximum number of open sets, satisfying (T1)–(T3) trivially since arbitrary collections of subsets of X are always subsets of X.

4. Fill in the blank: axiom (T3) requires only ___ intersections of open sets to be open, mirroring exactly the restriction Real Analysis A needed for the same claim about ℝ.

Finite is the required qualifier — infinite intersections of open sets can fail to be open even in ℝ, as Real Analysis A's ⋂(−1/n, 1/n) example showed.

5. Fill in the blank: the topology τ = {∅, X}, with the fewest possible open sets of any topology on X, is called the ___ topology.

The indiscrete topology sits at the opposite extreme from the discrete topology, and its scarcity of open sets will matter a great deal once Unit 6 asks which topologies can distinguish points at all.
