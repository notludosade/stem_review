The Separation Axioms

Hausdorff was one rung on a ladder — T1 below it, regular and normal above it, each demanding more from a space than the last.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Hausdorff separates points from each other. This lesson extends that idea in two directions — separating points from closed sets, and separating closed sets from each other — building a short hierarchy of increasingly demanding conditions, and locating exactly where ℝⁿ sits on it.

T1: a weaker condition than Hausdorff

Definition — T1 Space X is T1 if for any distinct x, y ∈ X, there is an open set containing x but not y (and, by symmetry of the requirement, one containing y but not x). Equivalently, every singleton {x} is closed.
Why Hausdorff implies T1, but not conversely Hausdorff's disjoint U, V trivially give T1's weaker one-directional separation, so every Hausdorff space is T1. The reverse fails: let X be any infinite set with the cofinite topology (open sets are ∅ and every set whose complement is finite). Singletons are closed (their complements are cofinite, hence open), so X is T1. But any two nonempty open sets U, V in this topology must intersect: X \ U and X \ V are both finite, so X \ (U ∩ V) = (X\U) ∪ (X\V) is finite, meaning U ∩ V = X \ (finite set) is infinite, hence nonempty. No two points can ever be separated by disjoint open sets, so X is not Hausdorff.

Regular and normal: separating sets, not just points

Definition — Regular (T3), Normal (T4) X is regular if X is T1, and for every closed set F and point x ∉ F, there are disjoint open sets U ∋ x, V ⊇ F. X is normal if X is T1, and for every pair of disjoint closed sets F, G, there are disjoint open sets U ⊇ F, V ⊇ G.
Theorem — The Hierarchy Normal ⟹ Regular ⟹ Hausdorff ⟹ T1, and none of these implications reverses in general.
Why each implication holds Normal ⟹ Regular: a single point {x} is closed (T1), so treating {x} as one of the two closed sets in the normal condition immediately gives the regular condition. Regular ⟹ Hausdorff: treating {y} (closed, by T1) as the closed set F not containing x directly gives Hausdorff's separation for x and y. Each stronger axiom is exactly the previous one applied to a more general kind of object — points, then a point versus a closed set, then two closed sets.

ℝⁿ sits at the top

Theorem — ℝⁿ Is Normal (ℝⁿ, τstd) is normal.
Why (proof sketch) For disjoint closed F, G ⊆ ℝⁿ, define d(x, F) = inf{|x − z| : z ∈ F} (well-defined, since F is nonempty and distances are bounded below by 0). The function h(x) = d(x, F) − d(x, G) is continuous (a fact provable directly from the triangle inequality applied to the infimum, in the same style as Real Analysis A's continuity proofs), h < 0 exactly on F and h > 0 exactly on G (since F, G are closed and disjoint, x ∈ F forces d(x,F) = 0 < d(x,G), and symmetrically for G). Then U = h⁻¹((−∞, 0)) and V = h⁻¹((0, ∞)) are disjoint open sets (preimages of open sets under a continuous function, Lesson 4) with F ⊆ U and G ⊆ V.
Example F = {0} and G = [2, 3] are disjoint closed subsets of ℝ. d(x, F) = |x|, d(x,G) = distance from x to the nearest point of [2,3]. At x = 0.5, d(x,F) = 0.5 and d(x,G) = 1.5, so h(0.5) = 0.5 − 1.5 = −1 < 0, correctly placing 0.5 in U (the side containing F).

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does it mean for X to be T1?

T1 is a strictly weaker, one-directional version of Hausdorff's two-directional disjoint-set separation.

2. Why does the cofinite topology on an infinite set show T1 does not imply Hausdorff?

Two open sets with finite complements can never be disjoint on an infinite set, so Hausdorff fails even though T1 holds.

3. In the proof that ℝⁿ is normal, what role does the function h(x) = d(x,F) − d(x,G) play?

The sign of h, continuous and negative exactly on F and positive exactly on G, is what produces the two disjoint open separating sets U and V.

4. Fill in the blank: the strongest condition in this lesson's hierarchy, requiring any two disjoint closed sets to be separated by disjoint open sets, is called ___.

Normal is the top of the hierarchy in this lesson, and ℝⁿ is proved to satisfy it directly.

5. Fill in the blank: T1 is equivalent to the condition that every singleton {x} is ___ in X.

Closed singletons is the equivalent, and often more practical, way to check the T1 condition directly.
