NP-Completeness & the Cook-Levin Theorem

Lesson 30 showed reductions transfer hardness between two problems. This lesson asks: is there a problem so general that every problem in NP reduces to it? Cook and Levin proved yes — and named the first one.

← Lesson 30 Lesson 32 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 30's Independent Set ≤ₚ Vertex Cover reduction relates exactly two problems. This lesson asks a far more ambitious question: is there a single problem that every problem in NP reduces to — a problem that is, in a precise sense, at least as hard as all of NP simultaneously? In 1971, Stephen Cook (and independently, Leonid Levin) answered yes, and identified the first such problem: Boolean satisfiability.

NP-hard and NP-complete

Definitions A problem B is NP-hard if every problem A in NP satisfies A ≤ₚ B — every NP problem reduces to it. A problem is NP-complete if it is both NP-hard and itself in NP. NP-complete problems are exactly the hardest problems within NP: they're at least as hard as every other NP problem, while still being NP problems themselves (their yes-instances have polynomial-time-checkable certificates).
Why NP-complete problems are equivalent to each other If B and C are both NP-complete, then B ≤ₚ C and C ≤ₚ B both hold (each is NP-hard, so every NP problem — including the other — reduces to it). This means a polynomial-time algorithm for any single NP-complete problem would immediately give a polynomial-time algorithm for every problem in NP, resolving P vs. NP outright. Every NP-complete problem is, in this precise sense, exactly as hard as every other one.

Boolean satisfiability — the first NP-complete problem

SAT Given a Boolean formula built from variables, AND (∧), OR (∨), and NOT (¬) — for example (x₁ ∨ ¬x₂) ∧ (x₂ ∨ x₃) — does some assignment of true/false to each variable make the whole formula evaluate to true? SAT is clearly in NP: a satisfying assignment is a certificate, and plugging it in and evaluating the formula is polynomial time.
Theorem (Cook, 1971; Levin, 1973) SAT is NP-complete.
Why this proof looks nothing like a reduction between two named problems Proving SAT is NP-hard means showing every problem in NP reduces to it — not one specific problem, but the entire infinite class at once. Cook and Levin's proof works directly from the definition of NP itself: any NP problem has, by definition, a polynomial-time verifier — an algorithm (formally, a Turing machine) that checks a certificate in polynomial time. The proof mechanically encodes that verifier's entire computation — every step, every possible memory configuration, at every time slice up to its polynomial time bound — as one gigantic Boolean formula, built so that the formula is satisfiable exactly when some certificate makes the verifier accept. This is a one-time, foundational construction: it doesn't reduce SAT from some other named problem, it reduces the abstract notion of "any NP verifier's computation" into SAT directly.
Why Cook-Levin is the seed every later NP-completeness proof depends on Once SAT is confirmed NP-hard by this direct construction, proving any other problem X is NP-hard no longer requires repeating that machine-simulation argument. It suffices to show SAT ≤ₚ X (or, by the transitivity of reductions, that some already-confirmed-NP-complete problem reduces to X) — a much simpler proof technique, comparable in spirit to Lesson 30's Independent Set/Vertex Cover reduction. This is exactly why Cook-Levin is described as "the seed": every NP-completeness proof since 1971 chains back to it, directly or through a few intermediate reductions, rather than re-deriving hardness from the definition of NP from scratch.
3-SAT — the form most reductions actually start from 3-SAT restricts SAT to formulas in conjunctive normal form with exactly 3 literals per clause, e.g. (x₁ ∨ ¬x₂ ∨ x₃) ∧ (¬x₁ ∨ x₂ ∨ x₄). A short reduction (SAT ≤ₚ 3-SAT, via a standard clause-splitting transformation, omitted here for space) shows 3-SAT is exactly as hard as general SAT — so most textbook NP-completeness proofs, including Lesson 32's, reduce from 3-SAT rather than unrestricted SAT, since its rigid per-clause structure is easier to encode into other problems' instances.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What distinguishes "NP-hard" from "NP-complete"?

NP-hardness is purely about being a reduction target for all of NP; NP-completeness adds the requirement of also belonging to NP.

2. Why does the Cook-Levin proof of SAT's NP-hardness look different from a reduction like Lesson 30's Independent Set ≤ₚ Vertex Cover?

Cook-Levin reduces the entire abstract class of NP verifiers into SAT directly, which is why it needed a fundamentally different argument than a reduction between two specific problems.

3. Why is Cook-Levin described as "the seed" that later NP-completeness proofs depend on?

Chaining reductions back to SAT is exactly what spares every later proof from repeating Cook-Levin's foundational machine-simulation argument.

4. Fill in the blank: the Cook-Levin theorem proves that Boolean ___ was the first problem shown to be NP-complete.

SAT (Boolean satisfiability) is the problem Cook and Levin proved NP-complete, founding the entire theory of NP-completeness.

5. Fill in the blank: most textbook NP-completeness proofs reduce from ___ rather than unrestricted SAT, since its rigid three-literals-per-clause structure is easier to encode into other problems.

3-SAT's fixed clause width makes it the more convenient starting point for most reductions, despite being equally hard as general SAT.
