Differentiability, Continuity, and Rules of Differentiation

Why every differentiable function is continuous, why the reverse fails, and the product/quotient rules proved from the definition.

← Lesson 25 Lesson 27 → Contents Glossary →

AP Calc BC stated "differentiable implies continuous" without proof, and used |x| as an example of continuous-but-not-differentiable without dwelling on why the implication only runs one way. This lesson proves both directions rigorously.

Differentiable implies continuous

Theorem If f is differentiable at a, then f is continuous at a.
Proof For x ≠ a, write f(x) − f(a) = [(f(x) − f(a))/(x − a)] · (x − a). As x → a, the bracketed term → f′(a) (by differentiability) and (x − a) → 0, so by the product rule for functional limits (Unit 4, Lesson 21, which extends immediately from sequences to functions), the whole right side → f′(a) · 0 = 0. So limx→a f(x) = f(a), meaning f is continuous at a. ∎

The converse fails

Example — continuous but not differentiable f(x) = |x| is continuous everywhere (a composition of continuous functions, Unit 4 Lesson 21) but not differentiable at 0: the difference quotient (|x| − 0)/(x − 0) equals 1 for x > 0 and −1 for x < 0. Taking xₙ = 1/n → 0 gives quotient → 1; taking yₙ = −1/n → 0 gives quotient → −1. Since these disagree, the limit defining f′(0) does not exist (using the sequential characterization of functional limits, the direct analogue of Unit 4 Lesson 20's sequential test for continuity).
Why this matters beyond the specific example "Differentiable ⇒ continuous but not conversely" is exactly the same one-way-implication pattern seen already: convergent ⇒ Cauchy (Unit 2) held one way only in general, though the converse held specifically over ℝ by completeness. Here, no amount of extra structure rescues the converse — continuous functions failing to be differentiable anywhere at all exist (the Weierstrass function, mentioned in MISSION.md as beyond this course's fundamentals scope).

Rules of differentiation, proved

Theorem — Product Rule If f, g are differentiable at a, then fg is differentiable at a, with (fg)′(a) = f′(a)g(a) + f(a)g′(a).
Proof (f(x)g(x) − f(a)g(a))/(x−a) = [f(x)g(x) − f(a)g(x)]/(x−a) + [f(a)g(x) − f(a)g(a)]/(x−a) = g(x)·(f(x)−f(a))/(x−a) + f(a)·(g(x)−g(a))/(x−a). As x → a: g(x) → g(a) (continuity, from differentiability of g just proved above), the first quotient → f′(a), and the second quotient → g′(a). By the sum and product rules for functional limits, the whole expression → g(a)f′(a) + f(a)g′(a). ∎
Why "add and subtract f(a)g(x)" is the key trick This is the same additive-insertion trick used in Unit 2, Lesson 9's product rule for sequences (there: aₙbₙ − AB = aₙ(bₙ−B) + B(aₙ−A)) — inserting a cross term that lets the numerator be split into two pieces, each isolating exactly one function's difference quotient. The quotient rule (proved the same way, omitted here for space) and chain rule follow by similar direct manipulation of the definition.

1. Why does differentiability at a imply continuity at a?

The algebraic trick of multiplying back by (x−a) is exactly what forces f(x) − f(a) → 0.

Disagreeing one-sided behavior of the difference quotient is exactly why the limit, and hence the derivative, fails to exist at the corner.

This additive-insertion trick reappears from Unit 2's sequence product rule proof, applied here to difference quotients instead.
