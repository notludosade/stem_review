RC Circuits as a Differential Equation

Every circuit so far has reduced to algebra. Add a capacitor to a loop with a resistor and the loop rule stops being an equation you solve once — it becomes a differential equation in charge, and its solution is a curve that unfolds over time.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Setup — The Loop Rule Around a Charging Capacitor A battery of EMF ε, a resistor R, and an initially uncharged capacitor C are wired in a single series loop; a switch closes at t = 0. Applying the loop rule at any instant: ε − IR − Q/C = 0. Unlike every earlier circuit, I isn't a fixed number here — it's the rate charge is arriving on the capacitor, I = dQ/dt. Substituting turns the loop rule into R(dQ/dt) + Q/C = ε, a first-order differential equation for Q(t) instead of a plain algebraic equation for I.
Method — Solving by Separation of Variables Rearrange to dQ/dt = (Cε − Q)/(RC), then separate the variables: dQ/(Cε − Q) = dt/(RC). Integrating both sides and applying the initial condition Q(0) = 0 gives Q(t) = Cε(1 − e^(−t/RC)). Dividing by C (and noting Cε = Q_max = the final charge) converts this into a voltage statement: V(t) = V_max(1 − e^(−t/RC)), where V_max = ε. Differentiating Q(t) back to get the current gives I(t) = (ε/R)e^(−t/RC) — current starts at ε/R (the capacitor briefly acts like a wire) and decays toward zero as the capacitor fills up.
Why τ = RC Is the Only Time Scale in the Problem The combination τ = RC, the time constant, is the one quantity in V(t) = V_max(1 − e^(−t/τ)) with units of time — check: Ω·F = (V/A)·(C/V) = C/A = s. At t = τ, the exponential term is e⁻¹ ≈ 0.368, so the capacitor has reached 1 − 0.368 = 63.2% of V_max, regardless of what ε, R, or C individually are. By t = 5τ, it's over 99% charged — "fully charged" is only ever an approximation, but a good enough one by then. Bigger R slows charging by limiting current; bigger C slows charging by demanding more charge to reach the same voltage — either one alone stretches τ.
Worked Example — Charging Curve ε = 12 V, R = 2000 Ω, C = 500 μF = 5×10⁻⁴ F. Time constant: τ = RC = (2000)(5×10⁻⁴) = 1 s. At t = τ = 1 s: V = 12(1 − e⁻¹) ≈ 12(0.632) ≈ 7.59 V. The current at that same instant: I_max = ε/R = 12/2000 = 6 mA at t = 0, falling to I(1 s) = 6 mA × e⁻¹ ≈ 2.21 mA — already down to about a third of its starting value, exactly as the exponential predicts.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Applying Kirchhoff's loop rule around a series circuit with a battery ε, resistor R, and charging capacitor C, using I = dQ/dt, gives:

The loop rule gives ε − IR − Q/C = 0; substituting I = dQ/dt and rearranging gives R(dQ/dt) + Q/C = ε, a differential equation because I itself depends on how fast Q is changing.

2. Fill in the blank: a series RC circuit has R = 4000 Ω and C = 250 μF. Its time constant is τ = ___ s.

τ = RC = (4000)(250×10⁻⁶) = (4000)(2.5×10⁻⁴) = 1 s.

3. As t → ∞ in a charging RC circuit, V(t) = V_max(1 − e^(−t/RC)) approaches:

As t → ∞, e^(−t/RC) → 0, so V → V_max = ε. With the capacitor fully charged, it draws no more current, so I = (ε/R)e^(−t/RC) → 0 as well.

4. Physically, why does the charging current I(t) = (ε/R)e^(−t/RC) shrink over time even though the battery's EMF ε never changes?

From ε − IR − Q/C = 0, the current is I = (ε − Q/C)/R. As Q builds up, Q/C rises toward ε, shrinking the net voltage (ε − Q/C) available to push current through R — the capacitor's rising voltage is doing the throttling, not any change in the battery or resistor.
