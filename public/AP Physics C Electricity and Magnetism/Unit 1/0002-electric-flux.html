Electric Flux

Before Gauss's law can relate field to enclosed charge, you need a precise way to measure "how much field pierces a surface" — that quantity is flux.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Electric Flux For a uniform field E through a flat surface of area A, electric flux is Φ_E = E·A = EA cos θ, where θ is the angle between E and the surface's outward normal vector Â (a unit vector perpendicular to the surface). Flux is maximum when the field is parallel to the normal (θ = 0°, field pierces straight through) and zero when the field grazes the surface edge-on (θ = 90°).
Method — Nonuniform Fields and Curved Surfaces When E isn't uniform, or the surface isn't flat, chop the surface into infinitesimal patches dA, each nearly flat with its own local normal, and sum: Φ_E = ∫E·dA. For a closed surface (one that fully encloses a region, like a sphere or box), the convention is to take the normal pointing outward everywhere; field lines leaving the surface contribute positive flux, field lines entering contribute negative flux, and Φ_E is the net of the two.
Why Flux, and Why the Outward-Normal Convention Flux through a closed surface is really counting field lines: how many net lines leave versus enter. That count turns out to depend only on the charge sealed inside the surface — not on the surface's shape or size — which is exactly the statement Gauss's law makes precise next lesson. The outward-normal convention is what makes "net lines leaving" a well-defined sign: enclosed positive charge produces net outward flux (positive), enclosed negative charge produces net inward flux (negative).
Worked Example A uniform field E = 500 N/C strikes a flat surface of area A = 0.2 m² at an angle of 60° between E and the surface's normal. Φ_E = EA cos θ = (500)(0.2)(cos 60°) = (100)(0.5) = 50 N·m²/C.

1. Flux through a flat surface is greatest when:

Φ_E = EA cos θ is maximized when cos θ = 1, i.e. θ = 0°, field and normal aligned.

2. For a closed surface, the standard sign convention takes the normal vector to point:

The outward-normal convention makes "lines leaving minus lines entering" well-defined — necessary for Gauss's law to have a consistent sign.

3. Fill in the blank: a uniform field of 1000 N/C strikes a 0.05 m² surface head-on (θ = 0°). The flux through the surface is ___ N·m²/C.

Φ_E = EA cos θ = (1000)(0.05)(1) = 50 N·m²/C.

4. A surface is oriented so its normal is perpendicular to a uniform field (θ = 90°). The flux through it is:

cos 90° = 0, so Φ_E = EA cos θ = 0 — no field lines pierce a surface that's edge-on to them.
