Unit 1: Electrostatics

Electric field, E(r) = (kQ/r²) r̂

A vector field a point charge Q creates at every point in space — a function you can evaluate anywhere, not a single number computed for one specific pair of charges. This course's sign convention: positive Q produces E along +r̂ (away from the charge); negative Q produces E along −r̂ (toward the charge).

Superposition of electric fields, E_net = ΣEᵢ

The net field from several point charges is the vector sum of each one's individual field. For a continuous charge distribution, superposition becomes an integral over infinitesimal elements: E = ∫dE, dE = k dq/r² r̂.

Electric flux, Φ_E = E·A = EA cos θ

A measure of how much field pierces a surface, where θ is the angle between E and the surface's outward normal vector. For nonuniform fields or curved surfaces, Φ_E = ∫E·dA.

Gauss's law, ∮E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀

Relates the flux through any closed surface to the charge enclosed by it — always true, but only a practical shortcut for solving for E when the charge distribution has spherical, cylindrical, or planar symmetry.

Gaussian surface

A closed surface chosen (not a physical object) so that E has constant magnitude and is everywhere parallel or perpendicular to dA, letting E factor out of Gauss's law and be solved for algebraically.

Unit 2: Electric Potential

Electric potential, V(r) = −∫∞r E·dl

Taking V(∞) = 0, the negative of the field integrated along any path from infinity in to a point. Equivalently E = −dV/dr: field is potential's derivative, potential is field's integral. For a point charge, V(r) = kQ/r.

Superposition of potential, V_net = ΣVᵢ

Because potential is a scalar, combining several charges' potentials is ordinary addition — no components or angles required, unlike combining electric fields.

Potential from continuous charge, V = ∫k dq/r

The general recipe for continuous distributions, with dq = λ dl (line, linear density λ), dq = σ dA (surface, density σ), or dq = ρ dV (volume, density ρ).

Equipotential surface

The set of all points sharing the same value of V. Moving a charge along one does zero work, and the electric field is always perpendicular to it at every point.

Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium

Once charge stops moving: E = 0 everywhere inside the conducting material, the entire conductor sits at one single potential, and E at the surface is perpendicular to the surface. Any excess charge is stranded entirely on the outer surface, since a Gaussian surface drawn just inside the conductor always encloses zero net charge.

Unit 3: Capacitors and Dielectrics

Capacitance, C = Q/V

A proportionality constant, measured in farads, fixed by a capacitor's geometry — not by whatever particular Q or V happens to be present at a given moment.

Parallel-plate capacitor, C = ε₀A/d

Derived from Gauss's law (E = σ/ε₀ between the plates) and integrating E over the gap (V = Ed); charge Q cancels out entirely, leaving a result set only by plate area A and separation d.

Energy stored in a capacitor, U = ½QV = ½CV² = Q²/(2C)

The factor of ½ comes from the capacitor's voltage rising linearly from 0 to V as it charges, so the work integral effectively uses the average voltage V/2, not the final voltage V.

Dielectric constant κ, C = κC₀

An insulating material filling a capacitor's gap raises capacitance by factor κ ≥ 1, via bound polarization charges that partially cancel the free charges' field inside the material.

Isolated vs. battery-connected capacitor

Inserting a dielectric with Q fixed (isolated) drops V to V₀/κ; with V fixed (battery-connected) instead, Q rises to κQ₀. Which one happens depends entirely on whether the battery is still connected.

Unit 4: Current and Resistance

Current, I = dQ/dt

The instantaneous rate charge crosses a conductor's cross-section. Conventional current points in the direction positive charge would flow — opposite to actual electron motion in a metal.

Current density, J = I/A

Current per cross-sectional area — a quantity that doesn't depend on how thick a wire happens to be.

Drift velocity, I = nqAv_d

The average speed of charge carriers (density n, charge q) through a conductor of cross-section A. Solving for v_d = I/(nqA) gives a speed that's typically a fraction of a millimeter per second — a circuit responds quickly not because electrons sprint, but because the field itself propagates near light speed.

Resistance and resistivity, R = ρL/A

Ohm's law V = IR holds for ohmic materials. Resistance depends on both geometry (length L, area A) and the material's intrinsic resistivity ρ, which itself typically rises with temperature.

Series and parallel resistors

Series: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + … (resistances add directly). Parallel: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … (reciprocals add) — the exact opposite combination pattern from capacitors.

Unit 5: Circuit Analysis

Kirchhoff's junction rule (KCL), ΣI_in = ΣI_out

At any node, total current in equals total current out — charge conservation, since charge can't pile up or vanish at a junction.

Kirchhoff's loop rule (KVL), Σ(voltage changes) = 0

Around any closed loop, energy gained equals energy lost. A battery traversed − to + contributes +ε; a resistor traversed in the assumed current direction contributes −IR.

RC circuit as a differential equation, R(dQ/dt) + Q/C = ε

Applying the loop rule to a charging capacitor, with I = dQ/dt, turns the loop rule from algebra into a first-order differential equation for Q(t).

RC charging and discharging

Charging: Q(t) = Cε(1 − e^(−t/RC)), rising toward Q_max. Discharging (no battery in the loop): Q(t) = Q₀e^(−t/RC), decaying toward zero. Same shape, opposite direction.

Time constant, τ = RC

The one quantity with units of time in the RC exponential. At t = τ, charging reaches 63.2% of V_max and discharging falls to 36.8% of V₀ (complements of each other). By t = 5τ, a circuit is conventionally treated as "practically" done.

Unit 6: Magnetic Fields

Magnetic force law, F = qv × B

A charge q moving with velocity v through field B feels a force F = |q|vB sin θ, always perpendicular to both v and B. Since F·v = 0 at every instant, magnetic force never does work — it changes a charge's direction, never its speed.

Circular motion in a field, r = mv/(qB), T = 2πm/(qB)

When v is perpendicular to a uniform B, the magnetic force is centripetal, producing circular motion. The period T has no v in it — a faster particle just carves a proportionally wider circle in the same time.

Force on a current-carrying wire, F = IL × B

Scales the single-charge force law up to a whole current: F = BIL sin θ, with L pointing along the wire in the direction of conventional current.

Torque on a current loop, τ = IA × B = μ × B

The net force on any closed current loop in a uniform field is always zero, but the forces on opposite sides don't share a line of action, producing torque τ = IAB sin θ. Magnetic moment μ = IA is a vector along the loop's normal. This torque, cheated past its zero-torque equilibrium by a commutator, is the physical basis of a DC motor.

Biot-Savart law and Ampère's law

Biot-Savart: dB = (μ₀/4π)(I dl × r̂)/r² builds up B from a current element, giving B = μ₀I/(2πr) for an infinite straight wire. Ampère's law, ∮B·dl = μ₀I_enc, is the magnetic analog of Gauss's law — trivial algebra whenever symmetry lets you choose an Amperian loop where B is constant and parallel to dl, as with a solenoid's interior field B = μ₀nI.

Unit 7: Electromagnetism

Magnetic flux, Φ_B = ∫B·dA = BA cos θ

The same field-through-a-surface bookkeeping as electric flux, applied to B. The net flux through any closed surface is always zero — magnetic field lines never terminate.

Faraday's law, EMF = −N dΦ_B/dt

A changing magnetic flux through a circuit of N turns induces an EMF. No change in flux means no induced EMF, however strong the field itself is — B, A, or θ can each be the thing that's changing.

Motional EMF, EMF = BLv

The EMF induced in a conducting rod of length L sliding at speed v through field B — derivable either from Faraday's law (dΦ_B/dt as swept area grows) or directly from the magnetic force qv×B integrated along the rod.

Lenz's law

An induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change in flux that produced it — the minus sign in Faraday's law made concrete, and a direct requirement of energy conservation (the alternative would be a free-energy machine).

Self-inductance, EMF = −L dI/dt

A coil's own changing current induces an EMF back on itself, opposing the change (unit: the henry). In an RL circuit, current charges as I(t) = (ε/R)(1 − e^(−t/τ)) with τ = L/R — the exact mirror of an RC circuit, with current standing in for charge.

Maxwell's equations

Four equations spanning this whole course: Gauss's law (∮E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀), Gauss's law for magnetism (∮B·dA = 0, no monopoles), Faraday's law (∮E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt), and the Ampère–Maxwell law (∮B·dl = μ₀I_enc + μ₀ε₀ dΦ_E/dt).

Displacement current, μ₀ε₀ dΦ_E/dt

Maxwell's addition to Ampère's law, needed so a charging capacitor's B field comes out consistent regardless of which surface (cutting the wire, or passing between the plates) bounds the chosen loop — a changing electric flux standing in for a literal current.

Electromagnetic wave speed, v = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3.00×10⁸ m/s

Falls directly out of combining Faraday's law with the Ampère–Maxwell law in empty space (Q_enc = I_enc = 0): a changing E creates a circulating B and vice versa, each regenerating the other. This speed exactly matches the independently measured speed of light — the reason light itself is understood as an electromagnetic wave.
