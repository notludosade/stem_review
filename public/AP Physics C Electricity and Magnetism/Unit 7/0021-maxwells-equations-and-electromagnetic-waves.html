Maxwell's Equations and Electromagnetic Waves

Four equations, built from nothing but charges, currents, and loops, turn out to demand that empty space itself can carry a self-sustaining wave — and that wave's predicted speed is exactly the speed of light.

← Lesson 20 Contents Glossary →

Twenty lessons ago, this course opened with Coulomb's law and a single point charge. Since then, an electric field became flux through a Gaussian surface, flux became potential, potential became capacitance, moving charge became current and circuits, current became magnetic fields, and — just last lesson — a changing magnetic flux became an induced EMF. Every one of those results is really just one of four field equations in disguise. Written down together, correctly, those four equations do something none of them do alone: they predict a wave.

Maxwell's Equations, Brought Together

Every physical law about electricity and magnetism this entire course covered is contained in these four statements. The first three are exactly what Unit 1, Unit 6, and Lesson 19 already established. The fourth has one term that's new.
The Piece That Was Missing — Displacement Current Ampère's law, as Unit 6 first stated it, read simply ∮B·dl = μ₀I_enc. That works everywhere current physically punches through the surface bounded by the loop — but pick a capacitor mid-charge. Draw an Amperian loop around the wire feeding one plate, then bulge the surface it bounds so it passes between the plates instead of cutting the wire. No actual charge crosses that bulged surface — I_enc = 0 there — yet the wire on either side is plainly still carrying current, and there has to be the same B field in the same place regardless of which surface you happened to pick. Ampère's law as originally written gives two different, contradictory answers for the same physical situation. Maxwell's fix was the term μ₀ε₀ dΦ_E/dt: as the capacitor charges, the electric flux between its plates is changing at exactly the rate needed to stand in for the "missing" current — a displacement current, carrying no actual charge, but doing everything a real current does in Ampère's law.
From Four Equations to a Wave Consider a region of empty space, far from any charge or current: Q_enc = 0 and I_enc = 0 everywhere. The Ampère–Maxwell law reduces to ∮B·dl = μ₀ε₀ dΦ_E/dt, sitting right alongside Faraday's law, ∮E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt. Read them together: a changing E creates a circulating B; that changing B in turn creates a circulating E; that changing E creates another circulating B — each field regenerating the other, propagating outward with neither one ever needing a charge or current to sustain it. Carrying this out in full (genuinely a step beyond this course, requiring partial differential equations in three dimensions) turns these two coupled equations into a single wave equation for E, and an identical one for B — transverse waves, E and B mutually perpendicular and both perpendicular to the direction of travel, moving at a speed set entirely by two constants that, until this moment, had nothing to do with light at all: v = 1/√(μ₀ε₀).
Worked Example — Computing the Wave Speed μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A and ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²). μ₀ε₀ = (4π×10⁻⁷)(8.85×10⁻¹²) ≈ 1.112×10⁻¹⁷, so v = 1/√(1.112×10⁻¹⁷) ≈ 3.00×10⁸ m/s. That is not approximately the speed of light — it is the speed of light, measured independently decades before Maxwell, by entirely optical methods (Fizeau's rotating-toothed-wheel experiment, 1849) that involved no electricity or magnetism whatsoever. Maxwell's equations, built entirely out of Coulomb's law, current loops, and induction experiments, predicted the speed of light without light ever entering the derivation. The conclusion is unavoidable: light itself is an electromagnetic wave.
Why This Was Such a Big Deal Before Maxwell (1861–1865), electricity, magnetism, and optics were three separate branches of physics, studied by different people with different equipment. After Maxwell, optics became a special case of electromagnetism — light is simply the electromagnetic wave your eyes happen to detect. Heinrich Hertz confirmed this directly in 1887, building a circuit that generated and detected an electromagnetic wave in a laboratory, and showing it reflected, refracted, and polarized exactly like light — just invisible, at a much longer wavelength. Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all the same physical phenomenon at different frequencies, all governed by the same four equations above.

1. Which two of Maxwell's four equations, applied in a region with no charges or currents, combine to predict a self-sustaining electromagnetic wave?

A changing B induces a circulating E (Faraday's law); that changing E induces a circulating B (the Ampère–Maxwell law, with I_enc = 0 in empty space). Each field regenerates the other — that mutual regeneration is the wave.

2. Fill in the blank: using μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A and ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²), v = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) works out to ___ ×10⁸ m/s (enter just the coefficient).

μ₀ε₀ = (4π×10⁻⁷)(8.85×10⁻¹²) ≈ 1.112×10⁻¹⁷, so v = 1/√(1.112×10⁻¹⁷) ≈ 3.00×10⁸ m/s — the speed of light.

3. Maxwell's added term in the Ampère–Maxwell law, μ₀ε₀ dΦ_E/dt (the "displacement current"), was necessary because without it:

Without the displacement current term, the loop's B field would depend on an arbitrary choice of surface — a genuine inconsistency, not just an inconvenience. The added term fixes it by supplying the "missing" contribution wherever changing electric flux stands in for a literal current.

4. Maxwell computed a predicted wave speed from purely electric and magnetic constants (μ₀ and ε₀), with no light involved in the derivation at all. That speed turned out to:

v = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3.00×10⁸ m/s, matching light's independently measured speed exactly — strong enough evidence that light itself must be an electromagnetic wave, later confirmed directly by Hertz.
