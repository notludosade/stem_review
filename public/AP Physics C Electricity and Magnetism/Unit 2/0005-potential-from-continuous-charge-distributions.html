Potential from Continuous Charge Distributions

Because potential is a scalar, integrating it over a continuous charge distribution skips the component bookkeeping that made the same job hard for the field back in Lesson 1.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Method — The General Recipe Slice the object into infinitesimal charge elements dq, each a distance r from the field point, and integrate: V = ∫k dq/r. The element itself depends on the object's shape: dq = λ dl for a line with linear charge density λ, dq = σ dA for a surface with surface charge density σ, and dq = ρ dV for a volume with volume charge density ρ.
Worked Example — On the Axis of a Uniformly Charged Ring A ring of radius R carries total charge Q, spread uniformly. Find V at a point on the ring's axis, a distance x from its center.
Every charge element dq on the ring — no matter where around the ring it sits — is exactly the same distance r = √(x² + R²) from the axial point. That distance is a constant with respect to the integration, so it pulls straight out: V = ∫k dq/r = (k/r)∫dq = kQ/√(x² + R²). No components, no symmetry argument required — a genuinely one-line integral, once you notice r doesn't depend on position around the ring.
Why This Beats the Field Calculation Finding E on the ring's axis (a standard companion problem) requires a symmetry argument: each dq contributes a dE with both an axial and a radial component, the radial components cancel in pairs by symmetry, and only the axial components survive the integral — extra bookkeeping potential simply doesn't need, because there's no direction to a scalar. This is the payoff promised back in Lesson 4: solve for V first when you can, then differentiate for E only if you need it.

Try it — potential along a ring's axis

Move along the axis and watch V(x) = kQ/√(x² + R²) — largest at the center (x = 0), falling off like a point charge kQ/x once x is much larger than R.

1. Computing V on a charged ring's axis is simpler than computing E there mainly because:

Since r is the same constant distance for every dq on the ring, and V has no direction to track, the integral collapses to (k/r)∫dq = kQ/r — no symmetry cancellation needed at all.

2. Fill in the blank: a ring of radius 0.3 m carries total charge 5 μC. The potential on its axis at x = 0.4 m is ___ V. (k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²; note r = √(x²+R²) = 0.5 m)

V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(5×10⁻⁶)/(0.5) = 44,950/0.5 = 89,900 V.

3. For a uniformly charged flat surface with surface charge density σ, the correct infinitesimal charge element to integrate is:

Surface charge density σ has units of charge per area, so it pairs with an area element dA; λ dl is for line charge, ρ dV is for volume charge.

4. Far from a charged ring's center (x ≫ R), the on-axis potential V(x) = kQ/√(x²+R²) approaches:

When x ≫ R, √(x²+R²) ≈ x, so V(x) ≈ kQ/x — any localized charge distribution looks like a point charge from far enough away.
