Equipotential Surfaces and Conductors

A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is a single equipotential object — and that one fact, combined with Gauss's law, pins down where its charge has to sit and how its field has to behave.

← Lesson 5 Unit 3, Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Equipotential Surface An equipotential surface is the set of all points sharing the same value of V. Moving a charge along an equipotential surface does zero work, since W = qΔV and ΔV = 0 by definition. Because E = −dV/dl, and moving along the surface produces no change in V, E must be perpendicular to every equipotential surface at every point — if E had any component along the surface, V would have to change there.
Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium Once charge has stopped moving on a conductor (equilibrium), three facts follow together: E = 0 everywhere inside the conducting material (any leftover interior field would keep pushing charge until it vanished); the entire conductor — interior and surface alike — sits at one single potential (E = 0 inside means no potential drop between any two interior points); and E at the surface is perpendicular to the surface (a surface-parallel component would drive charge to keep moving along the surface, contradicting equilibrium).
Why Excess Charge Has to Sit on the Surface This connects straight back to Gauss's law (Lesson 3). Draw a Gaussian surface just inside the conductor's actual surface, entirely within the conducting material. Since E = 0 everywhere on this Gaussian surface, the flux through it is zero — and Gauss's law then forces Q_enc = 0. That has to hold no matter how you shrink or grow this interior surface, which is only possible if there's no net charge anywhere inside the conductor. Any excess charge is therefore stranded on the outer surface.
Worked Example A charge of +3×10⁻⁶ C is moved between two points on the surface of an isolated conductor in equilibrium. Since the entire surface is one equipotential, ΔV = 0 between any two surface points, so the work done is W = qΔV = (3×10⁻⁶)(0) = 0 J — regardless of the path taken or how far apart the two points are.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. At any point in space, the electric field is:

Since V doesn't change along an equipotential surface, E (which equals −dV/dl along any direction) can have no component along it — E must point straight through, perpendicular to the surface.

2. Inside the conducting material of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field is:

Any interior field in a conductor would keep pushing free charges until they redistributed enough to cancel it — equilibrium means E = 0 throughout the interior.

3. Fill in the blank: one point on the surface of an isolated conductor in equilibrium is measured at V = 500 V. At any other point on that same conductor's surface, V = ___ V.

A conductor in equilibrium is a single equipotential object — every point on it, interior or surface, shares the same V.

4. Why must any excess charge on an isolated conductor in equilibrium reside entirely on its outer surface?

Since E = 0 on any Gaussian surface drawn inside the conducting material, Gauss's law forces Q_enc = 0 for every such surface — the only way that's possible is if there's no net charge anywhere inside, pushing it all to the outer surface.
