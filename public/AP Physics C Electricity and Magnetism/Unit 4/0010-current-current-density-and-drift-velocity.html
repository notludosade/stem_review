Current, Current Density, and Drift Velocity

Static charge is behind us for good. A light bulb turns on almost instantly — yet the electrons actually carrying that current creep along slower than a snail.

← Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Current and Current Density Current I = dQ/dt, the instantaneous rate at which charge crosses a cross-section of a conductor. Sign convention used throughout this course: conventional current points in the direction positive charge would flow — in a metal, where the actual mobile carriers are negatively charged electrons, conventional current therefore points opposite to the electrons' actual motion. Current density J = I/A (current per cross-sectional area) refines this into a quantity that doesn't depend on how thick the wire happens to be.
Worked Derivation — I = nqAv_d Picture carriers of charge q and number density n (carriers per volume), drifting with average velocity v_d through a wire of cross-section A. In a short time dt, every carrier within a distance v_d dt of the cross-section crosses it — that's a volume A(v_d dt), containing charge dQ = nq·A·v_d·dt. So I = dQ/dt = nqAv_d, and solving for the drift speed: v_d = I/(nqA).
Why the Light Turns On Instantly Anyway Drift velocity turns out to be absurdly slow — often a fraction of a millimeter per second, as the example below shows. What makes a switch feel instantaneous isn't any single electron sprinting the length of the wire; it's that the electric field itself, once the circuit closes, propagates through the conductor at a large fraction of the speed of light. Every electron throughout the entire wire starts drifting at nearly the same moment, even though each one is individually crawling.
Worked Example A copper wire (n ≈ 8.5×10²⁸ electrons/m³) carries I = 2 A through a cross-section A = 1×10⁻⁶ m² (q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C per electron). v_d = I/(nqA) = 2/[(8.5×10²⁸)(1.6×10⁻¹⁹)(1×10⁻⁶)] = 2/(1.36×10⁴) ≈ 1.47×10⁻⁴ m/s — about 0.15 mm every second.

1. Conventional current is defined as pointing in the direction:

Conventional current follows the direction positive charge would move; in a metal, the actual carriers are negative electrons drifting the opposite way.

2. Fill in the blank: a copper wire (n ≈ 8.5×10²⁸ /m³) carries I = 5 A through A = 1×10⁻⁶ m². The drift velocity is approximately ___ ×10⁻⁴ m/s (enter just the coefficient, e.g. 1.47).

v_d = I/(nqA) = 5/[(8.5×10²⁸)(1.6×10⁻¹⁹)(1×10⁻⁶)] = 5/(1.36×10⁴) ≈ 3.68×10⁻⁴ m/s.

3. A light bulb across a room turns on almost the instant a switch closes, even though drift velocity is extremely slow, because:

No individual electron needs to traverse the whole wire — the field's near-light-speed propagation gets every electron in the circuit moving together almost immediately.

4. If current I through a wire doubles while the wire's cross-sectional area A stays fixed, the current density J = I/A:

J = I/A is directly proportional to I at fixed A, so doubling I doubles J.
