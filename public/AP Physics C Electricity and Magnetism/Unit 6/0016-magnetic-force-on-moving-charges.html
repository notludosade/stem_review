Magnetic Force on Moving Charges

A magnetic field does nothing to a charge sitting still. The instant that charge moves, it feels a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field — a force that steers without ever speeding up or slowing down.

← Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — The Magnetic Force Law A charge q moving with velocity v through a magnetic field B feels a force F = qv × B. Magnitude: F = |q|vB sinθ, where θ is the angle between v and B — the force is zero when v is parallel to B and maximal when v is perpendicular to B. Direction: for a positive charge, point your right hand's fingers along v, curl them toward B, and your thumb gives F (the standard cross-product right-hand rule); for a negative charge, the force points the opposite way. Because F is built from a cross product, F is always perpendicular to both v and B, and therefore always perpendicular to the charge's own motion.
Method — Circular Motion in a Uniform Field When v is perpendicular to a uniform B, the magnetic force always points toward the same center — it's a centripetal force, and the charge moves in a circle. Setting the magnetic force equal to mv²/r: |q|vB = mv²/r, which solves to r = mv/(qB). The period of one full orbit is T = 2πm/(qB) — notice v canceled out, so a faster particle simply carves a wider circle in exactly the same amount of time as a slower one.
Why Magnetic Force Never Does Work F = qv × B is perpendicular to v by the very definition of a cross product, so F·v = 0 at every instant — the magnetic force never has a component along the direction of motion. That means it can never speed a charge up or slow it down; kinetic energy and speed stay exactly constant, only the direction of v changes. This is fundamentally different from the electric force, which has no such restriction and can freely change a charge's speed. It's why magnetic fields are used to steer and focus particle beams, while accelerating them is always an electric field's job — a cyclotron alternates the two, letting an E-field speed particles up in straight gaps while a B-field bends them back around.
Worked Example — Radius of Circular Motion A proton (q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, m = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg) moves at v = 2×10⁶ m/s perpendicular to a field B = 0.5 T. Radius: r = mv/(qB) = (1.67×10⁻²⁷)(2×10⁶)/[(1.6×10⁻¹⁹)(0.5)] = (3.34×10⁻²¹)/(8×10⁻²⁰) ≈ 0.0418 m ≈ 4.18 cm. Period: T = 2πm/(qB) = 2π(1.67×10⁻²⁷)/[(1.6×10⁻¹⁹)(0.5)] ≈ 1.31×10⁻⁷ s — and this period wouldn't change even if the proton were moving twice as fast.

1. A positive charge moves in the +y direction through a field B pointing in the +z direction. Using F = qv × B, the force on the charge points:

v × B = ŷ × ẑ = x̂, and since q is positive, F = qv × B points in the same direction as v × B: +x.

2. Fill in the blank: a proton (q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, m = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg) moves at v = 1×10⁶ m/s perpendicular to B = 0.2 T. Its orbit radius is r ≈ ___ cm (2 decimal places).

r = mv/(qB) = (1.67×10⁻²⁷)(1×10⁶)/[(1.6×10⁻¹⁹)(0.2)] = (1.67×10⁻²¹)/(3.2×10⁻²⁰) ≈ 0.0522 m ≈ 5.22 cm.

3. A charged particle moves in a circle inside a uniform magnetic field. Its speed:

F·v = 0 for a magnetic force at every instant, so no work is ever done on the particle — kinetic energy and speed remain constant; only the direction of motion changes.

4. A charged particle's speed is doubled while q, m, and B are all held fixed. What happens to its orbit radius and period?

r = mv/(qB) is directly proportional to v, so doubling v doubles r. But T = 2πm/(qB) has no v in it at all — a faster particle just travels a proportionally larger circle in the same time, so the period is unaffected.
