FDM Printing Fundamentals — Layers, Infill, Supports, and Orientation

A 3D printer doesn't see a solid model — it sees a stack of flat slices, printed one at a time, each one needing something solid underneath it.

← Unit 4, Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Everything up to this unit was about the design existing as geometry. This unit is about actually making it — starting with FDM, the most common desktop 3D printing process, and the handful of concepts that shape nearly every decision a designer makes about a printed part.

FDM: building up, one layer at a time

Definition FDM (Fused Deposition Modeling) builds a part by extruding molten thermoplastic filament through a heated nozzle, depositing it in a pattern one thin layer at a time, from the bottom up — each new layer fusing to the one directly below it as it cools.
Layer height The thickness of each individual deposited layer — commonly 0.1mm to 0.3mm. Smaller layer height gives finer surface detail and less visible stepping on curved or angled surfaces, at the cost of more layers and longer print time. Larger layer height prints faster but shows more visible "stair-stepping."
Infill percentage The density of the internal lattice pattern printed inside a part that isn't fully solid. 0% is a hollow shell; 100% is fully solid. Most functional parts land somewhere between 10% and 50% — a deliberate strength/weight/print-time trade-off, covered quantitatively next lesson.
Supports Temporary printed structures generated under overhanging geometry that would otherwise be printed into empty air, then removed after printing finishes. They exist because FDM fundamentally can't print into nothing — every new layer needs something solid directly beneath it to fuse onto.

Print orientation — a free decision with outsized consequences

How a part is rotated on the print bed, before slicing even begins, affects three things at once, none of which require changing the model's geometry:

Support requirements Orientations that minimize steep overhangs reduce (or eliminate) support material, print time, and the surface marring supports leave behind when removed.
Strength direction Layers bond less strongly to each other than the plastic within a single layer bonds to itself. A part loaded in a direction that tries to peel layers apart is meaningfully weaker than the same part loaded along the layer plane instead — this becomes central in Lesson 15.
Print time A taller orientation generally means more layers, and more layers generally means a longer print — the exact relationship is Lesson 14's subject.
Why orientation should be decided before tuning slicer settings An L-shaped bracket printed flat (both legs on the bed) needs zero support for the horizontal leg, and the vertical leg's height becomes many thin layers running along its length — if the load is applied vertically, that's strong layer bonding in the load direction. The same bracket printed on its side may need support at the corner, and now the layer lines run perpendicular to that same vertical load, making it more prone to splitting along a layer line. No slicer setting fixes a bad orientation choice — it should be decided first, since it changes strength and support needs more than nearly any other single setting.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does FDM printing need support material under steep overhangs?

FDM builds strictly bottom-up, layer by layer — without something solid underneath, a new layer has nothing to fuse to and can't form correctly.

2. What is the main trade-off of choosing a smaller layer height?

A fixed part height divided into more, thinner layers takes proportionally longer to print — that's the direct cost of the smoother surface finish.

3. Why does print orientation affect a part's strength, independent of any slicer setting?

Layer-to-layer bonds are inherently weaker than within-layer bonds — orientation decides whether an expected load tries to peel layers apart or loads them in-plane.

4. Fill in the blank: the density of the internal lattice pattern printed inside a part, ranging from 0% (hollow) to 100% (solid), is called ___.

Infill percentage is a deliberate strength/weight/print-time trade-off, not simply "more is always better."

5. Fill in the blank: rotating a part on the print bed before slicing, which affects support needs, layer-bond strength direction, and print time all at once, is called print ___.

Orientation is a free decision — no geometry change required — with a bigger real-world effect on print success and strength than most slicer settings.
