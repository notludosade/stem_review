Dimensional Tolerance and Tolerance Stack-Up

No manufactured part ever comes out at exactly its nominal size. Tolerance says how much variation is acceptable — and a chain of tolerances adds up fast.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Units 1 and 2 treated dimensions as exact numbers. In the real world, they never are — every cutting, printing, and machining process produces parts that land somewhere near their target size, not exactly on it. This lesson is about how CAD dimensions actually account for that.

Nominal size and tolerance

Definition The nominal size is a dimension's target value — what the drawing calls it, in round numbers. The tolerance is the allowable range of variation around that nominal size, usually written as upper and lower limits. A dimension written 25.00 ± 0.05mm means the part is acceptable anywhere from 24.95mm (lower limit) to 25.05mm (upper limit) — nominal 25.00mm, tolerance ±0.05mm.

Tighter tolerance (a smaller ± value) means less allowed variation, which generally means a slower, more expensive, or more carefully controlled manufacturing process. Tolerance is a deliberate choice, not just a formality: too loose, and the part might not function; too tight, and it costs far more than necessary to make.

Tolerance stack-up

Definition Tolerance stack-up happens when several toleranced dimensions combine in a chain — several parts stacked end to end, or several dimensions locating one feature. The individual tolerances add together, so the total possible variation across the whole chain can be substantially larger than any single dimension's own tolerance.

For dimensions that add in a simple chain, the worst-case total tolerance is the straightforward sum of the individual tolerances, since in the worst case every part in the chain could simultaneously land at its own extreme:

T_total (worst-case) = T₁ + T₂ + T₃ + …
Worked Example — Three Parts in a Housing Three parts are stacked inside a housing: Part A = 10.00 ± 0.05mm, Part B = 15.00 ± 0.03mm, Part C = 8.00 ± 0.02mm. Nominal stack length = 10.00 + 15.00 + 8.00 = 33.00mm. Worst-case tolerance = 0.05 + 0.03 + 0.02 = ±0.10mm. So the actual stack could measure anywhere from 32.90mm to 33.10mm, even though every individual part is perfectly within its own spec.
A less conservative alternative: RSS (statistical) stack-up Worst-case stack-up assumes every part lands at its extreme simultaneously — unlikely in practice, since manufacturing variation is usually random and roughly independent across parts. A common statistical alternative, root-sum-square (RSS) stack-up, gives a less conservative (but not guaranteed-safe) estimate: T_total(RSS) = √(T₁² + T₂² + T₃² + …). For the example above: √(0.05² + 0.03² + 0.02²) = √0.0038 ≈ 0.062mm — notably smaller than the worst-case 0.10mm, because it's very unlikely all three parts hit their extremes at once. Worst-case is the safer number to design against when a small batch or a safety-critical fit is involved; RSS is common in high-volume production where the statistics genuinely apply.
Why this can't be assumed away An assembly designed only against nominal dimensions can look perfectly fine on paper, while the real worst-case stack-up quietly eats all the clearance meant for something else — a gap meant for a moving part, room for a gasket, the slop a fastener needs to actually thread in. Every dimension in a chain adds to (or eats into) the final tolerance budget; stack-up has to be checked deliberately, dimension by dimension, not assumed to work out.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A dimension is written 40.00 ± 0.08mm. What is the lower limit?

Lower limit = nominal − tolerance = 40.00 − 0.08 = 39.92mm.

2. Two dimensions in a chain, 20.00 ± 0.04mm and 12.00 ± 0.03mm, stack end to end. What is the worst-case total tolerance?

Worst-case stack-up sums the individual tolerances: 0.04 + 0.03 = ±0.07mm.

3. Why does worst-case stack-up assume all parts land at their extreme simultaneously, while RSS stack-up gives a smaller number?

Worst-case is the safer, guaranteed bound; RSS is a less conservative statistical estimate appropriate when variation across many parts is genuinely independent and random.

4. Fill in the blank: the target value a dimension is written around, before tolerance is applied, is called its ___ size.

Nominal size is the round-number target; tolerance is the allowed variation around it.

5. Fill in the blank: when several toleranced dimensions combine in a chain and their individual tolerances add together, this is called tolerance ___.

Tolerance stack-up is exactly this accumulation — the reason a chain of "acceptable" parts can still fail to fit as an assembly.
