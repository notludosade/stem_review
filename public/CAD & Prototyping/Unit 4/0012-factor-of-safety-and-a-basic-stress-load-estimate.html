Factor of Safety and a Basic Stress/Load Estimate

Before printing or cutting a load-bearing part, a quick stress estimate answers a question CAD geometry alone can't: will this cross-section actually hold?

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 4 has covered what a material is (Lesson 10) and whether a process can produce a given shape (Lesson 11). This lesson closes the unit with the question that actually determines whether a part survives: given an expected load and a cross-section, is there enough margin before failure?

Stress: force over area, again

Definition Stress (σ) is internal force divided by the cross-sectional area carrying it: σ = F / A. A given force spreads over a small area produces far higher stress than the same force spread over a large area — which is exactly why a part's cross-section, not just the raw force on it, determines whether it survives.
σ = F / A

Factor of safety

Definition The factor of safety (FS) is the ratio of a material's failure (yield) strength to the actual stress the part is expected to experience: FS = yield strength / expected stress. FS = 1 means a part is designed to fail at exactly its expected load; real designs almost always target FS well above 1.
FS = yield strength / expected stress
Worked Example — A Printed PLA Bracket Tab A tab on a PLA bracket must support 150N. Its cross-section is 6mm wide × 3mm thick = 18mm² = 18×10⁻⁶ m². Stress: σ = F/A = 150 / (18×10⁻⁶) ≈ 8.33×10⁶ Pa ≈ 8.33 MPa. PLA's yield strength (Lesson 10's table) is about 60 MPa, so FS = 60 / 8.33 ≈ 7.2.
Why a printed part's real strength usually isn't the bulk material's strength Published material strengths (like PLA's ~60 MPa) describe fully solid, bulk material — but a printed part built from fused layers, with less-than-100% infill, typically achieves only about 60–80% of the bulk material's rated strength, depending on print orientation, infill, and settings (Unit 5 covers exactly why). A prudent designer derates the strength before computing FS rather than assuming full bulk strength: using 70% of PLA's 60 MPa gives an effective yield of 42 MPa, and the bracket tab's FS becomes 42 / 8.33 ≈ 5.0 — still healthy, but a real drop from the naive 7.2.
How much FS is "enough" For typical prototyping applications — jigs, brackets, enclosures, non-critical mechanisms — an FS of roughly 2 to 4 is a common conservative rule of thumb, after any appropriate derating. Safety-critical applications need far more rigorous engineering analysis than a single FS number from a classroom estimate, and should always be physically tested, not designed from hand-calculation alone.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A part carries a force of 80N over a cross-section 5mm × 2mm (10mm²). What is the stress?

σ = F/A = 80 / (10×10⁻⁶ m²) = 8×10⁶ Pa = 8.0 MPa.

2. Using the 8.0 MPa stress from question 1, and a PLA yield strength of 60 MPa, what is the factor of safety?

FS = yield / stress = 60 / 8.0 = 7.5.

3. Why should a designer derate a printed material's rated bulk strength before computing FS for a printed part?

Layer adhesion and infill mean printed parts generally underperform bulk-material strength ratings — ignoring this overstates the real factor of safety.

4. Fill in the blank: internal force divided by cross-sectional area is called ___.

σ = F/A — stress is what actually determines whether a cross-section survives a given force, not the raw force alone.

5. Fill in the blank: for typical non-critical prototyping applications like jigs and brackets, a common conservative factor-of-safety rule of thumb is roughly ___ (the low end of the usual target range).

Roughly 2 to 4 is a common target range for non-critical prototyping applications, after accounting for any appropriate derating.
