Design for Manufacturability — Draft Angles, Minimum Wall Thickness, and Undercuts

A geometrically perfect CAD model can still be physically impossible for a given process to produce. DFM is the process's physical limits, folded back into the design decision.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Every manufacturing process has physical rules a CAD model can violate without the software ever complaining — the model will render fine, the file will save fine, and the part will still be impossible to actually make. Design for manufacturability (DFM) means designing around those rules from the start.

Draft angle

Definition A draft angle is a slight taper — typically 1° to 3° per side — added to a part's vertical walls so it can be pulled out of a mold or cast without scraping or sticking. Without draft, a wall parallel to the pull direction can grip the mold through friction or vacuum even when it's dimensionally free to release.

Draft matters specifically for mold- and cast-based processes (injection molding, die casting). It's not relevant to FDM 3D printing or laser cutting — neither process pulls a part out of a rigid tool — but it's worth designing in from the start if a part might later move to mass production by molding, since retrofitting draft onto an already-finalized model can force other dimensions to shift with it.

Minimum wall thickness

Definition Minimum wall thickness is the thinnest a wall can be made and still reliably fill, print, or cut without warping, breaking, or failing to form. It's process-dependent: injection-molded ABS commonly needs walls of at least 1.0–1.5mm to fill reliably; FDM 3D printing commonly needs at least 0.8–1.2mm depending on nozzle width (Unit 5 covers this quantitatively). Laser-cut parts are inherently the full stock thickness everywhere — "wall thickness" there really means the minimum width of a thin feature, so a sliver doesn't snap off during cutting or handling.

Undercuts

Definition An undercut is a feature that blocks a part from being made cleanly by a given process — a hook or internal lip that a mold can't pull straight past, an overhang an FDM printer can't print without added support material, or a feature a 2-axis laser cutter simply cannot reach because it only cuts straight through flat stock.
Example — A Snap-Fit Clip A one-piece molded snap-fit clip needs a retention hook that, by definition, blocks the mold from pulling straight up and off the part — an undercut relative to the main pull direction. Handling it means either a mold with a side-action or lifter (added tooling cost), or redesigning the hook as a flexible cantilever the mold can release straight up, trading a small amount of holding force for a dramatically simpler (and cheaper) mold.
Why DFM rules aren't arbitrary restrictions Every one of these rules is the physical process talking back to the design, not an arbitrary style guideline. A wall too thin to fill, a wall with no draft that grips its mold, an undercut with nowhere for the tool to go — none of these are things a CAD file can "just handle" through better modeling technique. Catching them during design is close to free; catching them after a mold is cut or a print run has already failed is not.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is a draft angle for, and which processes does it matter for?

Draft angle exists specifically so a molded or cast part can be pulled free of its tool without friction or vacuum grip — it isn't relevant to processes that don't use a pull-apart mold.

2. What does "minimum wall thickness" mean for a laser-cut part, specifically?

Since a laser-cut part is always the full material thickness, the relevant "wall thickness" concern shifts to how narrow a thin feature can be before it's too fragile to survive cutting and handling.

3. In the snap-fit clip example, what makes the retention hook an "undercut" specifically?

An undercut is defined relative to a specific process's motion or reach — here, the hook geometrically blocks the mold's straight-line pull direction.

4. Fill in the blank: a feature that geometrically blocks a given manufacturing process from producing a part cleanly — like a hook a mold can't pull past — is called an ___.

Undercuts are process-specific — a feature that's fine for one process (like FDM printing with support material) may be a hard blocker for another (like injection molding without a side-action).

5. Fill in the blank: designing a part around a specific manufacturing process's physical limits, from the start, is called ___.

DFM folds the manufacturing process's real physical constraints back into the design decision, rather than discovering them after the fact.
