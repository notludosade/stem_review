1. A material barely deflects under load but shatters suddenly with almost no warning at a fairly low stress. Which two properties does this describe?

Barely deflecting under load means high stiffness (resists elastic deformation); shattering at low stress means low strength (fails early) — the two properties are independent.

2. Fill in the blank: for a weight-limited design, comparing strength ÷ ___ (specific strength) is usually more meaningful than comparing raw strength alone.

Specific strength normalizes strength by density, which is what actually matters when a design is limited by weight, not raw strength.

3. Why does an injection-molded part typically need a draft angle on its vertical walls, while an FDM-printed part doesn't?

Draft angle exists specifically to let a part release from a mold or cast — a concern that simply doesn't apply to a process, like FDM, that never pulls a part out of a rigid tool.

4. Fill in the blank: a factor of safety of exactly ___ means a part is designed to fail at exactly its expected load, with no margin at all.

FS = 1 means yield strength equals expected stress exactly — no margin above the expected load.

5. A one-piece molded part has an internal hook that blocks the mold from pulling straight off. What is this feature called?

An undercut is any feature that geometrically blocks a given process from producing the part cleanly — here, blocking the mold's straight-line pull direction.

6. A bracket tab carries 200N over a cross-section 8mm × 4mm (32mm²), made from a material with 60 MPa yield strength. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the stress is σ = F/A = ___ MPa.

(b) Fill in the blank: the factor of safety is FS = yield / stress = ___.

(a) σ = 200N / (32×10⁻⁶ m²) = 6.25×10⁶ Pa = 6.25 MPa. (b) FS = 60 / 6.25 = 9.6.

7. A designer is choosing between PLA and Nylon for a bracket that will be dropped occasionally outdoors. Answer both parts:

(a) Which material is generally tougher (more resistant to sudden impact) but also absorbs moisture and is harder to print well?

(b) Fill in the blank: PLA is generally the stiffest and easiest of common 3D-printing plastics to print cleanly, but also the most ___ under sudden impact.

(a) Nylon is the toughest of the three common printing plastics discussed, at the cost of moisture sensitivity and printability. (b) PLA's brittleness is the trade-off for its stiffness and ease of printing.

8. A designer computes FS using PLA's full rated bulk yield strength of 60 MPa for a printed part. Answer both parts:

(a) Why might this overstate the part's real factor of safety?

(b) Fill in the blank: the prudent practice is to ___ the bulk material strength before computing FS for a printed part.

(a) Layer adhesion and infill mean printed strength typically falls short of bulk-material ratings. (b) Derating (reducing) the assumed strength before computing FS avoids overstating the real margin.

9. A designer is preparing the same thin rib feature for both FDM printing and laser cutting. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does "minimum wall thickness" mean something different for the laser-cut version?

(b) Fill in the blank: designing a feature around a specific process's physical limits, from the start, is called ___.

(a) Since laser cutting doesn't build up thin walls the way FDM does, its "wall thickness" concern is really about a feature's minimum width. (b) This process-aware design practice is DFM.

10. A designer computes FS = 3.2 for a non-critical printed jig, after appropriately derating the material's rated strength. Answer both parts:

(a) Is this generally an acceptable margin for a non-critical prototyping application?

(b) Fill in the blank: for a safety-critical application, a hand-calculated FS is not enough — the part should always be physically ___ as well.

(a) 3.2 sits comfortably within the common 2–4 range for non-critical prototyping parts. (b) Safety-critical applications need rigorous engineering analysis and physical testing, not a single hand-calculated FS number.
