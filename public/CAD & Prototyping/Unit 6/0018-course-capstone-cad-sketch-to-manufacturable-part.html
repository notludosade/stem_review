Course Capstone — From Parametric Sketch to Manufacturable Part

Every stage of this course — sketch to assembly to tolerance to manufacturing method — converges on one decision: given a real design goal, how should it actually get made?

← Lesson 17 Contents Glossary →

Eighteen lessons ago, this course opened with a single constrained line in a 2D sketch. Since then, sketches became parametric models, single parts became assemblies with tracked degrees of freedom, nominal dimensions grew tolerances and fits, and materials met the physical limits of two very different manufacturing processes. This capstone walks one design goal through that entire pipeline, ending where every real project ends: choosing how to actually make the thing.

The design goal

Design Brief A small desk-organizer bracket, roughly 100mm × 60mm × 30mm, needs to hold a phone at a fixed angle. It's needed in a batch of 5 for a class project, and it includes a slot that must snugly hold a 3mm-thick phone case — no compound-curved surfaces anywhere in the design.

The pipeline, briefly retraced

A parametric sketch (Unit 1) defines the bracket's profile with named parameters — width, height, slot width — so the slot dimension can be tuned without rebuilding the model from scratch. If the bracket were split into a base and a separate wedge support, assembly mates (Unit 2) would define exactly how they fit and what stays free to move. The slot itself needs a deliberate fit choice (Unit 3): holding a 3mm phone case without rattling calls for something closer to a transition fit than a loose clearance fit. And the material and process choice (Unit 4 and this unit) decide whether that fit is even achievable at the scale this part needs.

3D printing vs. laser cutting, for this specific design

Decision Framework, Applied to This Brief
FactorFDM 3D PrintingLaser Cutting
Geometry fitHandles any 3D shape, including compound curvesFlat stock only — geometrically cannot produce a compound-curved surface
Slot toleranceTypically ±0.1–0.2mm on X/Y features on a well-tuned consumer printerTypically ±0.05–0.1mm once kerf-compensated (Lesson 17), and more repeatable part-to-part without recalibration
Batch of 5, flat designPrints sequentially — often on the order of a couple hours per part for a part this sizeAll 5 nest onto one sheet and cut in a single job — often just minutes total, regardless of quantity
Finishing detailPossible but adds print time (raised text, etc.)Raster engraving (Lesson 16) adds a logo or label cheaply in the same job
The Verdict — and Why It's Quantitative, Not a Guess This specific brief has no compound-curved surfaces — it's fundamentally a flat, 2.5D shape. That single fact already rules out the one advantage that would force 3D printing regardless of cost: laser cutting cannot produce a true compound curve, but this design doesn't need one. Given that, the batch-of-5 math and slot tolerance both favor laser cutting: nesting five flat panels on one sheet and cutting them in one job (minutes) beats five sequential prints (hours, stacking up), and a kerf-compensated slot (Lesson 17) reliably lands within the tolerance a snug phone-case fit needs, without the part-to-part calibration drift consumer FDM printers are more prone to. Laser cutting wins for this brief — not because it's always better, but because none of this design's requirements happen to need what only 3D printing can do. Change one requirement — say, a compound-curved cradle instead of a flat angled slot — and the verdict flips immediately, regardless of the cost and time numbers above, because that requirement becomes a hard geometric constraint laser cutting simply cannot satisfy.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. For a flat, batch-of-5 design with no compound curves, why does laser cutting typically win on cost and time over FDM printing?

Nesting a batch onto one flat sheet is a decisive time advantage for laser cutting specifically when the design is flat enough to nest — it doesn't apply to every design.

2. What would flip this capstone's verdict from laser cutting to 3D printing, regardless of the cost/time numbers?

A compound curve is a hard geometric limit — laser cutting works on flat stock only, so no amount of favorable cost or time math can substitute for a process that can't produce the shape at all.

3. Why does this bracket's slot call for something closer to a transition fit rather than a loose clearance fit?

A clearance fit guarantees a gap, which is exactly wrong for a slot meant to hold something snugly — the same reasoning as Lesson 8's dowel-pin example.

4. Fill in the blank: hitting a tight slot tolerance on the laser-cut version of this bracket depends directly on applying ___ from Lesson 17.

Without kerf compensation, the slot would come out systematically oversized by roughly the kerf width, undermining the snug fit the design needs.

5. Fill in the blank: defining the bracket's slot width as a named variable in the sketch, rather than a fixed typed-in number, is what makes the design ___ (Unit 1) — able to be re-tuned for a different phone case thickness without rebuilding the model.

A parametric slot width means adapting the bracket to a different phone case is a one-parameter change, not a redesign.
