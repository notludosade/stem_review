Degrees of Freedom in an Assembly — What "Fully Constrained" Means

Every rigid part starts with 6 ways it can move in 3D space. Mates spend that budget down, one mate at a time, until nothing (or exactly the intended something) is left.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 counted degrees of freedom for 2D sketch geometry. This lesson does the same thing one dimension up: a rigid 3D part has 6 degrees of freedom, and every mate removes some specific number of them.

Six degrees of freedom in 3D

Definition A free rigid body in 3D space has 6 degrees of freedom: 3 translational (sliding along x, y, and z) and 3 rotational (spinning about x, y, and z). An assembly component starts with all 6 the moment it's dropped into an assembly with no mates applied yet.

A simplified DOF-removal model per mate

Different mate types remove different amounts of freedom. The table below is a common simplified accounting used for reasoning about a fresh mate applied to freedoms nothing else has touched yet:

Simplified Mate Budget
MateDOF removedWhat it fixes
Coincident (planar face-to-face)31 translation (perpendicular to the face) + 2 rotations (tilt)
Concentric (cylindrical axis-to-axis)42 translations (radial position) + 2 rotations (axis direction)
Distance11 translation along the constrained direction
Angle11 rotation about the constrained axis
This model assumes each new mate is constraining freedom nothing earlier has already touched. Real assemblies aren't always that clean — the worked example below shows exactly what happens when a later mate overlaps with one already applied.
Worked Example — Fully Constraining the Pin from Lesson 4 Start at 6 DOF. Concentric mate (pin axis to hole axis): removes 4 DOF (2 radial translations + 2 tilt rotations) → 2 DOF remain (slide along the axis, spin about the axis). Coincident mate (pin's shoulder face to bracket face): in general this removes 3 DOF, but the concentric mate already removed both tilt rotations — the only freedom left for this face-to-face contact to remove is the 1 remaining axial translation. 2 − 1 = 1 DOF remains (spin only). A final angle mate (aligning a flat on the pin to a matching flat on the bracket) removes the last DOF — the spin. 1 − 1 = 0. Fully constrained, using exactly 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 DOF worth of mates against a 6 DOF budget.
Why you can't just sum the table blindly The coincident mate in the example above "should" remove 3 DOF by the general rule, but only had 1 left to give, because the concentric mate had already claimed the other 2. CAD software doesn't sum a fixed table at all — it tracks the assembly's actual remaining freedom directly, and flags a mate as redundant if it tries to remove freedom that's already gone (harmless if the redundant mate agrees with what's already true, an error if it conflicts) or under-constrained if freedom remains. The table above is a reasoning tool for a human, not the literal algorithm running underneath.

Just as with sketches (Lesson 1), an assembly component can be under-constrained (DOF > 0 — it can still be dragged around, which is exactly right for a hinge pin or a slider that's supposed to move), fully constrained (0 DOF — locked in place), or produce a conflict error if two mates demand contradictory positions for the same freedom.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. How many total degrees of freedom does a free rigid part have in 3D space, before any mates are applied?

3 translational (x, y, z) + 3 rotational (about x, y, z) = 6 total.

2. In the pin worked example, why does the coincident (shoulder-face) mate only remove 1 DOF instead of the usual 3?

The general rule (3 DOF) assumes no prior overlap — here, the concentric mate already claimed 2 of those 3 freedoms, leaving only 1 for the coincident mate to actually remove.

3. What does CAD software do when a new mate tries to remove a degree of freedom that's already been removed by an earlier mate, and the two mates disagree?

Two mates that disagree about the same already-removed freedom produce a conflict error — the same "flag it, don't guess" behavior as an overdefined sketch.

4. Fill in the blank: an assembly component is fully constrained when exactly ___ degrees of freedom remain.

Same idea as a fully constrained sketch: zero DOF means the part can't move at all without violating a mate.

5. Fill in the blank: a mate that tries to remove a degree of freedom already removed by an earlier mate — and agrees with it rather than conflicting — is called ___.

A redundant mate is harmless (it agrees with an already-established position) but still worth noticing, since it can hide the fact that the "real" constraint doing the work is somewhere else.
