LANs, WANs, and the Internet as a Network of Networks

"The internet" isn't one network — it's a name for the agreement that lets millions of independently owned networks forward each other's traffic.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 defined a network as nodes plus links plus protocols, and pointed out that two laptops with a cable between them already qualify. That's obviously not the same scale of thing as "the internet." This lesson fills in the missing size categories, and — more importantly — explains that the internet isn't a single giant network at all. It's a network of networks, held together by nothing more than a shared agreement to forward each other's traffic.

LAN: a network that fits in one place

Definition A LAN (Local Area Network) is a network confined to a small physical area — a home, a single office, a school building. Your home Wi-Fi, connecting your laptop, phone, and smart TV to a single router, is a LAN. LANs are typically owned and managed by one person or organization, and the devices on them are usually connected by cables the owner installed or a Wi-Fi signal the owner controls.

WAN: a network that spans distance

Definition A WAN (Wide Area Network) is a network that spans a large geographic area — connecting LANs in different cities, states, or countries. A company with offices in New York and Tokyo, with a private connection linking their two office LANs, has built a WAN. WANs almost always rely on infrastructure the owner doesn't fully control — leased fiber lines, satellite links, or another company's cables — because no single organization owns cable running under an ocean.
Telling them apart A university connecting every computer in one building: LAN. That same university connecting its main campus network to a second campus 300 miles away: WAN. A coffee shop's guest Wi-Fi: LAN. Your internet service provider's network connecting your neighborhood to the rest of the world: functionally a WAN (and, as the next section covers, one piece of a much bigger picture).

The internet: a network of networks

Here's the part that trips people up: the internet is not owned by any single company or government, and it isn't one continuous network engineered top-down. It's an enormous, decentralized collection of independently owned and operated networks — home LANs, business LANs, university networks, and the WANs run by internet service providers (ISPs) and backbone carriers — that have all agreed to speak the same core set of protocols (which you'll meet starting in Unit 2) and to forward each other's traffic.

Why "network of networks" is the accurate description, not a simplification When you send data to a server on the other side of the world, that data doesn't travel through one company's wires the whole way. It leaves your home LAN, enters your ISP's network, gets handed off to one or more much larger backbone networks that specialize in long-haul transport, gets handed off again to the destination's ISP, and finally arrives at the destination's LAN. Each of those networks is independently owned, independently managed, and often run by a company that competes with the others — yet they all forward traffic that isn't theirs, because doing so is what makes the whole internet useful to everyone, including themselves. No single point of control exists; there's no "internet headquarters" that could flip a global off switch. That property is a direct consequence of it being built from cooperating networks rather than one centrally administered one.

This is also why the internet has no single owner to blame when something breaks, and no single company you could call to "fix the internet." A problem is almost always localized to one network along the path — your LAN, your ISP, a specific backbone link, or the destination's network — and diagnosing which one is broken is exactly the skill Lesson 26 builds toward, once you have the vocabulary to talk about each layer of the journey.

Worked example: tracing the ownership boundaries You (on your home LAN) load a website hosted on a server in another country. Ownership boundaries crossed, in order: your home LAN (you own it) → your ISP's network (they own it) → one or more backbone/transit networks (a different company owns each) → the destination's ISP (another company) → the destination's data center LAN (the hosting company owns it). Five or more independently owned networks, none of which answer to a single authority, cooperated to deliver one web page — and did it, typically, in well under a second.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What's the key difference between a LAN and a WAN?

Scale and ownership are the defining traits — not speed, wiring type, or device count, which vary within both categories.

2. Why is "the internet is one giant network" an inaccurate way to describe it?

It's a cooperating network of networks, with ownership and management split across many independent organizations.

3. What consequence follows from the internet having no central point of control?

Decentralization means no single failure point can take the whole thing down — but it also means troubleshooting means figuring out which specific network along the path is at fault.

4. Fill in the blank: the company that connects a home or business LAN to the wider internet is called an ___.

An ISP is the on-ramp connecting a smaller local network to the much larger network of networks.
