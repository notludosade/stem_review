Continuity and Types of Discontinuity

"Draw it without lifting your pencil" made precise — and the three distinct ways a function can fail at it.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Every widget in Lessons 1–3 quietly relied on limits and function values sometimes disagreeing — that's exactly what made them worth studying. Continuity is the special, well-behaved case where they don't disagree at all. Pinning that down precisely also gives a vocabulary for exactly how it can fail.

1. The three-part definition

Definition f is continuous at x = c only if all three hold: (1) f(c) is defined, (2) limx→c f(x) exists, and (3) limx→c f(x) = f(c). Fail any one of the three and f is discontinuous at c — but which one fails tells you what kind of discontinuity it is.

2. Three functions, same point, three different failures

All three functions below have a problem at x = 2 — but drag through each tab and check the list to see it's never the same problem.

Try It — Three Kinds of Discontinuity at x = 2

p(x) = x + 1 everywhere except at x = 2 itself, where p(2) is defined to be 5 instead.

—

  • ✓ f(2) is defined — f(2) = 5 (the filled dot)
  • ✓ the limit exists — limx→2 p(x) = 3 (the hollow dot)
  • ✗ limit ≠ f(2) — 3 ≠ 5, so continuity fails right here

q(x) = x for x < 2, and q(x) = x + 2 for x ≥ 2.

—

  • ✓ f(2) is defined — f(2) = 4
  • ✗ the limit exists — left side → 2, right side → 4, they disagree, so limx→2 q(x) does not exist
  • ✗ limit = f(2) — can't even ask, since there's no limit to compare

r(x) = 1 / (x − 2). Drag close to x = 2 from either side and watch the readout grow without bound.

—

  • ✗ f(2) is defined — 1/0 is undefined outright
  • ✗ the limit exists — r(x) grows without bound on both sides, so there's no finite number to converge to
  • ✗ limit = f(2) — neither side of this even exists to compare

3. Only one of these can be "fixed"

Look back at the removable case: the limit does exist (3) — the only problem is that f(2) was set to the wrong value. Redefine p(2) = 3 instead of 5, and all three conditions hold; the discontinuity is gone. That's precisely why it's called removable. The jump and infinite cases have no such fix — there's no single value you could assign at x = 2 that would make a nonexistent limit suddenly exist. A hole can be patched; a cliff or a spike cannot.

Check your recall

1. In the removable-discontinuity panel, which single condition fails at x = 2?

Both f(2) = 5 and limx→2 p(x) = 3 exist individually — they just don't match each other, which is condition 3 failing specifically.

2. Why can't the jump discontinuity in q(x) be fixed by redefining q(2)?

Redefining a single point can only fix condition 3 (limit vs. f(c)). It can't manufacture a limit that doesn't exist in the first place — and here the left and right limits disagree, so there's no single number to patch in.

3. A discontinuity where the function grows without bound near a point, like r(x) = 1/(x − 2) at x = 2, is called an ___ discontinuity.

Both f(2) and the limit fail to exist here, and the reason is the same in both cases: r(x) shoots toward positive or negative infinity as x approaches 2, which is exactly what an infinite discontinuity looks like.
