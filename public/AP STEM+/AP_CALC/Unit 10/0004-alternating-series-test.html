The Alternating Series Test

The harmonic series Σ1/n diverges. Just flip every other sign, and it converges. Sign-flipping is a genuinely different mechanism for taming an infinite sum.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

1. Convergence by cancellation

Definition — Alternating Series An alternating series has the form Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹ bₙ (or Σ(−1)ⁿ bₙ), where bₙ > 0 for every n — the sign strictly alternates term to term. Every series in this lesson has this shape.
Method — The Alternating Series Test Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹ bₙ converges if both hold: (1) bₙ is eventually decreasing (bₙ₊₁ ≤ bₙ), and (2) limₙ→∞ bₙ = 0. Unlike the divergence test, here a limit of 0 is exactly what's needed — combined with decreasing terms, each new term overcorrects the running total by a shrinking amount, squeezing it toward a single value.
Worked Example The alternating harmonic series Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n = 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ...: bₙ = 1/n is decreasing, and limₙ→∞ 1/n = 0. Both conditions hold, so it converges — even though the un-alternated Σ1/n from Lesson 2 diverges. It converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.6931 (a fact usually proved with power series in Lesson 9), but the test itself only certifies convergence, not the value.

2. Watching the overshoot shrink

Each partial sum overshoots ln 2 in the opposite direction from the one before — but by a smaller amount every time, since the terms are shrinking.
