Choosing the Right Strategy

Every technique in this course, reduced to a handful of questions you can ask about any claim before writing a single line of proof.

← Lesson 14 Contents Glossary →

Fourteen lessons have each introduced one technique in isolation, with the claim already sorted into the right bucket for you. Real claims don't arrive labeled. This capstone lesson is the missing piece: a small set of questions that, asked in order, point you toward the right technique before you've committed to an approach that stalls halfway through.

The Decision Framework 1. Unpack the claim first (Lessons 2–3). Identify every quantifier and the exact condition being asserted — you cannot choose a strategy for a claim you haven't fully parsed.
2. Is it a "for every natural number n" claim, especially one built from smaller cases? → Induction. If proving case k+1 only ever needs case k itself, use weak induction; if it could need an arbitrary earlier case, use strong induction.
3. Is it a conditional, P → Q? → Try direct proof first, always. If assuming P doesn't unpack into anything workable but ¬Q does, switch to contrapositive. If neither gets traction — often because the claim denies something ("is irrational," "has no solution") — use contradiction, assuming P and ¬Q together.
4. Does it assert something is impossible or doesn't exist? → Contradiction, directly: assume the impossible thing exists (or the claim is false), and derive a falsehood.
5. Does it naturally split by an underlying property (parity, sign, remainder)? → Proof by cases, with an exhaustive split, usually using direct proof inside each case.
6. Does it claim an object exists, is the only one, or both? → Existence (produce a witness), uniqueness (assume two witnesses, show they're equal), or both separately for ∃!.
Why "try direct proof first" is step 3, not an afterthought Direct proof is the default for a reason: it's the easiest technique to verify, the easiest to read, and it needs no auxiliary trick. Every other technique in this course exists specifically because direct proof sometimes stalls — an awkward hypothesis (contrapositive), a claim that denies existence (contradiction), infinitely many cases at once (induction), a domain that splits naturally (cases). Reaching for contradiction or contrapositive before actually attempting direct proof first is choosing a heavier tool before checking whether the light one works.

Reference — One Line Per Unit Unit 2: assume P, reason forward, land on Q. Unit 3: prove ¬Q → ¬P instead (contrapositive), or assume P ∧ ¬Q and break something (contradiction). Unit 4: prove a base case, then show truth propagates forward one step (or from any earlier step) at a time. Unit 5: split into exhaustive cases, or split "does it exist" from "is it the only one."
Try It — Run the Decision Framework on Your Own Claim

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Claim: "There is no largest even number." Which technique fits best?

A "no largest" / "no such object exists" claim is exactly contradiction's territory: assume one exists, and derive a contradiction (here, by exhibiting a still-larger even number).

2. Claim: "For every n ≥ 1, 1/(1·2) + 1/(2·3) + ⋯ + 1/(n(n+1)) = n/(n+1)." Which technique fits best?

A claim about every natural number n, where the (n+1)-term sum is the n-term sum plus one new piece, is exactly induction's shape — here, weak induction, since only the immediately preceding case is needed.

3. Claim: "If xy is irrational, then x is irrational or y is irrational." Which technique fits best?

The hypothesis "xy is irrational" gives little to compute with directly, while the contrapositive's hypothesis — both x and y rational — unpacks immediately into fractions whose product is easy to compute.

4. Claim: "Every positive integer can be written as a sum of distinct powers of 2." Which technique fits best?

This mirrors Lesson 12's "product of primes" theorem exactly: decomposing k+1 can require an earlier case that isn't k, so strong induction's full range of assumptions is what's needed.

5. Fill in the blank: proving "the equation 3x − 7 = 11 has a unique real solution" requires proving both ___ and uniqueness, as two separate arguments.

∃! claims split into an existence half (produce x = 6) and a uniqueness half (any two solutions must be equal) — exactly Lesson 14's two-part structure.
