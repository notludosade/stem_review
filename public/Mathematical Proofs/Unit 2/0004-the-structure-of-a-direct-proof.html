The Structure of a Direct Proof

The simplest, most common proof technique there is: assume the hypothesis, reason forward one legal step at a time, and land on the conclusion. Every other technique in this course is a workaround for when this one doesn't apply cleanly.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Almost every claim in this course has the shape of a conditional, P → Q (Lesson 1). A direct proof attacks that shape head-on: take P as given, and reason forward until Q falls out. It's the first strategy to try on any claim, and Unit 5's decision framework will confirm that instinct — direct proof is the default, not one option among equals.

Definition — Direct Proof A direct proof of P → Q proceeds by: (1) assuming P is true, (2) producing a finite chain of statements S₁, S₂, …, Sₙ = Q, where each Sᵢ follows from P, from definitions, from previously established theorems, or from earlier statements in the chain by valid logical or algebraic steps, and (3) concluding Q. Nothing about the method is exotic — it's simply forward reasoning made explicit and checkable at every link.
Why "each step must be justified" is the entire discipline The gap between a convincing-sounding paragraph and an actual proof is almost always the same gap: whether every step is licensed by something already established, or whether one step is quietly doing more work than it's entitled to. A reader checking a direct proof should be able to point at any single line and ask "why is this true?" and get an answer that traces back only to the hypothesis, a definition, or an already-proven result — never to the conclusion itself, and never to "it looks right."
Worked example Claim: If n is an odd integer, then n² is an odd integer.
Proof. Assume n is odd. By the definition of odd, n = 2k + 1 for some integer k. Then n² = (2k + 1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k² + 2k) + 1. Since k is an integer, 2k² + 2k is also an integer — call it m. So n² = 2m + 1 with m ∈ ℤ, which by the definition of odd means n² is odd. ∎

Notice the shape: assume the hypothesis, unpack the relevant definition (Lesson 3's skill), do algebra, and land on exactly the target definition again. That "land on exactly the definition's form" step is not optional — n² = 4k² + 4k + 1 is true but says nothing about oddness until it's rewritten as 2(something) + 1.

Flawed attempt — assumes the conclusionClaim: If n is odd, then n² is odd.
"Proof": Since n² is odd, n² = 2m + 1 for some integer m.
Then n = sqrt(2m + 1), which is odd since 2m + 1 is odd.
Therefore n odd implies n² odd. ∎
Why this "proof" is worthless despite reaching a true conclusion It opens by writing "n² = 2m + 1" — which is exactly the thing to be proved, smuggled in as a starting assumption instead of a hypothesis. A direct proof of P → Q is only allowed to assume P ("n is odd"); assuming Q ("n² is odd") and reasoning toward P proves the converse, Q → P, not the original claim, and does so invalidly here besides. The conclusion happens to be true, but this argument supplies no actual evidence of it.
Try It — Reveal a Direct Proof One Step at a Time

Claim: If a and b are both even integers, then a + b is an even integer. Click "Reveal next" to build the proof.

  1. Assume the hypothesis. Let a and b be even integers.
  2. Unpack the definitions. By definition of even, a = 2j for some integer j, and b = 2k for some integer k.
  3. Do the algebra. a + b = 2j + 2k = 2(j + k).
  4. Match the target definition's form. Since j and k are integers, j + k is an integer — call it m. So a + b = 2m with m ∈ ℤ.
  5. Conclude. By definition of even, a + b is even. ∎ Every line traces back to the hypothesis or a definition — nothing was assumed that wasn't earned.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A direct proof of P → Q begins by doing what?

Direct proof takes the hypothesis P as given and reasons forward, step by justified step, until it reaches Q.

2. In the flawed attempt above ("n² = 2m + 1 for some integer m" as a starting line), what is the actual error?

A direct proof of P → Q may only assume P. Starting from Q and working toward P (even if the algebra along the way is fine) does not establish P → Q — a true conclusion doesn't rescue an invalid argument.

3. Which of these is a valid justification for a step in a direct proof?

Every step must trace back to the hypothesis, a definition, an axiom, or an already-proven result — nothing else licenses a line in a direct proof.

4. Fill in the blank: assuming the hypothesis and reasoning forward through justified steps to the conclusion is called a ___.

Direct proof — the technique this entire lesson defines, and the one Unit 5 confirms is the correct first strategy to try.

5. Fill in the blank: by definition, an integer n is odd exactly when n = ___ for some integer k.

n = 2k + 1 is the standard definition of odd, used throughout this unit and the next.
