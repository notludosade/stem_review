Working a Design or Whiteboard Problem

A structured approach to an open-ended design prompt, and why narrating your reasoning matters more than the final answer.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

A whiteboard or design problem — "design a mechanism to do X" or "how would you approach Y" — is one of the highest-stakes stages in Lesson 4's process. This lesson covers a repeatable structure for working through one live, in front of an interviewer.

The core mistake: jumping straight to a solution

Why jumping to an answer backfires An interviewer watching you leap to a specific solution learns almost nothing about how you think — and if that first instinct turns out wrong, you now have to backtrack in front of them with no established reasoning to fall back on. The whiteboard problem is testing process, not a lucky first guess, so the structure below deliberately delays committing to a solution.

Step 1: clarify the actual problem

What to ask Real prompts are usually deliberately underspecified. Ask about scale ("how many users / how much load"), constraints (budget, timeline, existing systems it must work with), and the actual goal (what does success look like, specifically). This mirrors Engineering 1's design process (define, then constrain) directly — and asking good clarifying questions is itself part of what's being evaluated.

Step 2: state assumptions out loud

Example "I'll assume we need this to handle roughly 10x current load, since you mentioned rapid growth — let me know if that's off." Stating an assumption explicitly gives the interviewer a chance to correct it immediately, rather than silently designing against a wrong premise for the next twenty minutes.

Step 3: propose a rough approach before refining it

Why sketch first, refine second Presenting a simple first-pass approach, then explicitly identifying its weaknesses and refining it, shows iterative reasoning — the same design-test-iterate loop from Engineering 1's design process. It also gives the interviewer natural points to interject with a follow-up constraint, which is often exactly how they're probing your reasoning depth.

Step 4: narrate trade-offs as you go

Example "I could use approach A, which is simpler but doesn't handle this edge case well, or approach B, which handles it but costs more to build — given what you said about timeline, I'd lean toward A for now." This single sentence demonstrates awareness of alternatives, a clear criterion for choosing, and a connection back to the stated constraints — all in one breath.

Step 5: state what you'd validate next

Real designs aren't finished at the end of a 30-minute conversation. Closing with "the next thing I'd want to test or verify is..." signals that you understand this is a starting point, not a finished, certain answer — an honest and accurate note, and one that leaves a strong final impression.

Why narrating matters more than the "correct" answer

Why this matters Many whiteboard problems don't have one single correct answer at all — they're deliberately open-ended so the interviewer can watch how you reason through ambiguity. A candidate who silently produces a decent design is harder to evaluate than one who talks through a good design, because the interviewer is graded on your process, not just your output.

1. Why does jumping straight to a specific solution in a whiteboard problem tend to backfire?

The problem is testing reasoning process, not a lucky first guess — jumping to an answer skips the part actually being evaluated.

2. Why is stating an assumption out loud ("I'll assume we need to handle roughly 10x current load") a better approach than silently assuming it?

An explicit, spoken assumption can be corrected right away, avoiding twenty minutes of designing against a premise nobody actually agreed to.

3. Why do many whiteboard problems not have a single "correct" answer at all?

Open-endedness is intentional — the interviewer is evaluating the reasoning process on display, not checking the answer against one fixed key.

Naming what's gained and lost by each option, tied back to a stated constraint, packs several signals of good reasoning into one sentence.
