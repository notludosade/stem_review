Breaking Down Work and Tracking Progress

Work breakdown structures, Gantt charts, and why a project plan is a living document, not a one-time forecast.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 10 named the three constraints a project balances. This lesson covers the basic tools for actually planning and tracking the work inside those constraints.

Work breakdown structure (WBS)

Why break work down at all "Build the bridge" is too large a unit to schedule, estimate, or assign to a specific person. A work breakdown structure decomposes a project into progressively smaller pieces — phases, then tasks, then subtasks — until each piece is small enough to estimate with reasonable confidence and assign to one person or small team.
Example "Design the footbridge" breaks into "structural analysis," "material selection," and "drawing set," each of which breaks down further — "structural analysis" into "load calculations," "factor of safety check," and "peer review." Each bottom-level task is small enough that someone can reasonably estimate how long it will take.

Gantt charts: work over time

What a Gantt chart shows A Gantt chart lists each task as a horizontal bar on a timeline, showing when it starts, how long it takes, and which tasks depend on others finishing first. This makes two things visible at a glance that a task list alone doesn't: which tasks can run in parallel, and which single chain of dependent tasks determines the earliest possible finish date — the project's critical path.
Why the critical path matters most Speeding up a task that isn't on the critical path doesn't shorten the project at all — the project still waits on the critical path either way. Knowing which tasks are actually critical focuses limited attention on the delays that would actually move the finish date, rather than the ones that just feel urgent.

Why a project plan is a living document

Why plans need to be updated, not just made A schedule made at the start of a project is a forecast based on the best information available at that time — and real projects almost always reveal new information as they proceed (a task takes longer than expected, a dependency changes, a requirement shifts). Treating the original plan as fixed regardless of new information means the plan silently stops reflecting reality, which is worse than having no plan at all, since it produces false confidence.
Example A task originally estimated at one week turns out to need three. Updating the schedule immediately — and re-checking whether that task is now on the critical path — lets a team react early. Waiting until the original deadline to notice the slip removes the option to react at all.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the main purpose of a work breakdown structure?

A task like "build the bridge" is too large to schedule or estimate directly — breaking it into smaller pieces makes each one tractable.

2. Why does speeding up a task that isn't on the critical path fail to shorten a project's overall timeline?

The project still has to wait on the critical path regardless of how quickly other, non-dependent tasks finish.

3. Why is treating an initial project schedule as permanently fixed, regardless of new information, described as worse than having no plan at all?

A schedule that no longer matches reality but is still trusted as accurate is more dangerous than no schedule, since it hides the actual state of the project.

4. Fill in the blank: the ___ is the single chain of dependent tasks that determines a project's earliest possible finish date.

A Gantt chart makes this chain visible, which is what lets a team focus attention on the delays that would actually move the finish date.

5. Fill in the blank: a ___ chart shows each task as a bar on a timeline, making parallel work and dependency chains visible at a glance.

This chart format is the standard way of visualizing task timing and dependencies across a project.
