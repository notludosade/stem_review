Confidence Intervals & Margin of Error

A sample almost never gives you the exact population value — it gives you an estimate, with some amount of uncertainty attached. This lesson explains how that uncertainty is quantified, and just as importantly, what it can't account for.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 2 introduced sampling: almost no dataset contains an entire population, only a sample meant to represent it. This lesson asks the natural follow-up question — given a number computed from a sample, how far off might it be from the true population value?

Margin of error

Definition The margin of error quantifies the expected range of random variation between a sample statistic (like a sample mean or a survey percentage) and the true population value, due purely to the fact that only a subset, not the whole population, was measured. A poll reporting "62% support, margin of error ±3%" means the true population support is estimated to fall roughly between 59% and 65%.

Confidence intervals

Definition A confidence interval is a range built around a sample statistic, at a stated confidence level (commonly 95%), meant to contain the true population value. A "95% confidence interval" doesn't mean "there's a 95% chance the true value is in this specific range" — more precisely, it means: if you repeated this same sampling process many times and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true population value. Margin of error and confidence interval are closely related: the margin of error is the "plus-or-minus" half-width of the confidence interval.
Why a smaller margin of error doesn't fix a biased sample Recall Lesson 2's sampling bias: a systematic mismatch between the sample and the population, caused by the collection method itself, that does not shrink with more data collected the same way. Margin of error and confidence intervals only quantify random sampling variability — the noise that genuinely does shrink as sample size grows. They say nothing about systematic error from a biased collection method. A poll of 100,000 people recruited entirely through one demographic-skewed app can report a tiny margin of error — because random variability really is small at that sample size — while still being wildly wrong about the general population, because the bias in who was reached never gets smaller no matter how many more of the same kind of respondent you add. A narrow confidence interval is not evidence against sampling bias; it only rules out one specific kind of error (random noise), and a careful reader always asks about the collection method (Lesson 2) before trusting the margin of error alone.
Worked example A random sample of 1,000 voters finds 54% support a policy, with a calculated margin of error of ±3% at 95% confidence. This says: if this exact random sampling process were repeated many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true population support level, which this particular sample estimates to be somewhere between 51% and 57%. It says nothing about whether the sampling frame itself (e.g. only landline phone numbers) systematically excluded some part of the voting population.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a margin of error quantify?

Margin of error is specifically about random sampling variability, not any systematic collection-method bias.

2. Why doesn't a very small margin of error rule out the sampling bias described in Lesson 2?

Random noise (what margin of error measures) and systematic bias (what sampling bias describes) are fundamentally different sources of error, and only one of them shrinks with sample size.

3. What does a "95% confidence interval" actually mean?

The 95% describes the long-run reliability of the method across repeated sampling, not a probability statement about one specific already-computed interval.

4. Fill in the blank: the "plus-or-minus" half-width of a confidence interval, quantifying expected random sampling variation, is called the ___.

Margin of error is precisely the half-width added and subtracted around the sample statistic to build the confidence interval.

5. Fill in the blank: the systematic error from a collection method itself, which a narrow margin of error cannot rule out and more same-method data cannot fix, is ___ (two words, from Lesson 2).

Sampling bias is the systematic error margin of error cannot detect or correct, since it only accounts for random variability.
