Solving Recurrence Relations

The course capstone. A recurrence relation is Lesson 19's recursive definition applied to a whole family of counting problems; solving one for a closed form is the payoff; and proving that closed form correct, for every n, is Lesson 20's induction doing exactly the job it was built for. Every earlier unit shows up here at once.

← Lesson 20 Contents Glossary →

1. Linear recurrence relations

Definition — Linear Homogeneous Recurrence (Constant Coefficients) A sequence satisfies a linear homogeneous recurrence relation with constant coefficients if aₙ = c₁aₙ₋₁ + c₂aₙ₋₂ + ... + cₖaₙ₋ₖ for fixed constants c₁,...,cₖ. This lesson focuses on the order-2 case: aₙ = c₁aₙ₋₁ + c₂aₙ₋₂, which needs two initial conditions (a₀ and a₁) to pin down a unique sequence — the Fibonacci recurrence from Lesson 19 is exactly this shape, with c₁ = c₂ = 1.
Where recurrences like this actually come from They're not just abstract exercises — Unit 3's counting problems generate them directly. The number of ways to tile a 2×n board with 1×2 dominoes, Tₙ, satisfies Tₙ = Tₙ₋₁ + Tₙ₋₂: the last column is either a single vertical domino (leaving a 2×(n−1) board, Tₙ₋₁ ways) or two horizontal dominoes stacked (leaving a 2×(n−2) board, Tₙ₋₂ ways) — the addition principle from Lesson 7, applied to these two mutually-exclusive cases. Tₙ turns out to literally be the Fibonacci sequence.

2. The characteristic equation method

Method — Characteristic Equation For aₙ = c₁aₙ₋₁ + c₂aₙ₋₂, form the characteristic equation x² = c₁x + c₂ (substitute aₙ → xⁿ, aₙ₋₁ → xⁿ⁻¹, aₙ₋₂ → xⁿ⁻², then divide out xⁿ⁻²). If it has two distinct roots r₁, r₂, the general solution is aₙ = A·r₁ⁿ + B·r₂ⁿ, where A and B are constants determined by the two initial conditions.
Worked example Solve aₙ = 5aₙ₋₁ − 6aₙ₋₂, with a₀ = 1, a₁ = 4. Characteristic equation: x² = 5x − 6, i.e. x² − 5x + 6 = 0, which factors as (x−2)(x−3) = 0 — roots r₁=2, r₂=3. General solution: aₙ = A(2ⁿ) + B(3ⁿ). Apply initial conditions: a₀ = A + B = 1; a₁ = 2A + 3B = 4. Solving this system: A = −1, B = 2. Closed form: aₙ = −2ⁿ + 2·3ⁿ. Check: a₀ = −1+2 = 1 ✓. a₁ = −2+6 = 4 ✓. a₂ = −4+18 = 14, and directly from the recurrence, a₂ = 5(4)−6(1) = 14 ✓.

3. Why induction is what makes the closed form trustworthy

The characteristic equation finds a candidate — induction proves it Checking a closed form against the first few terms (as above) is good evidence, but it's not a proof for every n. The actual proof is a strong induction (Lesson 20) on n: base cases confirm the closed form matches a₀ and a₁ directly; the inductive step assumes the closed form is correct for both n−1 and n−2 (this is exactly why it needs to be strong induction, reaching back two steps instead of one) and substitutes those two assumed-correct closed-form values into the recurrence aₙ = c₁aₙ₋₁ + c₂aₙ₋₂ to show the closed form is also correct at n. This is the whole course in miniature: a counting problem (Unit 3) generates a recurrence (Unit 7's own Lesson 19), algebra proposes a closed-form answer, and induction (Lesson 20) is what turns "looks right for the first few terms" into "provably right for every term."

1. What is the characteristic equation of aₙ = 7aₙ₋₁ − 10aₙ₋₂?

Substituting aₙ → xⁿ directly into the recurrence and dividing out xⁿ⁻² gives x² = 7x − 10.

2. x² − 7x + 10 = 0 factors as (x−2)(x−5) = 0. What is the general solution's form?

Distinct roots r₁=2, r₂=5 give general solution aₙ = A·r₁ⁿ + B·r₂ⁿ = A(2ⁿ) + B(5ⁿ), with A and B to be fixed by initial conditions.

3. Why does proving a closed form correct for a 2nd-order recurrence require strong induction, not ordinary induction?

Since aₙ depends on both aₙ₋₁ and aₙ₋₂, the inductive step must assume the closed form holds at both of those points — exactly the "assume it for every smaller case you need" flexibility strong induction provides.

4. Fill in the blank: for aₙ = 5aₙ₋₁ − 6aₙ₋₂ with a₀=1, a₁=4, the closed form aₙ = −2ⁿ + 2·3ⁿ gives a₂ = ___ × 7? — actually compute directly: a₂ = −2² + 2·3² = ___.

a₂ = −4 + 2(9) = −4 + 18 = 14, matching the recurrence directly: 5(4) − 6(1) = 14.

5. Fill in the blank: the number of ways to tile a 2×n board with 1×2 dominoes satisfies the Fibonacci recurrence, arising from the ___ problem discussed in this lesson.
