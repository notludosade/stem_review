Matrix Multiplication

Not entry-by-entry this time — rows meet columns, and the order you multiply in actually matters.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Addition and scalar multiplication were both entrywise, borrowed almost unchanged from vectors. Matrix multiplication breaks that pattern completely — and it's the operation the rest of this course leans on the most: solving systems (Unit 3), transforming the plane (Unit 4), even eigenvalues (Unit 6) are all, underneath, statements about multiplying matrices.

1. The rule

Rule — Multiplying Two Matrices A product AB is defined only when A's number of columns matches B's number of rows (the "inner dimensions" must agree). The result has A's row count and B's column count (the "outer dimensions"). Entry (i, j) of AB is found by pairing row i of A with column j of B, multiplying corresponding entries, and adding — a dot product of that row with that column.
Worked Example A = [[2, 0], [1, 3]], B = [[4, −1], [2, 5]]. Entry (1,1): (2)(4) + (0)(2) = 8. Entry (1,2): (2)(−1) + (0)(5) = −2. Entry (2,1): (1)(4) + (3)(2) = 10. Entry (2,2): (1)(−1) + (3)(5) = 14. So AB = [[8, −2], [10, 14]].
Why AB ≠ BA in general Multiplication isn't symmetric here because row-times-column isn't symmetric: entry (i,j) of AB pairs A's row i with B's column j, while entry (i,j) of BA pairs B's row i with A's column j — completely different numbers, in general. Try both orders below and see it happen.

2. Dimensions of the product

Worked Example — Non-Square Product If A is 2×3 and B is 3×4, the inner dimensions (3 and 3) agree, so AB is defined and comes out 2×4 — A's rows, B's columns. BA, on the other hand, would need B's 4 columns to match A's 2 rows — they don't, so BA isn't even defined here.

Check your recall

1. For a product AB to be defined, A's column count must match:

Each entry of AB pairs a row of A with a column of B; that pairing only makes sense if those two have the same length — A's columns and B's rows.

2. For A = [[2, 0], [1, 3]] and B = [[4, −1], [2, 5]], entry (2,2) of AB is:

Row 2 of A is (1, 3), column 2 of B is (−1, 5): (1)(−1) + (3)(5) = −1 + 15 = 14.

3. Fill in the blank: if A is 2×3 and B is 3×4, AB has dimensions ___.

The inner dimensions (3 and 3) agree, so the product exists, taking A's row count and B's column count: 2×4.

4. Is matrix multiplication commutative — is AB always equal to BA?

Row-times-column pairs different rows and columns depending on the order, so AB and BA generally differ — and can even have different dimensions, or fail to both be defined.

5. Fill in the blank: for A = [[1, 1], [−1, 1]] and B = [[3, −3], [0, 3]], the product AB = ___ (in the form [[row 1], [row 2]]).

Row 1 · col 1: (1)(3)+(1)(0)=3. Row 1 · col 2: (1)(−3)+(1)(3)=0. Row 2 · col 1: (−1)(3)+(1)(0)=−3. Row 2 · col 2: (−1)(−3)+(1)(3)=6. AB = [[3, 0], [−3, 6]].
