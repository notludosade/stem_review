The Second Law of Thermodynamics, Entropy, and Heat Engines

Why no engine is perfectly efficient, why heat never flows uphill on its own, and why a broken egg never unbreaks itself.

← Lesson 5 Unit 2, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

The Second Law of Thermodynamics Heat never flows spontaneously from a colder object to a hotter one. Equivalently: the total entropy (a measure of disorder) of an isolated system never decreases over time — it stays the same for an idealized reversible process, or increases for any real process.
Why entropy gives the universe a direction in time Every other law covered so far (Newton's laws, energy conservation, momentum conservation) works exactly the same whether time runs forward or backward — a video of a frictionless collision looks equally plausible played in reverse. The second law is different: entropy increasing over time is what makes a broken egg reassembling itself, or heat flowing from cold to hot on its own, look obviously wrong when played in reverse. This is why the second law is often called the arrow of time.
Heat Engines and Efficiency A heat engine converts heat into work by letting heat flow from a hot reservoir to a cold one, extracting some of it as useful work along the way. Efficiency e = W/QH, where W is work output and QH is heat absorbed from the hot reservoir.
Why no heat engine can be 100% efficient Converting 100% of absorbed heat into work (e = 1) would require dumping zero heat into a cold reservoir — but the second law forbids this, since it would mean entropy decreasing overall (all the disorder of the hot reservoir's heat converted perfectly into ordered mechanical work, with nothing left over). Some heat must always be exhausted to a colder reservoir, which is exactly why every real engine — car engines, power plants, jet turbines — wastes some fraction of its fuel's energy as waste heat, no matter how well-engineered it is.
Example: computing engine efficiency An engine absorbs 5000 J of heat from a hot reservoir and performs 1500 J of useful work. e = W/QH = 1500/5000 = 0.30, or 30% — the remaining 3500 J is exhausted as waste heat to the cold reservoir, not converted to work.
Example: heat exhausted to the cold reservoir Using the first law's energy accounting, the heat exhausted QC = QH − W = 5000 − 1500 = 3500 J — energy is fully accounted for (heat in equals work out plus heat rejected), even though not all of the input heat became useful work.

Entropy stays the same only for an idealized reversible process and increases for any real process, but never decreases in an isolated system.

2. Why can no real heat engine achieve 100% efficiency?

Some heat must always be exhausted to a colder reservoir to satisfy the second law, capping efficiency below 100% for any real engine.

3. An engine absorbs 8000 J of heat and exhausts 6000 J to its cold reservoir. What is its efficiency?

W = Q_H − Q_C = 8000 − 6000 = 2000 J, so e = W/Q_H = 2000/8000 = 0.25, or 25%.
