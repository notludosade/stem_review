Temperature, Thermal Equilibrium, and the Zeroth Law

Before heat can be measured, "same temperature" has to be defined precisely — and that definition turns out to need its own law.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Thermal Equilibrium and the Zeroth Law Two objects are in thermal equilibrium when no net heat flows between them — they are at the same temperature. The zeroth law of thermodynamics states: if object A is in thermal equilibrium with object C, and object B is also in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
Why this "obvious" law needs stating at all The zeroth law is what makes a thermometer meaningful. A thermometer (object C) is placed in contact with a patient (object A) until it reaches equilibrium with them, giving a reading. That same thermometer, after being placed in contact with a glass of water (object B) and reaching equilibrium with it, lets you compare the patient's temperature to the water's — even though the patient and the water were never in direct contact. Without the zeroth law, there would be no logical guarantee that this transitive comparison means anything.
Temperature Scales The Celsius scale sets water's freezing point at 0°C and boiling point at 100°C. The Kelvin scale shifts this so that 0 K is absolute zero — the theoretical temperature at which molecular motion is minimized — with T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15. Kelvin is the scale required in every gas-law and thermodynamics equation in this unit.
Why Kelvin, specifically, is required in the equations ahead Every equation in this unit (starting with the ideal gas law in Lesson 2) involves temperature directly proportional to some physical quantity like pressure or volume. That proportionality only holds if zero on the temperature scale means zero of that quantity — which is true for Kelvin (absolute zero) but not for Celsius or Fahrenheit, whose zero points are arbitrary reference points (water's freezing point, or a historical brine mixture) with no special physical meaning.
Example: converting to Kelvin Room temperature, 22°C, converts to T = 22 + 273.15 = 295.15 K. A very cold winter day, −15°C, converts to T = −15 + 273.15 = 258.15 K — still a large positive number in Kelvin, since 0 K is far colder than any everyday temperature.

1. The zeroth law of thermodynamics states that if A is in thermal equilibrium with C, and B is also in thermal equilibrium with C, then:

The zeroth law is precisely this transitive property, which is what justifies using a single thermometer to compare two objects that never touch each other.

2. Why must temperature be measured in Kelvin, not Celsius, in the gas-law equations covered in this unit?

Celsius and Fahrenheit have arbitrary zero points, so a direct proportionality (like pressure to temperature) would give nonsensical results if used with those scales.

3. A liquid has a temperature of 350 K. What is this temperature in Celsius?

T(°C) = T(K) − 273.15 = 350 − 273.15 = 76.85°C.
