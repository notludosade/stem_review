Lenz's Law

Induced currents always fight back against the change that created them — nature's way of enforcing energy conservation in electromagnetism.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Lenz's Law An induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change in flux that created it. This is the physical meaning behind the minus sign in Faraday's law (ε = −N ΔΦB/Δt) — it fixes the induced current's direction, not just its magnitude.
Why the induced current must oppose the change (not just react to it) If an induced current instead reinforced the change in flux that created it, the resulting field would strengthen the original change, which would induce an even larger current, which would strengthen the change further — a runaway loop that would generate unlimited energy from nothing, violating energy conservation. Lenz's law is nature enforcing energy conservation: opposing the change means work must be done (by whatever is causing the flux to change) to induce the current, and that work is exactly where the induced current's electrical energy comes from.
Applying Lenz's Law To find an induced current's direction: (1) determine whether flux through the loop is increasing or decreasing, (2) the induced current creates its own field that opposes this change — opposing an increase means the induced field points opposite the original field; opposing a decrease means it points the same direction, and (3) use the right-hand rule (Lesson 3) on this required induced-field direction to find the current's direction around the loop.
Example: a magnet approaching a loop A bar magnet's north pole approaches a loop, increasing the flux through it (into the page, say). By Lenz's law, the induced current opposes this increase, creating its own field pointing out of the page inside the loop — by the right-hand rule, this means the induced current flows counterclockwise as viewed from the approaching magnet's side.
Example: a magnet receding from a loop The same magnet now moves away, decreasing the flux (still into the page, but shrinking). By Lenz's law, the induced current opposes this decrease by trying to maintain the flux, creating its own field also pointing into the page — this requires the current to flow clockwise, the opposite direction from the approaching case.

1. Lenz's law states that an induced current always flows in a direction that:

The induced current's field always opposes the flux change that induced it, which is the physical content of Faraday's law's minus sign.

2. Why must the induced current oppose (rather than reinforce) the change in flux that created it?

An opposing induced current means work must be done to change the flux, which is exactly where the induced electrical energy comes from — reinforcement would violate energy conservation.

3. Flux through a loop is steadily decreasing. According to Lenz's law, the induced current will create a field that:

To oppose a decrease in flux, the induced current creates a field in the same direction as the original, trying to maintain the flux.

4. Fill in the blank: the rule stating that induced current opposes the change in flux that produced it is called ___.

Lenz's law is a direct consequence of energy conservation applied to electromagnetic induction.
