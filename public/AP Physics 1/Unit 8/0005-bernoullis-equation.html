Bernoulli's Equation

Energy conservation applied to flowing fluid — explaining why fast-moving air exerts less pressure, and how wings generate lift.

← Lesson 4 Contents Glossary →

Bernoulli's Equation For an ideal (incompressible, non-viscous) fluid in steady flow: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along any streamline. This is energy conservation (Unit 3, Lesson 4) applied per unit volume of fluid: pressure energy, kinetic energy, and gravitational potential energy trade off against each other, but their sum stays fixed.
Why this is really just energy conservation Each term has units of energy per volume (pressure is already J/m³, ½ρv² is kinetic energy per volume, and ρgh is gravitational PE per volume), and the equation states their sum is conserved along a streamline — precisely mirroring ME = KE + PE = constant from Unit 3, Lesson 4, just reframed on a per-volume basis for a fluid instead of a single object. Where a fluid speeds up (from continuity, Lesson 4), something else in the equation must decrease to compensate — usually pressure, if height stays roughly constant.
Example: pressure drop in a horizontal nozzle Water flows horizontally (h constant) through a hose at 2 m/s under pressure 150,000 Pa, then speeds up to 10 m/s through a nozzle (as in Lesson 4's example). With h unchanged, Bernoulli's equation gives P1 + ½ρv1² = P2 + ½ρv2²: 150,000 + ½(1000)(4) = P2 + ½(1000)(100) ⟹ 152,000 = P2 + 50,000 ⟹ P2 = 102,000 Pa — pressure drops as speed increases, exactly as the equation predicts.
Why airplane wings generate lift A wing's curved upper surface forces air traveling over the top to move faster than air traveling beneath it (a consequence of the wing's shape and the surrounding airflow pattern). By Bernoulli's equation with height roughly equal on both sides, faster-moving air above the wing corresponds to lower pressure there, while the slower air beneath maintains higher pressure — the resulting pressure difference between bottom and top produces a net upward force: lift. (The full aerodynamic picture involves more than Bernoulli's equation alone, but this pressure-speed relationship is a central piece of the explanation.)
Example: Torricelli's law — draining a tank A large open tank has a small hole at depth h below the surface. At the top surface, pressure is atmospheric and speed is negligible (large surface area draining slowly); at the hole, pressure is also atmospheric (open to air) but speed v is what we want. Bernoulli's equation simplifies to ρgh = ½ρv², giving v = √(2gh) — remarkably, the exact same speed a dropped object would reach after falling the same height h (Unit 1), even though this is a completely different physical situation.

Unit 8 and course summary

This unit introduced fluids as a new domain distinct from rigid-body mechanics: density and pressure as the basic descriptive quantities, including how pressure grows with depth (Lesson 1); Pascal's principle and hydraulic systems (Lesson 2); buoyancy and Archimedes' principle explaining floating and sinking (Lesson 3); the continuity equation governing fluid speed in pipes of varying width (Lesson 4); and Bernoulli's equation, extending energy conservation to moving fluids and explaining phenomena from nozzles to airplane lift (Lesson 5). 1. Bernoulli's equation, P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant, is fundamentally an expression of:

Each term in Bernoulli's equation has units of energy per volume, and the equation states their sum is conserved — a direct fluid analog of ME = KE + PE = constant.

2. According to Bernoulli's equation, where a horizontal-flowing fluid speeds up, its pressure:

With height constant, P + ½ρv² = constant means an increase in v must be balanced by a decrease in P.

3. Why does the speed of water draining from a small hole at depth h below a tank's surface equal √(2gh), the same as a freely falling object dropped from height h?

Bernoulli's equation at the hole reduces to ρgh = ½ρv² (both sides representing energy per volume), the exact same energy-conservation statement as mgh = ½mv² for a falling object, giving the identical result v = √(2gh).

4. Fill in the blank: the upward force on an airplane wing, arising partly from a pressure difference created by unequal air speeds above and below it, is called ___.

Lift arises from a pressure difference across the wing, connected to Bernoulli's equation via the difference in air speed above and below.
