The First Law of Thermodynamics

Lesson 2 kept heat, work, and internal energy carefully separate. Now they get tied together into one equation — conservation of energy, written for a system that can both absorb heat and do mechanical work.

← Unit 1, Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — The First Law, ΔU = Q − W ΔU = Q − W: a system's change in internal energy equals the heat added to it minus the work it does on its surroundings. Sign convention used throughout this course: Q is positive when heat flows into the system, negative when it flows out; W is positive when the system does work on its surroundings (e.g. a gas expanding), negative when the surroundings do work on the system (e.g. a gas being compressed). This is simply conservation of energy, restricted to systems where heat and mechanical work are the two channels energy can cross the boundary through.
Why the Minus Sign in Front of W Both Q and W are defined as positive when energy would naively seem to help internal energy grow — but they enter the equation with opposite signs because they're opposite directions relative to the system's energy content. Heat flowing in directly adds to U. Work done by the system, by contrast, is energy leaving to push against something outside — it subtracts from U even though W itself is recorded as positive. Get the sign convention backwards and every calculation from here through Unit 5 comes out wrong, even with perfectly correct arithmetic.
Worked Example — Applying the First Law A gas absorbs Q = 500 J of heat while expanding and doing W = 200 J of work on its surroundings. Find ΔU.
ΔU = Q − W = 500 − 200 = 300 J. The gas's internal energy rose by 300 J — less than the heat it took in, because some of that energy left again as work done pushing outward.

1. By this course's sign convention, when a gas is compressed by its surroundings (work is done on the gas rather than by it), the work variable W in ΔU = Q − W is:

W is positive only when the system does work on its surroundings; when the surroundings instead do work on the system (compression), W is negative — which correctly makes −W a positive contribution to ΔU.

2. Fill in the blank: a gas absorbs Q = 350 J of heat while doing W = 150 J of work on its surroundings. ΔU = ___ J.

ΔU = Q − W = 350 − 150 = 200 J.

3. A gas undergoes a sequence of processes that eventually brings it back to its exact original state (a complete cycle). Over the whole cycle, ΔU equals:

U is a state function, so returning to the identical starting state forces ΔU = 0 for the full cycle — meaning net Q = net W, a fact Unit 3 will use directly when analyzing engine cycles.

4. A gas is heated inside a perfectly rigid, sealed container, so its volume cannot change. Which of the following holds?

With no volume change possible, no boundary-moving work can occur, so W = 0 and every joule of heat added shows up directly as internal energy: ΔU = Q. (This is exactly the isochoric process named in Lesson 6.)
