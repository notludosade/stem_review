Specific and Latent Heat, and Calorimetry

Two formulas cover almost every "how much heat does this take" question you'll ever face — one for changing temperature within a phase, one for changing phase at constant temperature.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Specific Heat and Latent Heat Q = mcΔT — the heat needed to change a mass m by a temperature ΔT within a single phase, where c is the material's specific heat capacity (J/(kg·K)), an intrinsic property (water: c ≈ 4186 J/(kg·K)). Q = mL — the heat needed to change a mass m's phase at constant temperature, where L is the latent heat (J/kg) — L_f for fusion (melting/freezing, water: L_f ≈ 3.34×10⁵ J/kg) or L_v for vaporization (boiling/condensing, water: L_v ≈ 2.26×10⁶ J/kg).
Method — Calorimetry: Conservation of Energy in a Mixture When substances at different temperatures combine inside an isolated system (no heat escapes to the environment), the first law demands the heat lost by the hotter one equals the heat gained by the colder one: Q_lost = Q_gained, or equivalently ΣQ = 0 summed over every substance involved. Set up one Q = mcΔT (or Q = mL, if something changes phase) per substance, then solve for whatever's unknown — usually the shared final equilibrium temperature.
Why Latent Heat Is Invisible on a Thermometer During a phase change, every joule of added heat goes into breaking (melting, boiling) or forming (freezing, condensing) intermolecular bonds — none of it raises the average kinetic energy that a thermometer actually measures. That's why a pot of boiling water sits pinned at 100°C no matter how hard the burner is turned up, right up until every last drop has vaporized: the temperature is stuck at the phase-change plateau, even as heat keeps flowing in.
Worked Example — Mixing Hot and Cold Water 0.3 kg of water at 90°C is mixed with 0.5 kg of water at 15°C in an insulated container. Find the equilibrium temperature T_f. (Same substance, so c cancels out of the equation.)
Q_lost by hot water = Q_gained by cold water: m₁(T₁ − T_f) = m₂(T_f − T₂)
0.3(90 − T_f) = 0.5(T_f − 15) → 27 − 0.3T_f = 0.5T_f − 7.5 → 34.5 = 0.8T_f → T_f ≈ 43.1°C

1. While a substance is actively changing phase (e.g. ice melting) at constant pressure, its temperature:

During a phase change, added heat goes entirely into rearranging intermolecular bonds rather than raising kinetic energy, so temperature holds flat at the phase-change point — this is exactly why Q = mL has no ΔT in it at all.

2. Fill in the blank: melting 0.8 kg of ice (L_f ≈ 3.34×10⁵ J/kg) requires Q = ___ kJ (1 decimal place).

Q = mL = (0.8)(3.34×10⁵) = 267,200 J = 267.2 kJ.

This is the first law applied to a closed, isolated mixture: no energy leaves the system as a whole, so whatever heat the hot substance loses must exactly match what the cold substance gains.

4. Fill in the blank: 0.2 kg of water at 95°C is mixed with 0.4 kg of water at 20°C in an insulated container. The equilibrium temperature is T_f = ___ °C.

0.2(95 − T_f) = 0.4(T_f − 20) → 19 − 0.2T_f = 0.4T_f − 8 → 27 = 0.6T_f → T_f = 45°C.
