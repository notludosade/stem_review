The Second Law and Reversibility

The first law says energy is conserved. It says nothing about which direction a process is allowed to run — and it turns out nature is far pickier about that than conservation alone would suggest.

← Unit 3, Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Two Equivalent Statements of the Second Law Kelvin statement: no cyclic engine can convert heat extracted from a single reservoir entirely into work, with no other effect — some heat must always be exhausted somewhere colder. Clausius statement: heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter one, with no other effect — moving heat "uphill" always requires work input from somewhere. These sound like they're about different things (engines vs. refrigerators), but they are logically equivalent: violating either one lets you build a device that violates the other.
Definition — Reversible vs. Irreversible Processes A reversible process is an idealized limit — quasi-static (infinitely slow, passing through a continuous sequence of equilibrium states) and free of dissipation (no friction, no finite-temperature-difference heat flow) — that can be run exactly backward, retracing every intermediate state, leaving no trace anywhere. An irreversible process (friction, free expansion, heat flowing across a finite ΔT, anything real) cannot be undone without some permanent change somewhere else in the universe. No real process is perfectly reversible; it's a limit real processes can only approach.
Why 100% Efficiency Is Impossible, Not Just Unbuilt The Kelvin statement directly forbids a 100%-efficient heat engine — not as an engineering shortfall waiting on better materials, but as a law on the same logical footing as conservation of energy. Every heat engine ever built, and every one that ever will be built, must exhaust some heat to a cold reservoir. Unit 5 turns this qualitative fact into an exact number: the Carnot efficiency ceiling.
Worked Example — Why Violating One Statement Breaks the Other Suppose a "Clausius violator" existed: a device that moves heat Qc from a cold reservoir to a hot reservoir with no work input. Pair it with an ordinary heat engine running between the same two reservoirs, which extracts Qh from the hot side, dumps Qc into the cold side, and delivers work W = Qh − Qc. Run both together: the violator quietly returns exactly the Qc the engine dumped, back to the hot reservoir, for free. Net effect on the cold reservoir: nothing. Net effect on the hot reservoir: it lost Qh and regained Qc, a net loss of Qh − Qc = W. The combination has extracted heat from a single reservoir (the hot one) and converted all of it into work — a Kelvin violator. One impossible device builds the other.

1. The Clausius statement of the second law says:

The Clausius statement is specifically about the "uphill" direction — moving heat from cold to hot always requires work input from somewhere else.

2. The Kelvin statement of the second law says:

Kelvin's statement directly rules out a 100%-efficient cyclic engine — some heat must always be exhausted to a colder reservoir.

3. A truly reversible process is one that:

Reversibility requires quasi-static, dissipation-free motion through a continuous sequence of equilibrium states — an idealized limit, not something everyday processes achieve.

4. Fill in the blank: a block sliding to a stop due to friction, or a gas freely expanding into a vacuum, are both examples of ___ processes.

Friction and free expansion both dissipate energy or spread it out irretrievably — neither can be undone without a permanent change occurring somewhere else, the hallmark of an irreversible process.
