Course Capstone — Heat Engines, Entropy, and Why No Engine Is Perfect

Eighteen lessons ago, this course promised to explain why no engine is ever 100% efficient, with the rigor of a college engineering-science course. This lesson delivers on that promise directly, in one worked problem that uses nearly everything the course has built.

Recap — The Three Layers of the Efficiency Ceiling Layer 1 (Lesson 13): e = 1 − Q_c/Q_h can never equal 1, since Q_c = 0 is exactly what the Kelvin statement forbids. Layer 2 (Lesson 14): even in the best possible case, efficiency is capped at e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h — set purely by the two operating temperatures, unreachable by any working substance or design. Layer 3 (Lessons 10–12): real engines fall short of even that ceiling, because real processes are irreversible, generating entropy (ΔS_universe > 0) that a perfectly reversible Carnot cycle (ΔS_universe = 0) would not.
Method — Auditing a Real Engine Against Both Ceilings Given an engine's actual T_h, T_c, Q_h, and measured efficiency e: (1) compute e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h, the theoretical ceiling; (2) compute the actual W = eQ_h and Q_c = Q_h − W; (3) compute the total entropy change of both reservoirs, ΔS_total = −Q_h/T_h + Q_c/T_c. If ΔS_total > 0, the engine is confirmed irreversible — consistent with e < e_Carnot. Only in the idealized limit ΔS_total = 0 could e reach e_Carnot exactly, and even then, e_Carnot itself is still strictly less than 1.
Why No Real Engine Is Ever 100% Efficient Three independent layers stack against it, and any one alone is already fatal to the idea. First, the Kelvin statement of the second law directly forbids converting a single reservoir's heat entirely into work — some heat must always be exhausted, so e = 1 is not just difficult, it's provably impossible. Second, even discounting that and asking for the best possible efficiency between two given temperatures, Carnot's theorem caps it at e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h — strictly less than 1 for any finite T_h and nonzero T_c, and both an infinite T_h and an absolute-zero T_c are themselves physically unreachable. Third, every real engine carries friction, finite-temperature-difference heat transfer, and other irreversibilities that generate entropy and push its actual efficiency below even the Carnot ceiling. No engineering advance removes any of these three — the first two are consequences of the second law itself, and the third can only ever be reduced, never eliminated, since perfect reversibility is an idealized limit no real process reaches.
Worked Example — Full Audit of a Power Plant Cycle A power plant operates between T_h = 650 K and T_c = 310 K, absorbing Q_h = 5000 J per cycle at a measured efficiency e = 0.35 (35%). Audit it against both ceilings.
Carnot ceiling: e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h = 1 − 310/650 ≈ 0.523 (52.3%) — the plant's actual 35% falls well short.
Actual work and exhaust: W = eQ_h = (0.35)(5000) = 1750 J; Q_c = Q_h − W = 5000 − 1750 = 3250 J.
Entropy audit: ΔS_hot = −Q_h/T_h = −5000/650 ≈ −7.69 J/K (hot reservoir loses entropy by losing heat); ΔS_cold = Q_c/T_c = 3250/310 ≈ 10.48 J/K (cold reservoir gains entropy by gaining heat). ΔS_total ≈ −7.69 + 10.48 ≈ +2.79 J/K — strictly positive, confirming real irreversibility, and consistent with the plant landing below its 52.3% Carnot ceiling. A perfectly reversible engine between these same two temperatures would show ΔS_total = 0 and reach exactly 52.3% — still, notably, nowhere near 100%.

1. An engine operates between T_h = 500 K and T_c = 250 K, absorbing Q_h = 4000 J at efficiency e = 0.4. What is e_Carnot for this pair of temperatures?

e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h = 1 − 250/500 = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5 — above this engine's actual 0.4, as it must be.

2. Fill in the blank: for the same engine (T_h = 500 K, T_c = 250 K, Q_h = 4000 J, e = 0.4), Q_c = Q_h − eQ_h = 2400 J. The total entropy change of both reservoirs, ΔS_total = −Q_h/T_h + Q_c/T_c, is ___ J/K.

ΔS_total = −4000/500 + 2400/250 = −8 + 9.6 = 1.6 J/K — positive, confirming this engine (like any real one) is irreversible.

3. Which single statement most completely explains why no real engine is ever 100% efficient?

All three layers matter: the flat impossibility of e = 1 (Kelvin), the Carnot ceiling below 1 even ideally, and real irreversibility pushing actual engines below that ceiling — none of them are engineering shortfalls waiting on better materials.

4. If an engine's measured ΔS_total (summed over both reservoirs, per cycle) came out to be exactly 0, what would that imply?

ΔS_total = 0 is exactly the reversible limit — achievable only in the idealization, and even there capped at e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h, which is still strictly below 1.
