1. In the van der Waals equation (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT, setting a = b = 0 gives:

With both correction constants zeroed out, (P)(V) = nRT is exactly the ideal gas law — the van der Waals equation is a strict generalization of it.

2. Which curve on a phase diagram represents solid-to-gas equilibrium directly, without passing through the liquid phase?

The sublimation curve is specifically the solid-gas boundary; fusion is solid-liquid, and vaporization is liquid-gas.

3. According to this course's capstone argument, what stops any real engine from reaching 100% efficiency?

All three layers are independent barriers, none removable by engineering improvement — the first two are direct consequences of the second law, and the third is a fundamental feature of any real, irreversible process.

4. Which correction term, if it were omitted entirely, would make an equation of state unable to ever predict a gas condensing into a liquid?

Condensation is driven by intermolecular attraction pulling molecules together — an equation with no attraction term (like the plain ideal gas law) structurally cannot capture it.

5. On a heating curve (T vs. heat added), the slope of a ramp segment (not a plateau) is determined mainly by:

Since Q = mcΔT, a smaller c means less heat is needed for the same ΔT, so the same rate of heat input produces a faster-rising, steeper temperature ramp.

6. A gas has van der Waals constant a = 0.5 Pa·m⁶/mol². For n = 1 mol confined to V = 2×10⁻⁴ m³, answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the attraction correction term an²/V² is ___ MPa.

(b) Physically, this term represents:

(a) an²/V² = (0.5)(1)²/(2×10⁻⁴)² = 0.5/(4×10⁻⁸) = 1.25×10⁷ Pa = 12.5 MPa. (b) This term compensates for attractive forces softening molecular impacts on the walls — the "nb" term is the one for molecular volume instead.

7. 0.15 kg of water at 100°C is fully boiled into steam (L_v ≈ 2.26×10⁶ J/kg). Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the heat required is ___ kJ.

(b) During this boiling process, the water's temperature:

(a) Q = mL_v = (0.15)(2.26×10⁶) = 339,000 J = 339 kJ. (b) This is exactly the flat plateau on a heating curve — a phase transition proceeds at constant T.

8. An engine operates between T_h = 720 K and T_c = 432 K. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: e_Carnot = ___ (as a decimal, e.g. 0.4).

(b) If this engine's measured actual efficiency is 0.32, is that consistent with the second law?

(a) e_Carnot = 1 − 432/720 = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4. (b) Real engines are irreversible and so must fall at or below e_Carnot — landing below it is expected, not a violation.

9. An engine operates between T_h = 600 K and T_c = 300 K, absorbing Q_h = 4500 J at actual efficiency e = 0.3. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: with W = eQ_h = 1350 J and Q_c = Q_h − W = 3150 J, the total entropy change ΔS_total = −Q_h/T_h + Q_c/T_c = ___ J/K.

(b) This positive ΔS_total confirms:

(a) ΔS_total = −4500/600 + 3150/300 = −7.5 + 10.5 = 3.0 J/K. (b) A strictly positive ΔS_total is the signature of irreversibility — exactly what's expected of any real engine falling short of its Carnot ceiling.

10. An engine absorbs Q_h = 6000 J per cycle at actual efficiency e = 0.28. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the heat exhausted is Q_c = Q_h − eQ_h = ___ J.

(b) The general method of this lesson — computing e_Carnot, the actual W and Q_c, and the total entropy change — generalizes to any real engine because:

(a) Q_c = Q_h − eQ_h = 6000 − (0.28)(6000) = 6000 − 1680 = 4320 J. (b) The audit uses only universal thermodynamic laws — no detail about the engine's internal mechanism is ever needed.
