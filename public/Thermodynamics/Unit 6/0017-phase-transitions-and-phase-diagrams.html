Phase Transitions and Phase Diagrams

Lesson 5's Q = mL was a single formula for "changing phase." This lesson maps out every phase a substance can be in, and revisits that same latent heat as one leg of a much longer journey.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Phase Diagrams, Triple Points, and Critical Points A phase diagram plots pressure against temperature, divided into solid, liquid, and gas regions by boundary curves — the fusion curve (solid–liquid), vaporization curve (liquid–gas), and sublimation curve (solid–gas). At the triple point, all three curves meet, and all three phases coexist in equilibrium simultaneously at one exact P and T. Following the vaporization curve up to the critical point, the distinction between liquid and gas simply vanishes — beyond it, the substance is a supercritical fluid, with no phase boundary left to cross at all.
Method — The Heating Curve Plot temperature against total heat added, at constant pressure, starting from a solid. Within a single phase, T rises along a sloped ramp following Q = mcΔT (steeper for a smaller c). At each phase transition, the curve goes exactly flat — a plateau at constant T — while Q = mL is absorbed with zero temperature change, precisely Lesson 5's point about latent heat being invisible on a thermometer. A full heating curve from solid to gas has five segments: heat the solid, melt it (plateau), heat the liquid, boil it (plateau), heat the gas.
Why Water's Phase Diagram Is Unusual Most substances' fusion curve tilts slightly right (higher pressure raises the melting point) — water's tilts left instead, meaning higher pressure actually lowers its melting point. This is a direct consequence of water expanding, rather than contracting, when it freezes (ice is less dense than liquid water) — a quirk responsible for ice floating, lakes freezing top-down instead of bottom-up, and ice skates gliding (though the dominant mechanism there is friction-generated heat, not pressure melting, a common oversimplification worth flagging).
Worked Example — A Complete Heating Curve Find the total heat needed to take 0.2 kg of ice at −10°C all the way to steam at 100°C (c_ice ≈ 2100 J/(kg·K), c_water ≈ 4186 J/(kg·K), L_f ≈ 3.34×10⁵ J/kg, L_v ≈ 2.26×10⁶ J/kg).
Warm the ice: Q₁ = mc_iceΔT = (0.2)(2100)(10) = 4200 J.
Melt it (plateau at 0°C): Q₂ = mL_f = (0.2)(3.34×10⁵) = 66,800 J.
Warm the water: Q₃ = mc_waterΔT = (0.2)(4186)(100) = 83,720 J.
Boil it (plateau at 100°C): Q₄ = mL_v = (0.2)(2.26×10⁶) = 452,000 J.
Total: Q₁+Q₂+Q₃+Q₄ = 4200 + 66,800 + 83,720 + 452,000 = 606,720 J ≈ 606.7 kJ — and notice boiling alone (Q₄) costs more than every other step combined.

1. At a substance's triple point:

The triple point is the single (P, T) pair where the fusion, vaporization, and sublimation curves all meet, letting all three phases sit in equilibrium simultaneously.

2. Fill in the blank: warming 0.3 kg of ice (c_ice ≈ 2100 J/(kg·K)) by 15°C requires Q = ___ J.

Q = mc_iceΔT = (0.3)(2100)(15) = 9450 J.

3. Beyond a substance's critical point:

Past the critical point, there's no longer a meaningful boundary between "liquid" and "gas" — the substance is a single supercritical phase.

4. On a heating curve (T vs. heat added at constant pressure), what does a completely flat, horizontal segment represent?

Flat segments are exactly the phase-transition plateaus from Lesson 5 — all the added heat goes into rearranging bonds (Q = mL), none of it into raising temperature.
