Real Gases and the Van der Waals Equation

PV = nRT has been treated as exact since Lesson 6. It isn't — it's a limit two specific assumptions have to hold for, and real gases quietly stop obeying it the moment those assumptions strain.

← Unit 5, Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Where the Ideal Gas Assumptions Break Down The ideal gas law assumes gas particles have negligible volume compared to their container, and experience no intermolecular forces. Both assumptions are excellent at ordinary pressure and temperature, but strain at high pressure (particles are squeezed close enough that their own volume stops being negligible) and low temperature (particles move slowly enough that weak attractive forces between them start to matter, rather than being drowned out by kinetic energy). Pushed far enough, real gases condense into liquids — something PV = nRT, with no attraction term at all, can never predict.
Definition — The Van der Waals Equation (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT patches both assumptions with one constant each, both measured per gas: b is subtracted from V to represent the volume the molecules themselves actually occupy, shrinking the space left for them to move in; an²/V² is added to P to represent the extra pressure that would exist if attractive forces weren't quietly pulling molecules back from the walls, slightly softening their impacts. Set a = b = 0 and the van der Waals equation collapses exactly back to PV = nRT.
Why This Isn't Just a Textbook Correction Engineers who design high-pressure gas storage, refrigeration systems, or cryogenic equipment cannot use PV = nRT and expect safe numbers — the ideal gas law can be wrong by a large factor exactly in the regime (high pressure, low temperature) those systems operate in. Van der Waals-type corrections (there are several more refined equations of state beyond this one) are what keep those pressure predictions honest.
Worked Example — Ideal vs. Van der Waals Prediction 1 mol of CO₂ (a ≈ 0.364 Pa·m⁶/mol², b ≈ 4.27×10⁻⁵ m³/mol) is compressed to V = 1×10⁻⁴ m³ at T = 300 K — dense enough to strain both assumptions. Compare the ideal and van der Waals pressure predictions.
P_ideal = nRT/V = (1)(8.314)(300)/(1×10⁻⁴) ≈ 24.9 MPa.
P_vdW = nRT/(V − nb) − an²/V² = 2494.2/(5.73×10⁻⁵) − (0.364)/(1×10⁻⁴)² ≈ 43.5 MPa − 36.4 MPa ≈ 7.1 MPa — less than a third of the ideal prediction, dominated here by the attractive-force correction.

1. In the van der Waals equation, the term an²/V² added to P corrects for:

Attractive forces pull molecules back slightly as they approach the wall, softening their impacts — the an²/V² term adds back the pressure that "should" be there if attraction weren't present.

2. In the van der Waals equation, the term nb subtracted from V corrects for:

Real molecules aren't points — nb represents the volume they physically take up, which the ideal gas law assumes away entirely.

3. Real gases deviate most strongly from ideal gas behavior under which conditions?

High pressure makes molecular volume non-negligible, and low temperature lets weak attractive forces matter (rather than being overwhelmed by kinetic energy) — both are exactly where the ideal gas law's assumptions strain hardest.

4. Fill in the blank: the ideal gas law can never predict a gas condensing into a liquid at any temperature or pressure, because it completely omits intermolecular ___ — the very thing that pulls molecules together into a liquid in the first place.

Condensation is fundamentally an attractive-force phenomenon — molecules clumping together — which an equation with zero attraction term structurally cannot capture.
