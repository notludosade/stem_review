1. In the van der Waals equation (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT, which limit recovers the ideal gas law exactly?

With both correction constants zeroed out, (P)(V) = nRT is exactly the ideal gas law — the van der Waals equation is a strict generalization of it.

2. Which curve on a phase diagram represents liquid-to-gas equilibrium?

The vaporization curve is specifically the liquid-gas boundary; fusion is solid-liquid, and sublimation is solid-gas.

3. According to this course's capstone argument, what stops any real engine from reaching 100% efficiency?

All three layers are independent barriers, none removable by engineering improvement — the first two are direct consequences of the second law, and the third is a fundamental feature of any real, irreversible process.

4. Which correction term, if it were omitted entirely, would leave molecular size unaccounted for — treating molecules as true points even at high density?

The nb term is specifically the correction for the finite volume real molecules occupy — without it, molecules are implicitly treated as dimensionless points, exactly the ideal gas assumption.

5. On a heating curve (T vs. heat added), a completely flat plateau segment specifically indicates:

Flat plateaus are phase-transition segments — every joule goes into latent heat (Q = mL), none into raising temperature.

6. A gas has van der Waals constant a = 0.6 Pa·m⁶/mol². For n = 1 mol confined to V = 2.5×10⁻⁴ m³, answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the attraction correction term an²/V² is ___ MPa.

(b) Physically, this term represents:

(a) an²/V² = (0.6)(1)²/(2.5×10⁻⁴)² = 0.6/(6.25×10⁻⁸) = 9.6×10⁶ Pa = 9.6 MPa. (b) This term compensates for attractive forces softening molecular impacts on the walls — the "nb" term is the one for molecular volume instead.

7. 0.25 kg of water at 100°C is fully boiled into steam (L_v ≈ 2.26×10⁶ J/kg). Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the heat required is ___ kJ.

(b) During this boiling process, the water's temperature:

(a) Q = mL_v = (0.25)(2.26×10⁶) = 565,000 J = 565 kJ. (b) This is exactly the flat plateau on a heating curve — a phase transition proceeds at constant T.

8. An engine operates between T_h = 800 K and T_c = 440 K. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: e_Carnot = ___ (as a decimal, e.g. 0.45).

(b) If this engine's measured actual efficiency is 0.36, is that consistent with the second law?

(a) e_Carnot = 1 − 440/800 = 1 − 0.55 = 0.45. (b) Real engines are irreversible and so must fall at or below e_Carnot — landing below it is expected, not a violation.

9. An engine operates between T_h = 550 K and T_c = 275 K, absorbing Q_h = 3800 J at actual efficiency e = 0.32. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: with W = eQ_h = 1216 J and Q_c = Q_h − W = 2584 J, the total entropy change ΔS_total = −Q_h/T_h + Q_c/T_c = ___ J/K (2 decimal places).

(b) This positive ΔS_total confirms:

(a) ΔS_total = −3800/550 + 2584/275 = −6.909 + 9.396 ≈ 2.49 J/K. (b) A strictly positive ΔS_total is the signature of irreversibility — exactly what's expected of any real engine falling short of its Carnot ceiling.

10. An engine absorbs Q_h = 5200 J per cycle at actual efficiency e = 0.32. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the heat exhausted is Q_c = Q_h − eQ_h = ___ J.

(b) The general method of this lesson — computing e_Carnot, the actual W and Q_c, and the total entropy change — generalizes to any real engine because:

(a) Q_c = Q_h − eQ_h = 5200 − (0.32)(5200) = 5200 − 1664 = 3536 J. (b) The audit uses only universal thermodynamic laws — no detail about the engine's internal mechanism is ever needed.
