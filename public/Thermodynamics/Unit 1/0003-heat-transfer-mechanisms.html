Heat Transfer Mechanisms

Heat moves in exactly three ways — conduction, convection, and radiation — and only one of them reduces to a clean formula you can compute by hand at this level.

← Lesson 2 Unit 2, Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Conduction and Fourier's Law Conduction transfers heat through direct molecular contact within a material (or between touching materials), with no bulk motion of the material itself — faster-vibrating molecules pass energy to their slower neighbors. For a slab of material, the steady-state rate of heat flow is Fourier's law: Q̇ = kAΔT/L, where k is the material's thermal conductivity (W/(m·K)), A is the cross-sectional area perpendicular to the heat flow (m²), ΔT is the temperature difference across the slab (K), and L is the slab's thickness in the direction of flow (m). Q̇ is a rate (watts, J/s) — the energy transferred over a time t is Q = Q̇t only while the rate stays constant.
Definition — Convection and Radiation Convection transfers heat by the bulk motion of a fluid carrying thermal energy from one place to another — natural convection when density differences alone drive the flow (warm air rising), forced convection when a fan or pump drives it. Radiation transfers heat via electromagnetic waves and needs no medium at all — the only one of the three that works through a vacuum, which is how sunlight reaches Earth. A hotter surface radiates dramatically more strongly (radiated power scales with T⁴, the Stefan–Boltzmann law) — this course only needs that qualitative point, not the full calculation.
Why Conduction Is the One We Compute Precisely Conduction is the only mechanism of the three with a single clean formula suitable for hand computation at this level — convection depends on fluid dynamics condensed into empirically-measured convection coefficients, and radiation's T⁴ dependence makes it nonlinear. Real structures lose heat through all three simultaneously (a wall loses heat by conduction through the material, convection at both faces, and radiation to its surroundings) — but Fourier's law is the natural entry point precisely because it's linear in every one of its variables.
Worked Example — Heat Loss Through a Window Pane A single glass pane (k = 0.8 W/(m·K)) has area A = 1.5 m² and thickness L = 0.005 m (5 mm), with a temperature difference ΔT = 20 K between inside and outside.
Q̇ = kAΔT/L = (0.8)(1.5)(20)/(0.005) = 24/0.005 = 4800 W.
That's a startlingly large number for one window — and exactly why real windows use multiple panes separated by an insulating gas gap (effectively adding thickness L and swapping in a much lower-conductivity gas) rather than a single sheet of glass.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In Fourier's law Q̇ = kAΔT/L, if the slab's thickness L is doubled while k, A, and ΔT stay fixed, the conduction rate Q̇:

Q̇ is inversely proportional to L, so doubling the thickness halves the rate — a thicker barrier conducts heat more slowly.

2. Conduction transfers heat by:

Conduction is molecule-to-molecule energy transfer with no bulk material movement — the fluid-motion description is convection, and the vacuum-capable, wave-based description is radiation.

3. Fill in the blank: an aluminum plate (k = 205 W/(m·K)) has area A = 0.02 m² and thickness L = 0.01 m, with ΔT = 35 K across it. The conduction rate is Q̇ = ___ W.

Q̇ = kAΔT/L = (205)(0.02)(35)/(0.01) = 143.5/0.01 = 14,350 W.

4. Which of the three heat transfer mechanisms can carry heat through a total vacuum, with no medium present at all?

Radiation is electromagnetic waves, which propagate through empty space with no medium required — it's how the Sun's heat reaches Earth across essentially a vacuum.
