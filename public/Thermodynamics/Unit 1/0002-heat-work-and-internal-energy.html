Heat, Work, and Internal Energy

"This gas contains 50 J of heat" is a sentence that sounds fine and is simply wrong. Heat and work are not things a system has — they're two different ways energy can cross its boundary.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Internal Energy, Heat, and Work Internal energy U is the sum of all the microscopic kinetic and potential energy of a system's particles — a property the system has at a given instant, regardless of how it got there. Heat Q is energy transferred between a system and its surroundings purely because of a temperature difference between them. Work W is energy transferred by any other means — in thermodynamics, most commonly a system's boundary physically moving, like a gas expanding against a piston. Sign convention used throughout this course: Q is positive when heat flows into the system; W is positive when work is done by the system on its surroundings. (The exact bookkeeping that ties these three together — the first law — is Lesson 4's job; this lesson's job is just to keep them from blurring into one another.)
Why Heat and Work Are Not Things a System "Has" A system has internal energy the way a bank account has a balance. Heat and work are like deposits and withdrawals — labels describing how energy crossed the boundary, not amounts sitting inside afterward. Once energy that arrived as heat is inside the system, it's simply part of U, indistinguishable from energy that arrived as work. That's why heat and work are called process quantities (their value depends on the path a process takes), while internal energy is a state quantity (its value depends only on the system's current state, not its history).
Worked Example — Two Paths to the Same ΔU Two identical rigid, insulated gas samples both end up with internal energy exactly 100 J higher than they started, but reached it differently.
Path A: the container is briefly un-insulated and 100 J of heat flows in (Q = 100 J); the container is rigid, so no boundary moves and no work is possible (W = 0 J).
Path B (the classic Joule paddle-wheel setup): the container stays sealed and insulated (Q = 0 J), but an external motor spins an internal paddle wheel, doing 100 J of work on the gas (W = −100 J, by the sign convention above, since the gas does negative work on its surroundings).
Both processes land on the exact same final U — proof that U only cares where the system ends up, while Q and W individually depended entirely on which path was taken.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which of these is a state function — depending only on a system's current state, not the path taken to reach it?

U depends only on where the system currently is; Q and W are process quantities whose values depend on exactly which path the system took to get there.

2. In the classic Joule paddle-wheel experiment, a sealed, rigid, thermally insulated container of gas is stirred by an external motor until its temperature rises. Treating the gas as the system, which pair correctly describes Q and W?

Insulation blocks heat specifically, so Q = 0 regardless of the temperature rise; the paddle wheel does mechanical work on the gas without the container's boundary moving, so W (work done by the gas) is negative.

3. Fill in the blank: energy transferred between a system and its surroundings purely because of a temperature difference is called ___.

That's precisely the definition of heat — distinct from work, which is any other mode of energy transfer.

4. Why is it wrong to say "this gas contains 30 J of heat"?

Heat and work are process quantities describing how energy crossed the boundary; only internal energy is a property the system "has" at any given moment.
