Adiabatic Processes

Cut off heat entirely and a gas can still change temperature dramatically — every joule of that change now has to come from work alone, and the PV curve bends into a new shape to prove it.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Adiabatic Process, PVᵞ = const An adiabatic process is one with Q = 0 throughout — either the system is perfectly insulated, or the process happens too fast for heat to meaningfully transfer. Combining the first law (ΔU = −W, since Q = 0) with the ideal gas law leads to PVᵞ = constant along the path, where γ = C_P/C_V is the ratio of specific heats at constant pressure and constant volume (γ ≈ 5/3 for a monatomic gas, γ ≈ 7/5 for a diatomic gas like air). Equivalently, TVᵞ⁻¹ = constant.
Method — Solving Between Two Adiabatic States Given any two states 1 and 2 connected by an adiabatic process: P₁V₁ᵞ = P₂V₂ᵞ, so P₂ = P₁(V₁/V₂)ᵞ. Because γ > 1, this makes an adiabatic curve on a PV diagram noticeably steeper than an isothermal curve (PV = constant) through the same starting point — compressing a gas adiabatically raises its pressure faster than compressing it isothermally would.
Why Adiabatic Compression Heats a Gas More Than Isothermal Compression In an isothermal compression, any tendency for T to rise is instantly canceled by heat escaping to keep T fixed — that's what "isothermal" means. In an adiabatic compression, Q = 0 means there's nowhere for that energy to go: every joule of work done on the gas stays trapped as internal energy, so T rises for real. This is exactly why a bicycle pump gets warm during fast strokes (nearly adiabatic) but barely warms at all if compressed infinitely slowly (nearly isothermal).
Worked Example — Adiabatic Compression Air (γ = 1.4) starts at P₁ = 1×10⁵ Pa, V₁ = 0.02 m³, and is adiabatically compressed to V₂ = 0.01 m³. Find P₂.
P₂ = P₁(V₁/V₂)ᵞ = (1×10⁵)(0.02/0.01)^1.4 = (1×10⁵)(2)^1.4 ≈ (1×10⁵)(2.639) ≈ 2.64×10⁵ Pa — more than double, despite the volume only halving.

1. What single condition defines an adiabatic process?

Adiabatic specifically means Q = 0; W and ΔU are typically both nonzero, and T can change substantially.

2. Fill in the blank: a monatomic gas (γ = 5/3) starts at P₁ = 8×10⁴ Pa, V₁ = 0.03 m³, and is adiabatically compressed to V₂ = 0.015 m³. P₂ ≈ ___ ×10³ Pa (nearest whole number).

P₂ = P₁(V₁/V₂)ᵞ = (8×10⁴)(0.03/0.015)^(5/3) = (8×10⁴)(2)^1.667 ≈ (8×10⁴)(3.1748) ≈ 2.54×10⁵ Pa ≈ 254×10³ Pa.

3. On a PV diagram, starting from the same point and compressing by the same ΔV, an adiabatic curve compared to an isothermal curve is:

Since PVᵞ = const with γ > 1, pressure rises faster with decreasing V along an adiabatic path than along an isothermal path (PV = const, effectively γ = 1).

4. Why does adiabatically compressing a gas raise its temperature more than isothermally compressing it by the same ΔV?

Isothermal compression continuously bleeds off heat to hold T fixed; adiabatic compression has nowhere for that energy to go, so ΔU = −W drives a genuine temperature rise.
