The Carnot Cycle

Lesson 13 said efficiency always falls short of 100%. This lesson names the exact ceiling — a number set entirely by two temperatures, with no dependence on the working substance, the engine's design, or anything else.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — The Carnot Cycle and Carnot Efficiency The Carnot cycle is a specific four-leg reversible cycle between two reservoirs at T_h and T_c: isothermal expansion at T_h, adiabatic expansion, isothermal compression at T_c, adiabatic compression — closing back to the start. Carnot's theorem: no engine operating between T_h and T_c can exceed the Carnot cycle's efficiency, e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h (T_h and T_c both in Kelvin — this formula fails silently, giving a nonsense answer, if you plug in Celsius). This is the theoretical maximum, achievable only in the idealized, perfectly reversible limit.
Why the Carnot Ceiling Depends on Nothing but Two Temperatures It's a striking result: e_Carnot doesn't care whether the working substance is an ideal gas, steam, or anything else, and it doesn't care about the engine's mechanical design. Carnot's theorem proves this by contradiction — if a more-efficient engine existed, running a Carnot engine backward (as a refrigerator) off that hypothetical engine's output would let you move heat cold-to-hot with no net work input, violating the Clausius statement from Lesson 10. The ceiling is a consequence of the second law itself, not of any particular technology.
Worked Example — Carnot Efficiency A Carnot engine operates between T_h = 600 K and T_c = 300 K. Find its maximum possible efficiency.
e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h = 1 − 300/600 = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5 = 50%. No real engine running between these two temperatures — however cleverly built — can beat 50%.

1. Why must T_h and T_c be in Kelvin when computing e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h?

Because Celsius and Fahrenheit have arbitrary zero points, T_c/T_h in those scales is not a physically meaningful ratio — only Kelvin's absolute zero makes the ratio (and therefore the efficiency) correct.

2. Fill in the blank: a Carnot engine operates between T_h = 500 K and T_c = 320 K. e_Carnot = ___ (as a decimal, e.g. 0.36).

e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h = 1 − 320/500 = 1 − 0.64 = 0.36.

3. A real engine operating between the same T_h and T_c as a Carnot engine will have an actual efficiency that is:

The Carnot cycle is a perfectly reversible idealization; every real engine has some irreversibility, which always pulls its actual efficiency below the Carnot ceiling for the same two temperatures.

4. As T_c approaches T_h (the two reservoirs get closer in temperature), e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h approaches:

As T_c → T_h, the ratio T_c/T_h → 1, so e_Carnot → 0 — a large temperature difference is exactly what a heat engine needs to do useful work.
