SI Units and Dimensional Analysis

Every engineering discipline speaks the same seven-unit language — and that language can catch your mistakes before you make them.

← Unit 1, Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Whichever discipline a problem belongs to, every engineer measures the physical world with the same base units. This lesson covers that shared system, and a technique — dimensional analysis — that turns unit-tracking into a built-in error checker.

The seven SI base units

Definition The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units that every other unit is built from: the meter (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), ampere (electric current), kelvin (temperature), mole (amount of substance), and candela (luminous intensity).

Almost everything in this course only needs the first three. A newton (force) is defined as a kilogram·meter per second squared. A joule (energy) is a newton·meter. A watt (power) is a joule per second. Every "derived" unit you'll meet is just a combination of the seven base units — nothing is a free-floating, independently defined quantity.

Dimensional analysis: tracking units through a calculation

Definition Dimensional analysis means tracking the units through every step of a calculation, the same way you'd track the numbers — and confirming the units on both sides of an equation actually match.
Example A car travels at 20 meters/second for 3 seconds. Distance = speed × time = (20 m/s) × (3 s) = 60 m. Notice the seconds cancel — (m/s) × s = m — leaving exactly the unit you'd expect for a distance. If your answer had come out in m/s² instead, you'd know immediately that a step was wrong, before ever checking the number itself.
Why it works as an error check A physically correct equation must have matching units on both sides — this is called dimensional homogeneity. If you compute a result in the wrong units, you have hard proof a mistake happened somewhere, even if you can't immediately see which step. It's a free, mechanical sanity check available on every single calculation.

Unit conversion by multiplying by 1

Converting between units (say, inches to meters) is really just multiplying by a conversion factor that equals exactly 1 — because the numerator and denominator describe the same physical length, just in different units.

Example Convert 5 feet to meters, given 1 meter ≈ 3.281 feet:
5 ft × (1 m / 3.281 ft) ≈ 1.524 m

The "ft" units cancel — one in the numerator of 5 ft, one in the denominator of the conversion factor — leaving only meters. Set up correctly, the units themselves tell you whether to multiply or divide by the conversion factor; you never have to just remember which way it goes.

Why SI, and not the imperial system engineers sometimes still use

Almost every country and almost every scientific and engineering field uses SI units, because every conversion between SI units is a clean power of 10 (1 kilometer = 1000 meters), where imperial conversions are arbitrary (1 mile = 5,280 feet). The United States still uses imperial units in parts of daily life and some legacy engineering fields — which is exactly why dimensional analysis matters so much in practice: unit mismatches between systems are a real, recurring source of engineering error.

Why it matters In 1999, NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter — a $125 million spacecraft — because one engineering team's software used imperial units (pound-force seconds) while another team's software expected metric units (newton seconds), and the mismatch was never caught. Careful, consistent unit tracking isn't a formality; it's a real safeguard against a specific, well-documented category of failure.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. How many base units does the SI system define?

Meter, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela — seven base units that every derived unit is built from.

2. What does it mean for a physically correct equation to be "dimensionally homogeneous"?

Dimensional homogeneity means the units — not the numbers — must match on both sides, which is what makes dimensional analysis useful as an error check.

3. Why did the Mars Climate Orbiter mission fail?

The failure was a systems-level unit mismatch between two teams' software, not a single arithmetic slip — a real, well-documented case of dimensional analysis being skipped at scale.

4. Fill in the blank: the derived SI unit of force, defined as a kilogram·meter per second squared, is called the ___.

The newton (kg·m/s²) is built entirely from the base units of mass, length, and time.

5. Fill in the blank: a unit conversion factor always equals exactly ___, because the numerator and denominator describe the same physical quantity in different units.

Multiplying by a fraction that equals 1 changes the units without changing the actual quantity being measured.
