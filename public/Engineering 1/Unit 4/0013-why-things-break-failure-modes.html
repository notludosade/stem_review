Why Things Break — Failure Modes

"It held once" doesn't mean "it's safe" — three ways materials fail, none of which look like a single overload event.

← Lesson 12 Unit 5, Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Everything so far in this unit assumed a single, static load. But most real failures aren't caused by one dramatic overload — they're caused by repeated, gradual, or environmental effects that a single stress calculation misses entirely. This lesson covers the three big ones.

Fatigue: failure from repetition, not overload

Definition Fatigue is failure caused by repeated cycles of stress well below the material's yield point — stress that would never cause failure if applied just once, but which slowly grows a microscopic crack with every cycle until the crack suddenly propagates and the part fails.
Example Bend a metal paperclip back and forth repeatedly. Each individual bend applies stress far below what would snap it in one motion — but after enough repetitions, it breaks anyway. This is fatigue, and it's exactly why the paperclip failure surprises people who only think in terms of "how hard can I pull it once."
Why fatigue is dangerous in practice A part can pass every static load test with flying colors and still fail in service, because static tests apply load once, while fatigue depends on how many times a load repeats over the part's lifetime. Airplane fuselages, bridges under traffic, and rotating machine shafts all experience millions of load cycles — fatigue, not one-time overload, is the dominant failure concern for all three.

Fracture: sudden, brittle failure

Definition Fracture is sudden failure that propagates from an existing flaw or crack — even a microscopic one — especially in brittle materials, often with little or no warning deformation beforehand.

Recall from Lesson 11 that brittle materials fail suddenly, without the visible warning ductile materials give. Fracture failures are especially dangerous specifically because of this — they concentrate stress at the tip of a crack far beyond the average stress across the part, so a small, easy-to-miss flaw can trigger a failure that looks, from the outside, like it came out of nowhere.

Corrosion: gradual material loss

Definition Corrosion is the gradual degradation of a material through chemical reaction with its environment — most commonly a metal reacting with oxygen and moisture (rusting), slowly reducing the effective cross-sectional area carrying load.
Why corrosion compounds with the other failure modes Corrosion rarely causes failure entirely on its own — its real danger is how it interacts with the other two. Recall stress = F / A: as corrosion eats away at a member's cross-sectional area, the stress from the same original force keeps rising, even though nothing about the applied load has changed. And a corrosion pit is itself a flaw that concentrates stress exactly the way a fracture-triggering crack does — corrosion often creates the very flaw that a later fatigue or fracture failure starts from.

The common thread

All three failure modes share something a single static stress calculation misses: time. Fatigue depends on the number of cycles, fracture depends on a flaw that may have existed unnoticed for a long time, and corrosion is a slow accumulation. This is exactly why "it held when we tested it" is not the same claim as "it will hold for its full service life" — and why inspection, maintenance, and safety margins (the next unit's subject) exist as a permanent part of engineering practice, not a one-time step.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What makes fatigue failure surprising compared to a simple overload failure?

Fatigue accumulates from many cycles of stress that would each be individually harmless — a single static load test can miss it entirely.

2. Why is fracture especially dangerous in brittle materials?

Fracture concentrates stress at a crack tip and, in a brittle material, gives none of the visible bending that would otherwise provide advance warning.

3. Why does corrosion often make fatigue or fracture failure more likely, rather than acting as a separate, independent failure mode?

Corrosion both raises effective stress (by shrinking area) and creates crack-like flaws — often becoming the starting point for a later fatigue or fracture failure.

4. Fill in the blank: failure caused by many repeated cycles of stress, each individually below the yield point, is called ___.

Fatigue grows a microscopic crack a little further with every cycle, until it suddenly propagates and the part fails.

5. Fill in the blank: gradual material loss from a chemical reaction with the environment, like rusting, is called ___.

Corrosion shrinks a member's effective cross-sectional area over time, raising the stress a given force produces.
