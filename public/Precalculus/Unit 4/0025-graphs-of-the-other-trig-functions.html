Graphs of the Other Trig Functions & Inverse Trig Functions

Four more graphs, built entirely out of waves you already know — then the operation that undoes all six of them, turning a ratio back into an angle.

← Lesson 24 Lesson 26 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 24 unrolled sin x and cos x into waves. The other four functions don't need their own unrolling — every one of them is a ratio of sin x and cos x, so their graphs are already determined. This lesson just reads off what that ratio does.

1. Tangent and cotangent

Key features tan x = sin x / cos x is undefined wherever cos x = 0 — a vertical asymptote at every x = π/2 + kπ. Its period is only π, half of sine and cosine's period, because shifting x by π flips the sign of both sin x and cos x, which cancels out in the ratio. cot x = cos x / sin x is undefined wherever sin x = 0 instead, also has period π, and decreases everywhere tan x increases.

cot x is tan's mirror: its asymptotes fall at multiples of π (where sin x = 0) rather than at π/2 + kπ, and its branches slope the opposite way — decreasing left to right instead of increasing.

2. Secant and cosecant

Key features sec x = 1/cos x is undefined at the same x-values as tan x (wherever cos x = 0), but its period is 2π, inherited from cosine rather than halved. Its graph forms U-shaped branches that hug cos x's hills and valleys, opening away from the x-axis. csc x = 1/sin x behaves the same way relative to sin x, with asymptotes wherever sin x = 0.
Worked Example sec(0) = 1/cos(0) = 1/1 = 1. As x increases toward π/2, cos x shrinks toward 0⁺, so sec x = 1/cos x grows without bound — the same asymptote tan x has at π/2, for the same reason: cos x = 0 in the denominator.

3. Inverse trigonometric functions

Every trig function repeats its output forever, so none of them is one-to-one over its full domain — infinitely many inputs share any given output, and the graph fails the horizontal line test everywhere. To build an inverse, first restrict the domain to a single piece narrow enough to be one-to-one.

Formula — Restricted domains and inverses arcsin x (or sin⁻¹x): domain [−1, 1], range [−π/2, π/2]. arccos x: domain [−1, 1], range [0, π]. arctan x: domain all reals, range (−π/2, π/2). Each inverse's domain and range are the original function's range and (restricted) domain, swapped — the same domain/range-swap rule every inverse function follows.
Worked Example arcsin(1/2) = π/6, since sin(π/6) = 1/2 and π/6 falls inside arcsin's range [−π/2, π/2]. arccos(−1/2) = 2π/3, since cos(2π/3) = −1/2 and 2π/3 falls inside [0, π]. arctan(1) = π/4, since tan(π/4) = 1.

Check your recall

1. The period of sec x and csc x (matching cos x and sin x) is:

sec x = 1/cos x and csc x = 1/sin x inherit their period directly from cos x and sin x — no halving like tan and cot, since flipping the sign of the ratio's single term (not a ratio of two terms) doesn't cancel anything at half a period.

2. The period of tan x and cot x is ___.

Shifting x by π flips the sign of both sin x and cos x, and those two sign flips cancel in the ratio tan x = sin x/cos x — so the pattern repeats after only half of sine and cosine's 2π period.

3. arcsin(1/2) equals:

sin(π/6) = 1/2, and π/6 lies inside arcsin's range [−π/2, π/2]. The distractors are the other common special-angle outputs sin could also equal 1/2 outside that range, or answers to a different ratio entirely.
