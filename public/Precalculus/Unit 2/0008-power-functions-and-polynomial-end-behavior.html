Power Functions & Polynomial End Behavior

A new function family — and the single rule that predicts what it does infinitely far out.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 1 was about functions in general — evaluating them, composing them, transforming them. Starting here, the next few lessons zoom in on specific families of functions, beginning with polynomials: sums of whole-number powers of x.

Definition A polynomial function is a sum of terms, each a number times x raised to a non-negative integer power: f(x) = aₙxⁿ + ... + a₁x + a₀. The degree n is the highest power present; the leading term is aₙxⁿ, and aₙ is the leading coefficient.

1. Power functions, one degree at a time

The simplest polynomials are single-term power functions, f(x) = xⁿ. Slide n and watch the shape — and note which side of the graph goes up and which goes down.

Odd n and even n split into two distinct patterns: odd powers send the two ends of the graph in opposite directions; even powers send both ends the same direction. That split is about to explain every polynomial's end behavior, not just single-term ones.

2. The Leading Coefficient Test

Theorem Leading Coefficient Test: as x → ±∞, a polynomial's graph behaves exactly like its leading term aₙxⁿ — every other term becomes negligible in comparison. Only two things about the leading term matter: whether n is even or odd, and whether aₙ is positive or negative.
Why it works Factor out the leading term: aₙxⁿ(1 + an−1/(aₙx) + ...). As |x| grows huge, every fraction inside the parentheses shrinks toward 0, leaving just aₙxⁿ · 1. The lower-degree terms don't vanish — they just get overwhelmingly outpaced.

That gives four cases. Flip through them below — each one is just a power function's end behavior, with a sign attached.

Try It — The Four End-Behavior Cases

f(x) = x³ — odd degree, positive leading coefficient: falls left, rises right.

f(x) = −x³ — odd degree, negative leading coefficient: rises left, falls right.

f(x) = x² — even degree, positive leading coefficient: rises left, rises right.

f(x) = −x² — even degree, negative leading coefficient: falls left, falls right.

1. A degree-5 polynomial with a negative leading coefficient has end behavior:

Degree 5 is odd, so the ends go opposite directions; a negative leading coefficient flips the usual odd-positive pattern, giving rises-left, falls-right — the mirror image of x³.

2. A polynomial's end behavior is decided entirely by:

Parity alone isn't enough — the leading coefficient's sign matters too. The full leading term (degree and sign together) is what dominates as |x| grows huge, so it alone decides end behavior.

3. For very large |x|, a polynomial's graph behaves like its ___ term.

Every lower-degree term becomes negligible compared to the leading term as |x| grows without bound — that's the whole content of the Leading Coefficient Test.
