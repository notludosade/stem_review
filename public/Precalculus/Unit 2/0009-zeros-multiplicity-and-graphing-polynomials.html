Zeros, Multiplicity & Graphing Polynomials

End behavior handles the far edges. This lesson fills in everything in between.

← Lesson 8 Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

A polynomial's zeros — the x-values where it crosses or touches the x-axis — are exactly the values that make one of its factors equal 0. If f(x) = (x + 2)(x − 1)(x − 3), setting each factor to zero gives x = −2, 1, and 3 immediately, no further solving needed. The interesting part isn't finding them here; it's what the graph does at each one.

1. Multiplicity: not every zero looks the same

Definition If (x − r) appears k times in a polynomial's factored form, r is a zero of multiplicity k. Odd multiplicity (1, 3, 5, ...): the graph crosses the x-axis at r. Even multiplicity (2, 4, 6, ...): the graph touches r and bounces back without crossing.

2. Turning points

Between its zeros, a polynomial's graph rises and falls smoothly — no corners, no breaks (Lesson 8 already established the "smooth, continuous curve" idea for the ends; the same holds everywhere). Each place it switches from rising to falling (or back) is a turning point.

Rule A degree-n polynomial has at most n − 1 turning points. A cubic can wiggle at most twice; a quartic, at most three times. It can have fewer — this is a ceiling, not a guarantee.

3. Putting it together

Combine end behavior (Lesson 8), zeros, and multiplicity, and the whole shape of a polynomial is predictable before plotting a single extra point. Take f(x) = (x + 2)(x − 1)²(x − 3) — degree 4, positive leading coefficient, so both ends rise (Lesson 8's even-positive case). Drag along the curve and check each zero against its marker.
