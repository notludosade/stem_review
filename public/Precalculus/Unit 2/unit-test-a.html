1. A degree-4 polynomial has a positive leading coefficient. What is its end behavior?

Even degree means both ends point the same direction; a positive leading coefficient means that direction is up.

2. Fill in the blank: For a power function f(x) = xⁿ with n odd, the two ends of the graph point in ___ directions.

Odd powers send one end up and the other down — for example x³ falls on the left and rises on the right.

3. For f(x) = (x + 1)³(x − 4), answer both parts:

(a) What happens at x = −1?

(b) Fill in the blank: What is the degree of f? ___

(a) (x + 1) appears three times — odd multiplicity — so the graph crosses straight through the axis at x = −1. (b) Adding up the exponents on the factors, 3 + 1 = 4, gives the polynomial's degree.

4. Answer both parts about turning points:

(a) What is the maximum possible number of turning points for a degree-6 polynomial?

(b) A polynomial's graph has 2 turning points. What is the smallest possible degree it could have?

(a) A degree-n polynomial has at most n − 1 turning points, so degree 6 allows at most 5. (b) Running that rule in reverse, 2 turning points require a degree of at least 3.

5. Answer both parts about dividing f(x) by (x − r):

(a) Fill in the blank: dividing f(x) by (x − r) always leaves a remainder equal to ___.

(b) If that remainder happens to be 0, what must be true about r?

(a) That's the Remainder Theorem — the remainder equals the function evaluated at r, with no separate substitution needed. (b) A remainder of 0 means f(r) = 0, so r is exactly a zero of f — this is the Factor Theorem.

6. A degree-6 polynomial with real coefficients has, counting multiplicity:

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees exactly n zeros, counting multiplicity, for a degree-n polynomial — some of those zeros may be complex rather than real.

7. For f(x) = (x − 3)(x + 2) / [(x − 3)(x + 6)], answer both parts:

(a) What happens at x = 3?

(b) What happens at x = −6?

(a) (x − 3) divides out of both the numerator and denominator, so the graph is undefined at x = 3 but doesn't blow up there — a removable hole, not an asymptote. (b) (x + 6) is zero at x = −6 but doesn't cancel with anything in the numerator, so the graph grows without bound there instead — a true vertical asymptote.

8. What is the domain of f(x) = (x + 1) / (x² − 9)?

The denominator x² − 9 equals 0 at x = 3 and x = −3, so those two values are excluded from the domain.

9. For f(x) = (3x² + 1) / (x² − 4), answer both parts:

(a) What is the horizontal asymptote?

(b) Where are the vertical asymptotes?

(a) The degrees match, so the horizontal asymptote is the ratio of leading coefficients: 3/1 = 3. (b) The denominator x² − 4 factors as (x − 2)(x + 2) and doesn't cancel with the numerator, so vertical asymptotes sit at x = 2 and x = −2.

10. Fill in the blank: A rational function has a slant asymptote when the numerator's degree exceeds the denominator's degree by exactly ___.

Exactly one degree of difference leaves a linear quotient after division — the equation of the slant asymptote.
