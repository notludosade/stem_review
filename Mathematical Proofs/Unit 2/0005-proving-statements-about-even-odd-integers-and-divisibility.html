Proving Statements About Even/Odd Integers and Divisibility

The most common testing ground for direct proof: a small, closed world of integers, parity, and divisibility, where every claim reduces to substituting a definition and doing algebra.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 4 established the shape of a direct proof. This lesson runs that shape through its most common testing ground: parity (even/odd) and divisibility. Nearly every claim here follows the identical pattern — unpack a definition into an existential witness, do algebra, repackage the result to match the target definition's form.

Reference — Parity Facts even + even = even  ·  odd + odd = even  ·  even + odd = odd  ·  even × (any integer) = even  ·  odd × odd = odd. Lesson 4 already proved the first fact in full. This lesson proves one more in full and leaves the rest as the kind of routine direct-proof exercise this technique is built to make routine.
Theorem — Product of Two Odd Integers If a and b are odd integers, then ab is odd.
Proof Assume a and b are odd integers. By definition, a = 2j + 1 and b = 2k + 1 for some integers j, k. Then ab = (2j + 1)(2k + 1) = 4jk + 2j + 2k + 1 = 2(2jk + j + k) + 1. Since j and k are integers, 2jk + j + k is an integer — call it m. So ab = 2m + 1 with m ∈ ℤ, which means ab is odd by definition. ∎

Divisibility proofs follow the same pattern

Theorem — Transitivity of Divisibility If a ∣ b and b ∣ c, then a ∣ c.
Proof Assume a ∣ b and b ∣ c. By definition of divides, b = ak for some integer k, and c = bm for some integer m. Substituting the first equation into the second: c = (ak)m = a(km). Since k and m are integers, km is an integer. So c = a(km) with km ∈ ℤ, which by definition means a ∣ c. ∎
Why using two different letters, j and k, was not optional Every existential definition hands you a witness that is allowed to be different each time it's invoked, unless the claim explicitly says otherwise. a = 2j + 1 and b = 2k + 1 use two different letters precisely because a and b are two independently chosen odd integers — nothing in the hypothesis says they're equal or related. Reusing the same letter for both would silently assume they're the same number, smuggling in an extra hypothesis the claim never granted. The widget below is built around exactly this mistake.

1. What is the parity of the sum of two odd integers?

(2j+1) + (2k+1) = 2j + 2k + 2 = 2(j + k + 1), which matches the definition of even — this holds for every pair of odd integers, not just some.

2. In the proof that a product of two odd integers is odd, what is the witness m such that ab = 2m + 1?

Expanding ab = 4jk + 2j + 2k + 1 and factoring 2 out of the non-constant terms gives 2(2jk + j + k) + 1, so m = 2jk + j + k.

3. Is the claim "if a ∣ bc, then a ∣ b or a ∣ c" true in general?

4 ∣ 12 is true, yet 4 divides neither factor — a single counterexample (Lesson 2) disproves the general claim. (It does become true when a is prime, but that's a separate, stronger hypothesis.)

4. Fill in the blank: the property "if a ∣ b and b ∣ c, then a ∣ c" is called ___ of divisibility.

Transitivity — proved above by substituting b = ak into c = bm to get c = a(km).

5. Fill in the blank: reusing the same letter k for two independently chosen existential witnesses silently (and wrongly) assumes those two quantities are ___.

Using a = 2k and b = 2k for two arbitrary even integers a and b silently forces a = b — exactly the gap in the widget above.
