Strong Induction

Some inductive steps need more than just the case immediately before them — they need every earlier case, on call, all at once. Same overall shape as Lesson 10, one enlarged assumption.

← Lesson 11 Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Lessons 10 and 11 used the inductive hypothesis "P(k) is true" to prove P(k+1) — the single case immediately before the one being proved. Some claims can't be attacked that way, because P(k+1) genuinely depends on some earlier case P(j) that has no fixed relationship to k at all. Strong induction hands you every earlier case as an assumption, not just the last one.

Definition — The Principle of Strong Induction Let P(n) be a statement depending on a natural number n. Suppose: (1) Base case: P(n₀) is true for some starting value n₀, and (2) Inductive step: for every k ≥ n₀, if P(n₀), P(n₀+1), …, P(k) are all true, then P(k+1) is true. Then P(n) is true for every integer n ≥ n₀.
The precise difference from weak (ordinary) induction — get this exactly right The two techniques differ in exactly one place: what the inductive hypothesis is allowed to assume. Weak induction's inductive step assumes only P(k) — the single previous case — to prove P(k+1). Strong induction's inductive step assumes P(n₀) through P(k) — every case so far, not just the last one — to prove P(k+1). They are not different in what they ultimately prove (either can prove anything the other can, and strong induction can always be rewritten as an equivalent weak-induction argument on a combined statement), but they differ sharply in which claims are natural and easy to prove with each: some inductive steps only ever need the immediately preceding case, and some genuinely need an arbitrary earlier one.
Theorem — Every Integer ≥ 2 Is a Product of Primes Every integer n ≥ 2 can be written as a product of one or more prime numbers.
Proof Base case (n=2): 2 is itself prime, so it's a "product" of the single prime 2.
Inductive step: let k ≥ 2, and assume every integer from 2 through k is a product of primes (the strong inductive hypothesis). Consider k+1. Either k+1 is prime — in which case it's trivially a product of one prime, itself — or k+1 is composite, meaning k+1 = a · b for some integers a, b with 2 ≤ a, b ≤ k. Since both a and b lie in the range 2 through k, the inductive hypothesis applies to both: a is a product of primes, and b is a product of primes. Then k+1 = a · b is a product of primes (the primes from a's factorization together with the primes from b's factorization). Either way, k+1 is a product of primes. By strong induction, every integer n ≥ 2 is a product of primes. ∎
Why weak induction genuinely cannot run this argument Weak induction would only hand you "k is a product of primes" to work with. But if k+1 = 24, its factorization might be 24 = 4 × 6 — and neither 4 nor 6 is k = 23. Knowing 23 factors into primes says nothing directly useful about 4 or 6. What the proof actually needs is "some earlier number, whichever one a or b happens to be," which could be almost anywhere in the range 2 through k depending on how k+1 factors — exactly what strong induction, and only strong induction, hands you for free.
Try It — Why 12 Needs More Than Just P(11)

Suppose you've already proved P(2) through P(11). Click "Reveal next" to see why proving P(12) needs strong induction.

  1. 12 is composite. 12 = 3 × 4.
  2. Weak induction only offers P(11). But 11 is prime and has nothing to do with factoring 12 — P(11) alone is useless here.
  3. Strong induction offers P(2) through P(11), all at once. In particular, it offers P(3) and P(4) — exactly the two factors 12 actually needs.
  4. Apply P(3) and P(4). P(3): 3 is already prime. P(4): 4 = 2 × 2, a product of primes.
  5. Combine. 12 = 3 × 4 = 3 × 2 × 2 — a product of primes. ∎ The specific earlier cases needed (3 and 4) were only knowable after seeing how 12 happened to factor — strong induction is what makes both available regardless.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What exactly does the inductive hypothesis assume in strong induction, that weak induction does not?

Strong induction's inductive hypothesis is the full range P(n₀), …, P(k) — weak induction's is just the single case P(k).

2. Why does weak induction fail to directly prove "every integer ≥ 2 is a product of primes"?

k+1's factors could be any pair of smaller numbers, not specifically k — exactly the gap strong induction's full range of assumptions closes.

3. Fill in the blank: the technique whose inductive step assumes every case from the base case through k, not just P(k) alone, is called ___.

Strong induction — the technique this entire lesson defines, needed whenever P(k+1) can depend on an arbitrary earlier case.

4. What is the base case of the "every integer ≥ 2 is a product of primes" proof, and why does it need no further factoring?

2 is the smallest integer the claim covers, and since it's already prime, it needs no factoring to serve as a valid base case.

5. Fill in the blank: using strong induction, 15 = 3 × ___, and since both 3 and 5 are prime, 15 is a product of primes.

15 = 3 × 5, and applying the strong inductive hypothesis to both 3 and 5 (each already prime) completes the factorization.
