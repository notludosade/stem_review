Proof by Contradiction

Assume the claim is false, reason forward anyway, and watch the assumption collapse under its own weight. A genuinely different logical structure from contrapositive, not just another name for it.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 7 handled claims where the contrapositive was easier to work with directly. Some claims resist even that — there's no clean way to reason forward from any single hypothesis toward the conclusion. Proof by contradiction sidesteps the problem entirely: assume the claim is false, and show that assumption is untenable.

Definition — Proof by Contradiction To prove a statement S is true by contradiction: assume ¬S, then reason forward through justified steps until reaching some statement R together with its negation ¬R (or any other statement already known to be false). Since no true set of assumptions can produce a genuine contradiction, ¬S must itself be false — so S is true.
Applying it to a conditional P → Q Since ¬(P → Q) is logically equivalent to P ∧ ¬Q (Lesson 1's conditional is false in exactly this one case), a contradiction proof of P → Q assumes both P and ¬Q simultaneously, then derives a contradiction from that combined assumption.
Contradiction vs. contrapositive, restated precisely Lesson 7 already flagged this, and it's worth restating from the other side. Contrapositive proof of P → Q assumes only ¬Q, reasons forward with ordinary direct-proof steps, and concludes ¬P — no falsehood is ever reached, and P is never part of the assumption. Contradiction proof of P → Q assumes P and ¬Q together, and the argument ends not with a clean conclusion but with two statements that flatly conflict. If your proof only ever needed ¬Q to get moving and never touched P, you were doing contrapositive the whole time, whether you called it that or not.
Worked example Claim: If x is rational and y is irrational, then x + y is irrational.
Proof. Suppose, toward contradiction, that x is rational, y is irrational, and x + y is rational (the negation of the conclusion, held alongside the hypothesis). Since x is rational, x = a/b for integers a, b with b ≠ 0. Since x + y is rational, x + y = c/d for integers c, d with d ≠ 0. Then y = (x + y) − x = c/d − a/b = (cb − ad)/(db), a ratio of two integers with nonzero denominator — so y is rational. But this contradicts the assumption that y is irrational. This contradiction shows the assumption was false, so x + y must be irrational. ∎
Where the actual contradiction lives The proof above assumed "y is irrational" as part of its hypothesis, and separately derived "y is rational" three lines later, purely from algebra. Those two statements — one assumed, one derived — cannot both be true, and that clash, not any single line on its own, is the contradiction. 1. To prove a conditional P → Q by contradiction, what do you assume?

¬(P → Q) is equivalent to P ∧ ¬Q, so a contradiction proof of P → Q assumes both pieces together and hunts for a conflict.

2. In the worked example proving "x rational, y irrational ⟹ x+y irrational," what two statements form the actual contradiction?

y is assumed irrational at the start and shown to be rational three steps later — those two claims about the same object, y, cannot both hold.

3. Fill in the blank: reasoning forward from ¬S to a statement and its own negation is called reaching a ___.

A contradiction — two statements that cannot both be true — is the signal that ¬S must have been false, so S holds.

4. Which technique assumes the hypothesis P is true while also assuming the conclusion is false, all at once?

Only contradiction combines P and ¬Q as simultaneous assumptions; contrapositive uses ¬Q alone, and direct proof uses P alone.

5. Fill in the blank: reaching a genuine contradiction shows that the assumption ¬S must be ___, so S is true.

Since no true set of assumptions can produce a real contradiction, reaching one proves ¬S was false all along — so S holds.
