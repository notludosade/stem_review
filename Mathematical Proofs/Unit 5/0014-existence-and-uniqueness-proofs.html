Existence and Uniqueness Proofs

"There is a solution" and "there is exactly one solution" are different claims, proved by different arguments — and mixing them up is one of the easiest mistakes to make without noticing.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 3 already covered proving a plain existential claim: produce one witness and verify it. This lesson adds the second half of the picture — proving that a witness is the only one — and insists, carefully, that the two are genuinely separate proof obligations.

Definition — Existence and Uniqueness An existence claim, ∃x, P(x), asserts at least one object satisfying P(x) exists — proved by exhibiting one witness. A uniqueness claim asserts at most one object satisfies P(x) — proved by assuming two objects x₁ and x₂ both satisfy P, then showing x₁ = x₂. The combined claim "there exists a unique x such that P(x)," written ∃!x, P(x), asserts both: at least one witness exists, and any two witnesses must coincide. Proving ∃! therefore takes two separate arguments, not one.
Why these really are different claims, not two phrasings of the same thing Existence and uniqueness can hold independently of each other. "There is an even prime number" has existence (2 works) but not uniqueness in the interesting sense — the claim as stated doesn't even assert it, and in fact 2 is the only even prime, but that's a separate fact requiring its own argument. "The equation x² = −1 has at most one real solution" has uniqueness (vacuously — since no real solution exists at all, "at most one" is trivially true) but no existence. Only when both arguments are supplied does ∃! hold, and a proof that only produces a witness, or only shows two hypothetical witnesses must match, has proved just one half.
Theorem — Every Nonzero Real Number Has a Unique Multiplicative Inverse For every real number a ≠ 0, there exists a unique real number b such that ab = 1.
Proof Existence: let a ≠ 0. Take b = 1/a. Then ab = a(1/a) = 1, so b satisfies the required equation — a witness has been produced.
Uniqueness: suppose b and c are both real numbers with ab = 1 and ac = 1. Then b = b · 1 = b(ac) = (ba)c = 1 · c = c, using ac = 1 to substitute, associativity to regroup, and ba = ab = 1 from the hypothesis on b. So b = c — any two numbers satisfying the equation must in fact be the same number.
Both halves hold, so a unique multiplicative inverse exists. ∎
Why the uniqueness argument never had to say what b or c actually are Notice the uniqueness half never used b = 1/a — it worked purely from the two hypotheses ab = 1 and ac = 1, treating b and c as unspecified. This is deliberate and typical: a uniqueness argument shows that any two objects satisfying the property must coincide, without needing to already know what that common value is. It's the mirror image of Lesson 6's arbitrary-element technique, now applied to two arbitrary witnesses instead of one arbitrary set element.
Try It — Existence, Uniqueness, Both, or Neither?

Classify each claim by what it actually asserts.

"There is a prime number greater than 100."

"The equation x² = 4 has at most one real solution in the interval [0, ∞)."

"Every nonzero real number has a unique multiplicative inverse."

"For every real number x, x² ≥ 0."

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a uniqueness claim, on its own, actually assert?

Uniqueness alone only rules out two-or-more — it says nothing about whether even one exists, which is exactly why ∃! needs both halves proved.

2. In the multiplicative inverse proof's uniqueness half, what is assumed at the start?

Uniqueness proofs start from two arbitrary witnesses and derive that they must be equal — assuming they're already equal would prove nothing.

3. Fill in the blank: the symbol ∃! is read "___."

∃! combines existence and uniqueness into a single symbol, read "there exists a unique" (or "exists uniquely").

4. Which claim asserts existence but makes no uniqueness claim at all?

A plain existence statement like this one produces a witness (x = 7) and stops — it neither claims nor rules out other solutions.

5. Fill in the blank: a uniqueness proof typically assumes two objects, b and c, both satisfy the property, then derives b = ___.

Showing b = c is exactly what "any two witnesses must coincide" means — the entire content of a uniqueness argument.
