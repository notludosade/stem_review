1. What is the truth value of "If 6 is odd, then 9 is prime," and why?

P → Q is false only when P is true and Q is false. Here P ("6 is odd") is false, so the conditional is vacuously true.

2. Fill in the blank: proving ∃x ∈ D, P(x) is false requires showing P(x) fails for ___ x in D.

¬(∃x, P(x)) ≡ ∀x, ¬P(x) — disproving an existence claim requires ruling out the entire domain.

3. By mathematical convention, what does "if" mean when it appears in a definition?

Definitions are read as biconditionals by convention — licensing both "prove it satisfies the definition" and "prove it doesn't" as valid goals.

4. A direct proof of "if x is a multiple of 8, then x is a multiple of 4" should begin by assuming what?

A direct proof of P → Q assumes only the hypothesis P and reasons forward toward the conclusion.

5. Fill in the blank: for the claim 5 ∣ 70, the witness k satisfying 70 = 5k is k = ___.

5 × 14 = 70, so k = 14 is the required witness.

6. When proving A ⊆ C from A ⊆ B and B ⊆ C, why must x be an arbitrary element of A rather than one specific chosen element?

A universal claim needs an argument valid for every element, which is exactly what an arbitrary, property-free element provides.

7. What is the contrapositive of "If m is a multiple of 10, then m is even"?

Negate and swap both parts: ¬(m even) → ¬(m is a multiple of 10), i.e., m is odd → m is not a multiple of 10.

8. How does proof by contrapositive differ from proof by contradiction?

Contrapositive proof has exactly one assumption (¬Q) and ends with ¬P through ordinary forward reasoning; contradiction assumes both P and ¬Q at once and ends by deriving something false.

9. Fill in the blank: the √2 proof assumes p/q is written in ___, meaning p and q share no common factor greater than 1.

Lowest terms is the assumption later violated when both p and q turn out even, producing the contradiction.

10. In Euclid's proof, why can no prime on an assumed "complete" list divide N = p₁p₂⋯pₙ + 1?

The "+1" guarantees remainder 1 against every listed prime, forcing N's own prime factor to be new.

11. In the inductive step of a weak induction proof, what exactly is being proved?

The inductive step proves a conditional, using P(k) only as an assumption (the inductive hypothesis), not an independently established fact.

12. Why must the base case for "n! > 2ⁿ" be set at n = 4 rather than n = 1?

Induction can only prove what's actually true — since the inequality fails at n = 1, 2, 3, the base case must start at n = 4, the first true case.

13. Fill in the blank: using strong induction to show 35 is a product of primes, 35 = 5 × ___, and since both factors are prime, the proof is complete.

35 = 5 × 7, both prime, applying the strong inductive hypothesis to each factor rather than to the immediately preceding case.

14. A student splits a proof about real numbers x into "Case 1: x > 0" and "Case 2: x < 0." What is wrong with this split?

x > 0 and x < 0 together miss x = 0 entirely — a genuine gap that exhaustiveness is meant to rule out.

15. What does a uniqueness claim, on its own, actually assert?

Uniqueness alone only rules out two-or-more — it says nothing about whether even one exists, which is why ∃! needs both halves proved.

16. Claim: "If xy is irrational, then x is irrational or y is irrational." Which technique fits best, and why?

The hypothesis "xy is irrational" gives little to compute with directly, while the contrapositive's hypothesis — both rational — unpacks immediately into fractions whose product is easy to compute.
