Reading and Writing Mathematical Definitions

A definition isn't background reading — it's an exact specification of what you're allowed to assume and what you're required to produce. Misreading one is the single most common reason a proof attempt goes nowhere.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Lessons 1 and 2 gave you the vocabulary — statements, connectives, quantifiers. This lesson teaches you to point that vocabulary at a definition and extract, mechanically, exactly what a proof involving that definition has to do. Get this step wrong and no amount of algebraic skill downstream will save the proof; get it right and the rest of the proof is often just following the definition's own instructions.

Convention — "If" in a Definition Means "If and Only If" In ordinary theorems, "if P then Q" means exactly P → Q, nothing more. But by long-standing mathematical convention, when a sentence defines a new term — "A number n is called even if there exists an integer k with n = 2k" — the "if" is understood to mean if and only if, even though the word "iff" is never written. The definition is simultaneously handing you both directions: anything satisfying the condition gets the name, and anything with the name satisfies the condition.
Why this convention matters for proofs Because a definition is secretly a biconditional, it gives you two proof obligations for free, in opposite directions. To show some particular number is even, you must produce the witness the definition demands. To show a number is not even, you must show no such witness can exist — using the negated, quantified form from Lesson 2. Misreading a definition as a one-directional "if" silently throws away half of what it actually promises.

Unpacking a definition into its logical skeleton

Every mathematical definition worth proving things about has the same underlying shape: it names a quantifier structure, and it names a condition. Once you isolate both pieces, "prove X satisfies the definition" tells you exactly what you're on the hook to produce.

Definition — Divides Let a, b ∈ ℤ. We say a divides b, written a ∣ b, if there exists an integer k such that b = ak.
Unpacking it Strip away the sentence and what's left is a single existential claim: ∃k ∈ ℤ, b = ak. That tells you precisely two things at once. To prove a ∣ b, an existential claim, you must exhibit a specific integer k and verify b = ak — nothing more is required, and nothing less will do. To disprove a ∣ b, you're disproving an ∃ claim, which by Lesson 2's negation rule becomes a ∀ claim: you must show that b ≠ ak for every integer k — usually by contradiction or contrapositive (Unit 3), since checking infinitely many k directly isn't an option.
Worked example Claim: 4 ∣ 12. Proof: take k = 3. Then 4k = 4(3) = 12, so 12 = 4k as required, and 4 ∣ 12. ∎ Notice the entire proof is just "produce the witness the definition asked for, and check it." No cleverness was needed once the definition was correctly unpacked — only that step is easy to skip.
Try It — Parse a Definition, One Piece at a Time

Definition: "A real number x is rational if there exist integers a and b, with b ≠ 0, such that x = a/b." Click "Reveal next" to unpack it.

  1. Step 1 — Find the quantifier. "There exist integers a and b" is ∃a ∈ ℤ, ∃b ∈ ℤ — an existential claim about a pair of witnesses, not a universal one.
  2. Step 2 — Find the side condition. "with b ≠ 0" restricts which witnesses are allowed to count — a witness pair with b = 0 doesn't satisfy the definition, even if some other part matches.
  3. Step 3 — Find the actual condition being claimed. "x = a/b" is the equation the witnesses must make true — this is the part you'll verify once a and b are chosen.
  4. Step 4 — What a proof that some specific x is rational must do. Exhibit actual integers a and b (with b ≠ 0) and check x = a/b — exactly as with a ∣ b above, an existential definition is proved by production, not by abstract argument.
  5. Step 5 — What a proof that some specific x is irrational must do. Show no pair of integers a, b (b ≠ 0) can satisfy x = a/b — a universal claim over infinitely many candidate pairs, which is exactly why Lesson 9's proof that √2 is irrational needs contradiction rather than direct search.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. By mathematical convention, what does "if" mean when it appears in a definition?

Definitions are read as biconditionals by long-standing convention, which is exactly what licenses both "prove it satisfies the definition" and "prove it doesn't" as valid proof goals.

2. To prove 7 ∣ 91 directly from the definition of divides, what is the minimum required?

a ∣ b unpacks to an existential claim, so producing one witness (k = 13, since 7·13 = 91) and verifying it is the entire proof.

3. What is the correctly negated, quantified form of "a does not divide b"?

Negating ∃k, b = ak by Lesson 2's rule gives ∀k, b ≠ ak — every integer k fails to work, not just some.

4. Fill in the blank: mathematicians treat "if" inside a definition as meaning ___, even though the shorter word is what actually gets written.

"If and only if" (equivalently, "iff" or "biconditional") — the convention this whole lesson is built around.

5. Fill in the blank: the specific object you produce and check to prove an existential (∃) definitional claim is called a ___.

A witness — like k = 3 for 4 ∣ 12, or the pair (a, b) for a rational number. Producing one is the entire content of proving an ∃ claim.
