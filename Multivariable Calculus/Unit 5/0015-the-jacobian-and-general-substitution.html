The Jacobian and General Substitution

Polar's extra r and spherical's extra ρ² sin φ weren't two unrelated tricks — they're both the same single formula, evaluated for two particular coordinate changes.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

1. One formula behind every coordinate change

Definition — The Jacobian For a transformation x = x(u,v), y = y(u,v), the Jacobian is J = ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v) = (∂x/∂u)(∂y/∂v) − (∂x/∂v)(∂y/∂u) — the determinant of the matrix of first partial derivatives. |J| measures how much a tiny patch of area near (u,v) is stretched or shrunk once mapped into (x,y)-space.
Method — General Substitution For a region R in (x,y) that's the image of a region S in (u,v) under a one-to-one transformation T: ∬R f(x,y) dx dy = ∬S f(x(u,v), y(u,v)) · |J| du dv. This is the direct two-variable generalization of single-variable u-substitution's du = g′(x)dx — the Jacobian is what g′(x) becomes once there are two variables to track.
Worked Example — Recovering Polar Coordinates For x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ: ∂x/∂r = cos θ, ∂x/∂θ = −r sin θ, ∂y/∂r = sin θ, ∂y/∂θ = r cos θ. So J = (cos θ)(r cos θ) − (−r sin θ)(sin θ) = r cos²θ + r sin²θ = r. |J| = r exactly recovers Lesson 13's dA = r dr dθ — polar coordinates were a special case of this formula all along.
Why This Matters Any awkward region can potentially be straightened into a simple rectangle by choosing the right (u,v), at the one-time cost of computing a Jacobian — the same trade every coordinate change in this unit has been making. Spherical coordinates' ρ² sin φ factor from Lesson 14 is this exact same determinant, just built from three variables instead of two.
