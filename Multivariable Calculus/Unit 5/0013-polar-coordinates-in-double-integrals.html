Polar Coordinates in Double Integrals

A circular region forced into x and y coordinates fights you the whole way. Switch to r and θ, and the bounds — and often the integral itself — become dramatically simpler.

1. The area element picks up an extra factor of r

Definition — Polar Area Element Converting x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, the flat area element transforms as dA = dx dy = r dr dθ — not just dr dθ. A thin "polar rectangle" spanning Δr in radius and Δθ in angle at radius r has arc length r·Δθ (not Δθ alone), so its area is approximately (Δr)(r·Δθ) = r Δr Δθ. That extra factor of r is not optional — dropping it is the single most common polar-integral mistake.
Method — Converting a Double Integral to Polar ∬R f(x,y) dA = ∬R f(r cos θ, r sin θ) · r dr dθ, with R's bounds rewritten in terms of r and θ. This pays off whenever R is a disk, annulus, or wedge, or when the integrand itself involves x² + y² = r² — both situations where Cartesian bounds or algebra get painful fast.
Worked Example ∬D e−(x²+y²) dA over the disk x² + y² ≤ 4 (radius 2) — an integral with no elementary Cartesian antiderivative at all. In polar: ∫02π ∫02 e−r²·r dr dθ. Substituting u = r², du = 2r dr: ∫02 e−r² r dr = (1/2)(1 − e−4). Then the outer integral just multiplies by 2π: π(1 − e−4) ≈ 3.084.
Why This Matters This exact substitution is the standard trick behind evaluating ∫e−x²dx (the Gaussian integral), which underlies the normal distribution in statistics. It's also the direct preview of Lesson 15's general rule: swapping coordinate systems always costs an extra scaling factor in the area (or volume) element, and the Jacobian is what computes it in general.
