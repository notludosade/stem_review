Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

Polar coordinates fit round regions in the plane. Space offers two different kinds of roundness — around an axis, or around a point — and each gets its own coordinate system built to match.

1. Two ways to be round in three dimensions

Definition — Cylindrical Coordinates Cylindrical coordinates (r, θ, z) keep z as-is and replace x, y with polar coordinates: x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, z = z. The volume element picks up the same extra factor as plane polar coordinates: dV = r dr dθ dz. Ideal for solids built around the z-axis — cylinders, cones, and paraboloids of revolution.
Definition — Spherical Coordinates Spherical coordinates (ρ, θ, φ) measure ρ = distance from the origin, θ = the same azimuthal angle as polar/cylindrical, and φ = angle down from the positive z-axis (0 ≤ φ ≤ π). Conversion: x = ρ sin φ cos θ, y = ρ sin φ sin θ, z = ρ cos φ, and the volume element is dV = ρ² sin φ dρ dθ dφ. Ideal for solids built around a single point — balls, spherical shells, and cones with their vertex at the origin.
Worked Example Volume of a solid ball of radius a, in spherical coordinates: V = ∭ ρ² sin φ dρ dθ dφ, with ρ: 0→a, φ: 0→π, θ: 0→2π. Since everything separates: V = (∫02π dθ)(∫0π sin φ dφ)(∫0a ρ² dρ) = (2π)(2)(a³/3) = (4/3)πa³ — recovering the familiar sphere volume formula directly from the coordinate system built for it.
Why This Matters Attempting the same ball's volume in plain (x,y,z) coordinates means nested square roots at every bound. Matching the coordinate system to the solid's actual symmetry is what makes triple integrals over spheres, cones, and cylinders tractable by hand — and it's the exact reasoning gravitational and electric field problems in physics lean on constantly.
