Partial Derivatives and the Gradient

A surface has infinitely many directions to differentiate in at once. Start with the two simplest — straight along x, straight along y — then bundle both into a single vector.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

1. Differentiating one variable at a time

Definition — Partial Derivative The partial derivative of f(x, y) with respect to x is ∂f/∂x = limh→0 [f(x+h, y) − f(x, y)] / h — the ordinary derivative definition, with y frozen as a constant. ∂f/∂y is defined the same way, freezing x instead. In practice: to compute ∂f/∂x, differentiate normally while treating every y as a constant; to compute ∂f/∂y, treat every x as a constant.
Method — The Gradient Vector The gradient ∇f = ⟨∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y⟩ (or ⟨fx, fy, fz⟩ in three variables) packages both partial derivatives into one vector. At any point, ∇f points in the direction of steepest ascent of the surface and is always perpendicular to the level curve through that point. Second partial derivatives — fxx, fyy, and the mixed partials fxy, fyx — come from differentiating a first partial again. Clairaut's theorem guarantees fxy = fyx whenever both mixed partials exist and are continuous near the point — a condition true of essentially every function this course uses, but not a free guarantee for every conceivable function.
Worked Example f(x, y) = x²y + y³. Treating y as constant: fx = 2xy. Treating x as constant: fy = x² + 3y². At (1, 2): fx(1,2) = 2(1)(2) = 4, fy(1,2) = 1² + 3(2²) = 1 + 12 = 13, so ∇f(1,2) = ⟨4, 13⟩. Checking mixed partials: fxy = ∂/∂y(2xy) = 2x, and fyx = ∂/∂x(x² + 3y²) = 2x — equal, as Clairaut's theorem predicts for this smooth polynomial.
Why This Matters A single-variable slope only had two directions to point: up or down the x-axis. On a surface, "which way is steepest" is a genuine question with a specific answer — and the gradient is that answer, in vector form. Every later tool in this unit and the next (directional derivatives, tangent planes, the Second Derivative Test, Lagrange multipliers) is built directly on top of ∇f and its second-derivative cousins.
