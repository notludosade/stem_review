The Dot Product and Angles Between Vectors

The first way to multiply two vectors together — it throws away direction entirely and hands back a single number, but that number turns out to measure the angle between them.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

1. Multiplying two vectors into one number

Definition — Dot Product For u = ⟨u₁, u₂, u₃⟩ and v = ⟨v₁, v₂, v₃⟩, the dot product is u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ + u₃v₃ — multiply matching components and add. The result is a scalar (an ordinary number), not a vector. In 2D, drop the third term: u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂.
Method — The Angle Formula The dot product also equals u · v = |u||v|cos θ, where θ is the angle between u and v. Solving for θ gives cos θ = (u · v) / (|u||v|). Two nonzero vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular) exactly when u · v = 0, since cos θ = 0 there.
Worked Example u = ⟨3, 4⟩, v = ⟨4, −3⟩: u · v = (3)(4) + (4)(−3) = 12 − 12 = 0 — orthogonal, no angle computation needed. For u = ⟨1, 0⟩, v = ⟨1, 1⟩: u · v = 1, |u| = 1, |v| = √2, so cos θ = 1/√2 and θ = 45°.
Why This Matters Work done by a constant force F moving an object along displacement d is W = F · d — the dot product is what correctly discards the part of the force that doesn't act along the motion. Later units reuse this exact idea: a directional derivative is a dot product, and line integrals of vector fields are built from dot products taken along a path.

1. In component form, u · v for u = ⟨u₁, u₂⟩ and v = ⟨v₁, v₂⟩ equals:

Multiply matching components and add — the result is a single scalar, not a vector, which is exactly what rules out the second choice.

2. For u = ⟨3, 4⟩ and v = ⟨4, -3⟩, u · v = ___

u · v = (3)(4) + (4)(-3) = 12 − 12 = 0.

3. If u · v = 0 for two nonzero vectors, what does that mean geometrically?

u · v = |u||v|cos θ, and for nonzero vectors this is zero exactly when cos θ = 0, i.e. θ = 90°.

4. Solving u · v = |u||v|cos θ for the angle θ gives:

Divide both sides by |u||v| to isolate cos θ, then apply cos⁻¹ to both sides.
