Directional Derivatives

Partial derivatives only ever looked straight along the x-axis or the y-axis. A directional derivative asks the same "how fast is f changing" question in literally any direction at all.

← Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

1. Choosing your own direction

Definition — Directional Derivative For a unit vector u = ⟨a, b⟩ (|u| = 1), the directional derivative of f at (x, y) in the direction of u is Duf(x, y) = ∇f(x, y) · u. This is exactly the dz/dt from Lesson 6's Chain Rule, specialized to a straight-line path in direction u — and fx and fy themselves are just the special cases u = ⟨1,0⟩ and u = ⟨0,1⟩.
Method — Finding the Steepest Direction Because Duf = ∇f · u = |∇f||u|cos θ = |∇f|cos θ (since |u| = 1), Duf is largest when u points the same way as ∇f (θ = 0, cos θ = 1), giving a maximum rate of increase of exactly |∇f|. It is smallest — most negative — when u points opposite to ∇f, and exactly 0 when u is perpendicular to ∇f, i.e. tangent to the level curve.
Worked Example f(x, y) = x² + y² at (1, 2): ∇f(1,2) = ⟨2, 4⟩. In the direction u = ⟨3/5, 4/5⟩ (a unit vector, since (3/5)² + (4/5)² = 1): Duf = (2)(3/5) + (4)(4/5) = 6/5 + 16/5 = 22/5 = 4.4. The maximum possible rate of increase at (1,2), in any direction, is |∇f(1,2)| = √(4+16) = √20 = 2√5 ≈ 4.47 — achieved only in the direction of ∇f itself, ⟨2,4⟩/√20.
Why This Matters A hiker standing on a hillside doesn't only care about the steepness due east or due north — they care about the steepness in whichever direction they're actually walking. Duf answers exactly that, and its maximum, |∇f|, is the reason the gradient is called the direction of steepest ascent in the first place, not just an arbitrary vector built from two partials.

1. For a unit vector u, the directional derivative Duf equals:

The directional derivative is a dot product of the gradient with the (unit) direction vector — a scalar rate of change, not a vector.

2. For f(x,y) = x² + y² at (1,2), the directional derivative in the direction u = ⟨3/5, 4/5⟩ is ___

∇f(1,2) = ⟨2,4⟩, so D_u f = 2(3/5) + 4(4/5) = 6/5 + 16/5 = 22/5 = 4.4.

3. The direction of maximum increase of f at a point is:

D_u f = |∇f|cos θ is maximized when θ = 0 — u pointing the same direction as ∇f.

4. The maximum possible value of Duf at a point, over every possible unit direction u, is:

D_u f = |∇f|cos θ tops out at |∇f| when cos θ = 1 — the gradient's own magnitude, not a fixed constant like 1.

Duf only has a well-defined meaning when u is a unit vector.
