Tangent Planes and Linear Approximation

A single-variable tangent line was the best flat stand-in for a curve near one point. A surface's best flat stand-in near a point is an entire plane — built from exactly the two partial derivatives Lesson 5 already computed.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

1. The flat surface that best fits at a point

Definition — Tangent Plane If f has continuous partial derivatives at (a, b), the tangent plane to z = f(x, y) at (a, b) is z = f(a,b) + fx(a,b)(x − a) + fy(a,b)(y − b). This is the direct extension of the single-variable tangent line L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) — same structure, one more partial derivative term. Its normal vector is n = ⟨fx(a,b), fy(a,b), −1⟩, built exactly the way Unit 1 built any plane's normal.
Method — Linear Approximation The right-hand side of the tangent plane equation, renamed L(x,y) = f(a,b) + fx(a,b)(x−a) + fy(a,b)(y−b), is the linearization of f at (a,b) — a stand-in for f(x,y) that's cheap to evaluate and accurate for (x,y) close to (a,b). Just like the single-variable case, the approximation degrades the farther (x,y) strays from (a,b).
Worked Example f(x,y) = x² + y² at (a,b) = (1,2): f(1,2) = 5, fx(1,2) = 2, fy(1,2) = 4. Tangent plane: z = 5 + 2(x−1) + 4(y−2). Approximating f(1.1, 1.9): L(1.1,1.9) = 5 + 2(0.1) + 4(−0.1) = 5 + 0.2 − 0.4 = 4.8. The actual value is f(1.1,1.9) = 1.21 + 3.61 = 4.82 — an error of only 0.02, for a displacement of 0.1 in each direction.
Why This Matters Nearly every numerical method for multivariable functions — Newton's method in several variables, error propagation in measurements, the first step of Lagrange multipliers' reasoning in Unit 8 — leans on treating a smooth surface as flat over a small enough patch. The tangent plane is the precise statement of "smooth enough surfaces look flat up close."

Check your recall

1. The tangent plane to z = f(x,y) at (a,b) is:

The tangent plane is the direct two-variable extension of the tangent line L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x−a), with one linear term per partial derivative.

2. For f(x,y) = x² + y², the linear approximation L(1.1, 1.9) at (a,b) = (1,2) equals ___

L(1.1,1.9) = 5 + 2(0.1) + 4(-0.1) = 5 + 0.2 - 0.4 = 4.8.

3. Linear approximation is most accurate when (x,y) is:

Just like a single-variable tangent line, the tangent plane only stays close to the true surface near the point of tangency — accuracy degrades with distance.

4. A normal vector to the tangent plane of z = f(x,y) at (a,b) is:

Writing the plane as f_x(x-a) + f_y(y-b) - (z - f(a,b)) = 0 exposes its normal directly as ⟨f_x, f_y, -1⟩ (any nonzero scalar multiple, including ⟨-f_x,-f_y,1⟩, works equally well).

The tangent plane's normal vector ⟨f_x, f_y, −1⟩ is built from the same partial derivatives as the gradient ∇f = ⟨f_x, f_y⟩, just with an extra −1 tacked on. Ask your teacher how that third component connects to the fact that ∇f lives in the xy-plane while the tangent plane's normal has to point out of it.
