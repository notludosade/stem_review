Triple Integrals

One more variable, one more layer of integration — a solid region in space instead of a flat one, and mass or volume instead of area or volume-under-a-surface.

1. Integrating over a solid region

Definition — Triple Integral For f(x,y,z) defined on a solid region E in space, the triple integral ∭E f(x,y,z) dV is the limit of a triple Riemann sum Σ f(xi*, yj*, zk*)ΔV as the partition of E into small boxes gets infinitely fine. When f = 1 everywhere, ∭E 1 dV is exactly the volume of E. When f is a density function, ∭E f dV gives E's total mass.
Method — Iterated Triple Integrals Over a box B = [a,b] × [c,d] × [r,s], Fubini's theorem extends directly: ∭B f dV = ∫ab ∫cd ∫rs f dz dy dx, in any of the six possible orders, for continuous f. For a general solid E — say, bounded below by z = 0 and above by z = g(x,y), over a shadow region D in the xy-plane — the innermost integral runs 0 ≤ z ≤ g(x,y), and the remaining double integral over D (Lesson 11's machinery) finishes the job.
Worked Example f(x,y,z) = xyz over the box [0,1] × [0,2] × [0,3]. Since the integrand and bounds separate completely: ∭ xyz dV = (∫01 x dx)(∫02 y dy)(∫03 z dz) = (1/2)(2)(9/2) = 4.5. Separating into a product of three single integrals only works when both the integrand and the region factor this cleanly — most triple integrals need the full nested setup instead.
Why This Matters Volume, mass, center of mass, and moments of inertia for a genuinely three-dimensional object are all triple integrals. Unit 5 makes many of them dramatically easier to set up by trading (x,y,z) for coordinates that match the solid's natural symmetry, and Unit 7's Divergence Theorem later turns a flux integral over a closed surface into a triple integral over the volume it encloses.
