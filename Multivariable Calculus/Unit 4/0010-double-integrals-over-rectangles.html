Double Integrals Over Rectangles

A single integral summed infinitely many thin rectangles into an area. A double integral sums infinitely many thin boxes into a volume — starting with the simplest possible base shape, a rectangle.

← Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

1. Summing volume instead of area

Definition — Double Integral Over a Rectangle For f(x,y) defined on a rectangle R = [a,b] × [c,d], partition R into a grid of small rectangles of area ΔA, pick a sample point in each, and form the Riemann sum Σ f(xi*, yj*)ΔA. As the grid gets infinitely fine, this sum approaches the double integral ∬R f(x,y) dA. When f ≥ 0 on R, ∬R f dA equals the volume between the surface z = f(x,y) and the rectangle R.
Method — Fubini's Theorem and Iterated Integrals If f is continuous on R = [a,b] × [c,d], the double integral can be computed as an iterated integral in either order: ∬R f dA = ∫ab ∫cd f(x,y) dy dx = ∫cd ∫ab f(x,y) dx dy. The inner integral treats the outer variable as a constant — exactly a partial-derivative-style freeze, run in reverse.
Worked Example f(x,y) = xy over R = [0,2] × [0,3]. Integrating y first: ∫02 ∫03 xy dy dx = ∫02 x·[y²/2]03 dx = ∫02 (9x/2) dx = (9/2)·[x²/2]02 = (9/2)(2) = 9. Swapping the order: ∫03 ∫02 xy dx dy = ∫03 y·2 dy = 2·[y²/2]03 = 2(4.5) = 9 — the same answer, confirming Fubini's theorem.
Why This Matters Every technique from AP Calculus BC's Unit 6 (Riemann sums, the definite integral, FTC) reappears here one dimension up. Fubini's theorem is what makes double integrals actually computable by hand: instead of inventing new machinery, it reduces a double integral to two ordinary single-variable integrals, done back to back.
