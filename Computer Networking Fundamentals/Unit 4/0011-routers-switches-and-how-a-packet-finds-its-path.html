Routers, Switches, and How a Packet Finds Its Path

Nobody plans a packet's entire route in advance. Each device it passes through makes its own local decision about where to send it next — and that's enough to get it anywhere.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Units 2 and 3 built the vocabulary; this lesson puts it to work. A packet, addressed with an IP address, leaves your device with no pre-planned map of the entire route to its destination. Instead, it's handed off from device to device, and each device along the way makes an independent, local decision: "given this destination address, which direction should I send this next?" Do that correctly enough times in a row, and the packet arrives — potentially having crossed a dozen networks that never coordinated a route in advance.

Switches: forwarding within one network

Definition A switch operates at the Link layer, connecting multiple devices within the same local network and forwarding frames between them based on MAC addresses. A switch learns which MAC address lives on which of its physical ports by watching traffic, and forwards each frame only out the specific port where its destination actually lives — instead of blasting every frame out every port, which is how older, simpler hardware (a hub) used to work.

Routers: forwarding between networks

Definition A router operates at the Internet layer, connecting separate networks together and forwarding packets between them based on IP addresses. Every router maintains a routing table — a list of known destination networks and which direction ("next hop") to send a packet to reach each one. When a packet arrives, the router checks its destination IP address against its routing table, finds the best matching entry, and forwards the packet out toward that next hop — it does not need to know the entire remaining path, only the single next step.
Why "know only the next step" is enough to reach anywhere This might sound too simple to work reliably, but it's exactly the same idea as highway signage: a sign doesn't tell you the entire turn-by-turn route to a city 2,000 miles away — it just tells you which direction to go right now to eventually get closer. Follow enough correctly-placed signs in a row, each only responsible for one local decision, and you arrive, even though no single sign (or router) ever had the whole picture. Routers exchange information with their neighboring routers about which networks they can reach, which is how each router's local routing table stays accurate without any router needing global knowledge of the internet's entire topology.
Definition A default gateway is the specific router a device sends a packet to whenever the destination isn't on the device's own local network — it's the "exit door" out of your local network and onto the wider path toward everywhere else. Your home router is almost always your own devices' default gateway.

TTL: a safety net against infinite loops

Definition TTL (Time to Live) is a counter in every IP packet's header, set by the sender and decreased by exactly 1 at every router it passes through. If a router ever decreases a packet's TTL to 0, it discards the packet instead of forwarding it, and sends a message back to the sender reporting what happened. TTL exists purely as a safety net: if a routing misconfiguration ever created a loop (packet bounces between two routers forever), TTL guarantees that packet gets discarded eventually instead of circling the internet forever, consuming capacity endlessly.
Worked example A packet leaves your laptop with TTL = 64. It passes through your home router (TTL becomes 63), your ISP's first router (62), a backbone router (61), and two more routers before reaching the destination server (58). If, instead, a misconfiguration caused the packet to bounce between two routers indefinitely, its TTL would hit 0 after 64 hops of bouncing and get discarded — rather than looping forever.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What's the key difference between what a switch and a router forward on, and where?

Switches operate at the Link layer within a network; routers operate at the Internet layer, connecting separate networks together.

2. Why can a router get a packet to its destination without knowing the entire remaining route in advance?

Local, next-hop-only decisions, repeated correctly at each hop, are sufficient — no router needs the full path.

3. What is TTL protecting against?

TTL is a safety net against infinite loops, not a security or corruption-detection mechanism.

4. Fill in the blank: the specific router a device sends a packet to whenever the destination isn't on its own local network is called the ___.

The default gateway is the exit point out of a local network toward everywhere else.
