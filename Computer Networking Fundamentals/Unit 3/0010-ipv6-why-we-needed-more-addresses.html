IPv6 — Why We Needed More Addresses

32 bits sounded like plenty of addresses in the 1980s. It wasn't, and the fix required starting over with a completely different-sized address rather than patching the old one.

← Lesson 9 Unit 4, Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 8 mentioned IPv4 has been around since the 1980s. It also has a hard ceiling: a 32-bit address can represent at most 2³² addresses — about 4.3 billion. That sounded effectively unlimited when IPv4 was designed, decades before phones, smart thermostats, and billions of new internet users existed. It isn't unlimited anymore, and this lesson is both why that ceiling became a real problem and the address system built to blow past it entirely.

Address exhaustion

Definition Address exhaustion is what happens when the pool of available IPv4 addresses to assign runs out. It's not a hypothetical — the major regional registries responsible for allocating new public IPv4 address blocks (like ARIN in North America and RIPE NCC in Europe) have already exhausted their main pools, forcing them to rely on smaller reclaimed blocks and increasingly aggressive address-sharing techniques (Lesson 12's NAT is one of the biggest reasons IPv4 has lasted as long as it has despite this).
Why 4.3 billion addresses wasn't actually enough 4.3 billion sounds huge until you account for how addresses actually get consumed: every phone, laptop, smart TV, thermostat, and industrial sensor connected to the internet typically wants its own address (or at least a share of one via NAT); address blocks get allocated in large, sometimes inefficiently-sized chunks to organizations and countries, not doled out one at a time with perfect efficiency; and the number of connected devices has grown from a research-lab curiosity in the 1980s to tens of billions of devices worldwide today. NAT (Lesson 12) bought IPv4 a lot of extra runway by letting many private devices share one public address, but it's a workaround, not a permanent fix — it adds real complexity and breaks some kinds of direct device-to-device connections that a truly unique address for every device would support natively.

IPv6: the same idea, a vastly bigger address

Definition IPv6 (Internet Protocol version 6) is the successor address system, using 128-bit addresses instead of IPv4's 32 bits — a jump from about 4.3 billion possible addresses to roughly 340 undecillion (that's 3.4 × 10³⁸, a number so large it's effectively meant to never run out, even accounting for inefficient allocation). Rather than IPv4's four dotted-decimal octets, an IPv6 address is written as eight groups of four hexadecimal digits, separated by colons — like 2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334. Each of those eight groups is sometimes called a hextet (16 bits each, mirroring what an octet is to IPv4).
Shortening an IPv6 address IPv6 addresses are long enough that two shorthand rules are standard: leading zeros within a group can be dropped (0db8 becomes db8), and exactly one run of consecutive all-zero groups can be replaced with a double colon :: (used at most once per address, so it stays unambiguous). Applying both to the address above: 2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334 shortens to 2001:db8:85a3::8a2e:370:7334 — noticeably more compact, and the form you'll actually see in practice far more often than the fully written-out version.

Why IPv6 needed a bigger address, not just a bigger IPv4

It's a fair question: why not just extend IPv4 from 32 bits to, say, 64 bits, instead of designing an entirely new protocol? The honest answer is that changing the address size at all breaks compatibility with every existing device and every piece of network hardware built around a fixed 32-bit address field — there's no way to patch the number of bits in an address without it being effectively a new protocol regardless. Given that a new protocol was unavoidable either way, IPv6's designers went big enough (128 bits) that address exhaustion realistically never becomes a problem again, rather than risking having to repeat the same overhaul a second time down the road.

Why IPv4 hasn't simply disappeared yet IPv6 has existed as a standard since the late 1990s, but IPv4 still carries a large share of internet traffic today, because the two protocols aren't directly compatible — a pure-IPv4 device can't natively talk to a pure-IPv6 address — and upgrading the entire world's networking hardware and software simultaneously was never realistic. Most modern networks run both protocols side by side (called dual-stack), and the two only fully coexist through years of gradual, overlapping transition rather than a single cutover date.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Roughly how many addresses can IPv4's 32-bit address space hold?

32 bits caps IPv4 at 2^32, roughly 4.3 billion addresses — 340 undecillion is IPv6's vastly larger 128-bit space.

2. Why did 4.3 billion addresses turn out not to be enough?

Explosive device growth plus imperfect allocation efficiency, not any single cause, drove address exhaustion.

3. Why couldn't engineers just extend IPv4 to use more bits instead of designing an entirely new protocol (IPv6)?

Any change to the address size requires new, incompatible handling everywhere — so IPv6's designers went large enough to avoid needing to repeat the overhaul.

4. Fill in the blank: each of the eight 16-bit groups in an IPv6 address, separated by colons, is sometimes called a ___.

A hextet is IPv6's equivalent of IPv4's octet — a named chunk of the full address.
