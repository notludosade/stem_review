UDP — Fast, Unreliable, and Why That's Sometimes the Point

TCP's reliability isn't free — it costs a handshake, sequence tracking, and retransmission delay. UDP skips all of it, and for some applications, that's exactly the correct trade.

← Lesson 15 Unit 6, Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

UDP (User Datagram Protocol) is the internet's other Transport-layer protocol, and it's built on an almost aggressively opposite philosophy from TCP: no handshake, no sequence numbers, no acknowledgments, no retransmission. A program using UDP just sends a chunk of data — called a datagram — addressed with a destination IP and port, and hopes it arrives. If it doesn't, UDP itself will never know or tell anyone.

Connectionless, by design

Definition UDP is connectionless: there's no setup process before sending data, no ongoing connection state tracked between the two sides, and no built-in mechanism to detect or recover from a lost datagram. Each datagram is sent completely independently — one datagram's loss has no effect on any other, because UDP never links them together the way TCP's sequence numbers do.
A postcard analogy A TCP connection is like a phone call with a formal check-in ("Can you hear me?" "Yes, go ahead") before either side speaks, and both sides confirming receipt of everything said. A UDP datagram is like dropping a postcard in a mailbox: you write it, send it, and move on — no confirmation it arrived, no resending it if it gets lost, and each postcard is completely independent of any other postcard you've sent.

Why anyone would choose UDP over TCP's guarantees

Why "unreliable" is sometimes exactly what you want Consider a live video call. If one tiny fraction of a second of video data is lost, the right move is to skip it and keep playing the next frame in real time — a viewer barely notices a flicker. TCP's response to lost data is the opposite: pause, wait, retransmit, and deliver everything in perfect order — which for a live call means a noticeable freeze while old data is recovered, even though by the time it arrives it's already useless (nobody wants a half-second-old video frame delivered late; they want the current one, dropped frame or not). UDP's willingness to simply lose data and move on, instead of pausing to guarantee delivery, is a genuine feature for real-time applications where a late, perfectly-reliable packet is worse than a dropped one. Live video and voice calls, competitive online games, and DNS lookups (Lesson 13 — a single small, fast lookup where retrying at the application level is simpler and cheaper than TCP's full connection overhead) all commonly use UDP for exactly this reason.

Worked example: choosing between them Downloading a software update: TCP is the only reasonable choice, because a single corrupted or missing byte could make the whole program fail to run — reliability matters more than raw speed here. A live multiplayer game reporting a player's position 60 times per second: UDP is the better fit, because a single dropped position update is irrelevant once the 61st update arrives a fraction of a second later — waiting to retransmit an old, already-outdated position would only make the game feel laggier.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does "connectionless" mean about UDP?

UDP skips connection setup entirely — there's no handshake and no persistent state between sender and receiver.

2. Why might a live video call prefer UDP's "just drop it" behavior over TCP's guaranteed, in-order delivery?

Waiting for retransmission of outdated real-time data is worse than simply skipping the lost frame and moving on.

3. Downloading a critical software update, where a single corrupted byte could break the program, should use which protocol?

Correctness matters far more than raw speed for a file where any missing or corrupted byte can break the whole thing.

4. Fill in the blank: a single, independently sent chunk of data in UDP, with no relationship tracked to any other chunk, is called a ___.

Each UDP datagram is sent and handled completely independently of any other, unlike TCP's sequenced, connected stream.
