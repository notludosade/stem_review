What Is a Network, Really?

You already know how to write a program that runs on one computer. This course is about the much stranger problem of getting two programs on two different computers, anywhere in the world, to talk to each other reliably.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Every program you've written in Computer Programming 1 lived inside one machine. A function called another function, a loop read a variable, everything happened at the speed of the same processor touching the same memory. Networking blows that assumption up: now the "function call" has to survive being converted into electrical signals, sent across a wire (or through the air) it doesn't control, possibly through a dozen intermediate machines it's never heard of, and reassembled correctly on the other end — sometimes thousands of miles away, sometimes in a fraction of a second, sometimes not at all if something breaks along the way. This course is the vocabulary and mental model for that whole problem, building up from "what even is a network" to writing your own simple networked program by Unit 7.

A network, defined plainly

Definition A network is a collection of devices (called nodes) connected by communication pathways (called links) that allow them to exchange data. That's it — two laptops connected by a single cable already qualify as a network. The internet is a network too; it's just an enormous one, built out of millions of smaller networks connected together.

A node doesn't have to be a computer in the traditional sense. Your phone is a node. A printer is a node. A smart thermostat is a node. A router sitting in a closet, whose entire job is forwarding other devices' data, is a node. What makes something a node is simply that it has an address on the network and can send or receive data over it.

Links carry bits, but bits alone aren't enough

A link — a copper cable, a fiber-optic strand, a radio signal — is just a physical or wireless path capable of carrying a stream of bits (1s and 0s) from one node toward another. But a raw stream of bits, on its own, communicates nothing. If your computer sends 01001000 01101001 down a wire, the receiving computer has no way to know whether that's meant to be text, a number, part of an image, or garbage from a corrupted transmission — unless both sides have agreed in advance on what the bits mean and how they're organized.

Definition A protocol is an agreed-upon set of rules that specifies exactly how data is formatted, sent, and interpreted so that two independently built pieces of software can understand each other. A protocol is to networking what a shared language and a shared set of conversational customs are to two strangers meeting for the first time — without them, sound comes out of one mouth and reaches the other person's ears, but nothing is actually communicated.
Why "just send the bits" isn't a real answer Imagine you and a friend agree to communicate only by flashing a flashlight on and off. A raw stream of flashes is meaningless until you also agree: does a short flash mean a dot and a long flash mean a dash (Morse code)? How do you mark the start and end of a letter? What if a flash is missed — does the receiver ask for it again, or just guess? Every one of those questions has to be answered by a rule both sides commit to before any flashing starts. A network protocol answers the exact same category of questions for bits on a wire instead of light through the air. Nearly everything in this course — addresses, packets, headers, handshakes — is really just "which rules did the engineers agree on to solve this specific piece of the flashlight problem."

You'll meet dozens of specific protocols in this course — IP, TCP, HTTP, DNS, and more — and it's easy to get lost in acronyms. Come back to this lesson's core idea whenever that happens: a protocol is just an agreed rulebook, and every rulebook exists to solve a specific, nameable problem (how do we address a message? how do we know if it arrived? how do we mark where it ends?). Learning networking is mostly learning which problem each protocol was invented to solve.

A network you already use without thinking about it Sending a text message involves a network the whole way: your phone (a node) sends data over a cellular link to a cell tower (another node), which forwards it through your carrier's internal network, out onto the wider internet, through several more intermediate nodes, and finally to your friend's phone (the destination node). Every single hop along that path has to agree on the same protocols, or the message never arrives intact — and it typically takes well under a second.

Why this course starts here instead of with IP addresses

It's tempting to jump straight to "here's an IP address, here's a port number" — those are concrete and countable. But every one of those concepts is downstream of the two ideas in this lesson: nodes need a way to be distinguished from each other (addressing, which Unit 3 covers in depth), and any two nodes that want to exchange meaningful data need to agree on a protocol for doing so (which every later unit is really about, one layer at a time). Get comfortable with "network = nodes + links + protocols" now, and the rest of this course is filling in the details of that one sentence.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the minimum required for two devices to form a network?

Even two laptops connected by a single cable already satisfy the definition — a network is just nodes plus links.

2. Why is a raw stream of bits, by itself, not enough for two computers to communicate?

Meaning comes from a shared rulebook (a protocol) both sides agree to in advance — the bits alone are just electrical or optical signals.

3. In the flashlight analogy, what does agreeing on "a short flash means a dot, a long flash means a dash" represent in real networking?

The flashlight itself is the link; the flashing pattern is the raw signal; the dot/dash rule is the protocol that makes the signal meaningful.

4. Fill in the blank: any addressable device on a network — a laptop, a phone, a printer, a router — is called a ___.

A node just needs an address and the ability to send or receive data — it doesn't have to be a general-purpose computer.
