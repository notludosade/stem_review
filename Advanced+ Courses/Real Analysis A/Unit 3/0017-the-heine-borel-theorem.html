The Heine-Borel Theorem

A second, completely different-looking definition of compactness — proved equivalent to the sequential one from Lesson 16.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 16 defined compactness using sequences. This lesson introduces an entirely different-looking definition, in terms of open covers, and proves the two are equivalent for subsets of ℝ — completing the "closed and bounded" characterization previewed at the end of Lesson 16.

Open covers

Definition — Open Cover An open cover of a set K is a collection of open sets {Oα} whose union contains K. A finite subcover is a finite subcollection of these open sets whose union still contains K.
Example The collection {(−n, n) : n ∈ ℕ} is an open cover of ℝ (every real number lies in some (−n, n)). No finite subcollection of it covers ℝ — any finite subcollection has a largest n, say N, and the finite union is just (−N, N), which misses everything outside it.

The theorem

Theorem — Heine-Borel Theorem A set K ⊆ ℝ is compact (in the sequential sense of Lesson 16) if and only if every open cover of K has a finite subcover.
Proof sketch — finite-subcover property ⇒ closed and bounded Suppose every open cover of K has a finite subcover. Boundedness: {(−n, n)} covers K; a finite subcover exists, and the largest such interval bounds K. Closedness: if x is a limit point of K not in K, the sets On = {y : |y − x| > 1/n} form an open cover of K (every point of K is some positive distance from x, so lands in some On), but no finite subcollection covers K, because x being a limit point means points of K get arbitrarily close to x, closer than any fixed 1/n — contradicting the finite-subcover hypothesis. So K must contain all its limit points, i.e. be closed.
Proof sketch — closed and bounded ⇒ finite-subcover property This direction uses the bisection technique from Bolzano-Weierstrass (Unit 2, Lesson 12) in reverse: assume for contradiction some open cover of a closed, bounded K has no finite subcover. Repeatedly bisect the bounding interval, at each step keeping a half that still cannot be finitely covered (such a half must exist, by the same pigeonhole reasoning as Lesson 12). This produces nested shrinking intervals converging to a single point x, which must lie in K (K is closed), hence is covered by some single open set Oα from the original cover. But Oα, being open, contains an entire interval around x — which eventually swallows one of the shrinking bisected intervals whole, giving a finite (in fact single-set) subcover of that piece, contradicting the assumption that no piece could be finitely covered.
Why two entirely different-looking definitions turn out to coincide The sequential definition (Lesson 16) is about sequences failing to escape a set; the open-cover definition is about a set resisting being covered by infinitely many "loose" open pieces without some finite selection already sufficing. That these say the same thing is a genuinely deep fact — and it's specifically why compactness, not "closed and bounded" directly, is the concept that generalizes to more abstract spaces where "bounded" no longer even makes sense (a fact flagged in this course's MISSION.md as future material, beyond fundamentals).

1. What is a finite subcover of an open cover of K?

A finite subcover must still cover K entirely, but drawn from only finitely many of the (possibly infinitely many) open sets in the cover.

2. Why does {(−n, n) : n ∈ ℕ} have no finite subcover of ℝ?

This is exactly the example showing ℝ, being unbounded, fails the finite-subcover property.

3. In the proof that the finite-subcover property implies closedness, what role does the collection On = {y : |y − x| > 1/n} play?

This cover is specifically engineered so that a missing limit point x would break the finite-subcover property, which is exactly the contradiction the proof needs.

Heine-Borel is the theorem this lesson proves, connecting the sequential and open-cover definitions of compactness.
