Combinations of Continuous Functions

Why "it's a sum of continuous functions" has been a fully legitimate proof step all along.

← Lesson 20 Lesson 22 → Contents Glossary →

Just as Unit 2, Lesson 9 proved algebraic limit laws for sequences once and reused them ever after, this lesson proves the corresponding laws for continuous functions, plus the composition rule that sequences don't have an analogue of.

Algebraic combinations

Theorem If f and g are continuous at a, then f + g, f − g, and fg are continuous at a; and f/g is continuous at a provided g(a) ≠ 0.
Proof sketch Use the sequential characterization (Lesson 20): let xₙ → a. Since f, g are continuous at a, f(xₙ) → f(a) and g(xₙ) → g(a). By the algebraic limit laws for sequences (Unit 2, Lesson 9), (f+g)(xₙ) = f(xₙ) + g(xₙ) → f(a) + g(a) = (f+g)(a), and similarly for the other combinations. So each combination is continuous at a, by the sequential characterization applied in reverse. ∎
Why this proof is almost free Every piece of work was already done in Unit 2 — this proof is really just Lesson 9's algebraic limit laws, viewed through Lesson 20's sequential characterization of continuity as a lens. This is a recurring pattern in analysis: results about functions often reduce to results about sequences that have already been proved.

Composition

Theorem If f is continuous at a, and g is continuous at f(a), then g ∘ f is continuous at a.
Proof Let xₙ → a. Since f is continuous at a, f(xₙ) → f(a). Since g is continuous at f(a), and (f(xₙ)) is a sequence converging to f(a), it follows that g(f(xₙ)) → g(f(a)). So (g∘f)(xₙ) → (g∘f)(a), and by the sequential characterization, g∘f is continuous at a. ∎
Why continuity has a composition rule but sequence limits don't need one Sequences don't compose with each other the way functions do — this rule is genuinely new content, not a restatement of Unit 2. It's also exactly why a chain of nested continuous operations (like sin(x²+1)) can be certified continuous piece by piece, applying this theorem once per layer.

Worked example

Worked Example Certify that f(x) = 1/(x² + 1) is continuous everywhere. The constant function 1 and the identity function x are both continuous everywhere (immediate from the epsilon-delta definition — any δ ≤ ε works for the identity). By the product rule, x·x = x² is continuous; by the sum rule, x² + 1 is continuous; since x² + 1 ≥ 1 > 0 everywhere, the quotient rule applies with no zero-denominator exception needed, so 1/(x²+1) is continuous everywhere.

2. What does the composition theorem require of g, beyond f being continuous at a?

Only continuity of g at the specific point f(a) is needed — not continuity of g everywhere.

3. In the worked example, why didn't the quotient rule need an exception for a zero denominator?

x² + 1 is bounded below by 1, so it's never zero — meaning the quotient rule's exception clause simply never triggers here.
