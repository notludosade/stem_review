The Intermediate Value Theorem

AP Calc BC's most-used existence theorem, finally proved from first principles instead of appealed to as "obvious."

← Lesson 21 Lesson 23 → Contents Glossary →

This lesson delivers on the promise made at the end of Unit 3, Lesson 18: the Intermediate Value Theorem falls out almost immediately once continuity is defined and connectedness is in hand — no appeal to "obviously, since the graph can't jump."

Theorem — Intermediate Value Theorem Let f be continuous on [a, b], and let y be any value between f(a) and f(b). Then there exists c ∈ [a, b] with f(c) = y.

The proof, via connectedness

Proof [a, b] is an interval, hence connected (Unit 3, Lesson 18). A continuous image of a connected set is connected — a fact that follows quickly from the sequential characterization of continuity (Lesson 20): a separation of f([a,b]) would pull back, via continuity, to a separation of [a,b] itself, contradicting its connectedness. So f([a, b]) is a connected subset of ℝ, and by Unit 3 Lesson 18's characterization theorem, connected subsets of ℝ are exactly the intervals. Since f(a) and f(b) both lie in f([a,b]), and f([a,b]) is an interval, every y between f(a) and f(b) lies in f([a,b]) too — meaning y = f(c) for some c ∈ [a, b]. ∎
Why this proof is more satisfying than a direct one IVT can also be proved directly (a classic approach: let c = sup{x ∈ [a,b] : f(x) < y} and show f(c) = y using continuity and the epsilon characterization of supremum). But the connectedness proof reveals why IVT is true at a structural level: it's not a fact about this specific function, but a fact about continuous functions preserving an entire topological property. This is the payoff for building Unit 3 before Unit 4, rather than proving IVT the direct way as many introductory treatments do.

An application: existence of roots

Worked Example Every polynomial of odd degree has a real root. Sketch: for p(x) = xⁿ + (lower order terms) with n odd, p(x) → +∞ as x → +∞ and p(x) → −∞ as x → −∞ (the leading term dominates for large |x|), so p(M) > 0 and p(−M) < 0 for M large enough. Since p is continuous (a finite sum of continuous power functions, Lesson 21) and 0 lies between p(−M) and p(M), IVT gives some c ∈ [−M, M] with p(c) = 0.

1. What does the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantee?

IVT guarantees every intermediate value gets hit — not monotonicity, not differentiability, and not that the endpoints agree.

2. In the connectedness-based proof, what key fact about continuous functions is used?

This is the structural fact the proof leans on: connectedness (Unit 3) is preserved by continuous functions, and IVT is a direct consequence.

3. In the odd-degree polynomial example, why does IVT apply to conclude a root exists?

The sign change plus continuity is exactly the IVT hypothesis needed to guarantee a root somewhere in between.

4. Fill in the blank: the proof shows f([a,b]) is ___, and then uses Unit 3's theorem that this property is equivalent to being an interval.

Connected is the topological property the proof establishes for the image, then translates into "is an interval" via Unit 3, Lesson 18.

5. Fill in the blank: applying IVT to a sign change of a polynomial guarantees the existence of a ___ somewhere in between.

Root — a point where the polynomial equals zero — is exactly what IVT guarantees given a sign change and continuity.
