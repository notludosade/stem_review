The Completeness Axiom

The one axiom ℝ has and ℚ doesn't — precisely naming the "no gaps" property Lesson 1 promised.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 2 showed that the field and order axioms alone don't distinguish ℝ from ℚ. Lesson 4 built the vocabulary — supremum — needed to state the one axiom that does.

Axiom — Completeness (Least Upper Bound Property) Every nonempty subset of ℝ that is bounded above has a supremum, and that supremum is itself a real number.

This is stated as an axiom, not a theorem, because it cannot be proved from the field and order axioms alone — ℚ satisfies every field and order axiom and still fails this property, as the next example shows directly.

Where ℚ fails

Example Let A = {q in ℚ : q² < 2} — every rational number whose square is less than 2. A is nonempty (0 is in A) and bounded above (2 is an upper bound, say). Within ℝ, sup A = √2. But √2 is irrational — so if the only numbers available were rationals, A would be a nonempty, bounded-above set of rationals with no rational supremum at all. Every rational candidate either isn't an upper bound (too small) or isn't the least one (a slightly smaller rational upper bound always exists).
Why this is exactly Lesson 1's gap, now stated precisely Lesson 1 described √2's approximating sequence as having "nowhere to converge" within ℚ. This is the same fact, in the vocabulary this unit has now built: A = {q ∈ ℚ : q² < 2} is a perfectly reasonable, bounded set — and it simply has no least upper bound in ℚ. Completeness is the axiom that guarantees this failure mode is impossible in ℝ.

Completeness works both directions

The axiom as stated only mentions suprema. The matching statement for infima follows as a theorem, not a second axiom:

Theorem — Greatest Lower Bound Property Every nonempty subset of ℝ that is bounded below has an infimum in ℝ.
Proof Let A be nonempty and bounded below by some m. Consider the set −A = {−a : a in A}. Every element of −A is ≤ −m (since a ≥ m for all a in A implies −a ≤ −m), so −A is bounded above by −m. By the completeness axiom, s = sup(−A) exists in ℝ. Then −s is a lower bound of A: for any a in A, −a ≤ s, so a ≥ −s. And −s is the greatest such lower bound, by the same mirrored argument applied to condition (ii) of the supremum definition. So inf A = −s exists in ℝ. ∎
Why this proof pattern matters Rather than stating a second axiom for infima, the proof reuses the completeness axiom by flipping every element's sign — turning a "bounded below" question into a "bounded above" question the axiom already answers. This reflection trick (negate the set, apply what you already have, negate back) reappears throughout analysis whenever a "lower" version of an "upper" fact is needed.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why is completeness stated as an axiom rather than proved as a theorem?

ℚ is a counterexample showing the field and order axioms are not enough on their own — completeness is a genuinely separate assumption.

2. Why does A = {q ∈ ℚ : q² < 2} fail to have a supremum within ℚ?

Within ℝ, sup A = √2 exactly — but since √2 ∉ ℚ, there is no candidate rational supremum at all, exposing the gap directly.

3. In the proof of the Greatest Lower Bound Property, what technique turns the "bounded below" question into one the completeness axiom directly answers?

Negating the set flips "bounded below" into "bounded above," letting the (already-established) completeness axiom for suprema do the actual work.

4. Fill in the blank: the completeness axiom states that every nonempty, bounded-above subset of ℝ has a ___ in ℝ.

Supremum — the least upper bound — is exactly what the completeness axiom guarantees exists.

5. Fill in the blank: {q ∈ ℚ : q² < 2} has no rational supremum because its real supremum, √2, is ___.

√2 being irrational is exactly why ℚ has no candidate to serve as this set's supremum, even though ℝ does.
