Bounds, Supremum, and Infimum

The vocabulary needed to finally state, precisely, what it means for the real numbers to have "no gaps."

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 promised that completeness — the gap-free property of ℝ — would eventually get a precise definition. It needs one more idea first: the "least" upper bound of a set.

Upper and lower bounds

Definition — Bounded Above / Below A nonempty set A ⊆ ℝ is bounded above if there exists some M in ℝ with a ≤ M for every a in A; any such M is called an upper bound of A. Bounded below and lower bound are defined the same way with a ≥ m.

An upper bound is not unique — if M works, so does M + 1, M + 100, anything bigger. That non-uniqueness is exactly why "the" upper bound isn't a useful idea on its own; what's useful is the smallest one.

The supremum: the least upper bound

Definition — Supremum Let A ⊆ ℝ be bounded above. s is the supremum of A (written s = sup A) if: (i) s is an upper bound of A, and (ii) s ≤ M for every other upper bound M of A. In words: s is the smallest possible upper bound.
Example For A = (0, 1) (the open interval), every M ≥ 1 is an upper bound, and sup A = 1 — even though 1 itself is not in A. For B = {1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, …}, sup B = 1, achieved exactly, since 1 is the largest element of B itself.

These two examples matter together: a supremum can be a member of the set (as with B), or it can sit just outside it, unreachable but still the tightest possible ceiling (as with A). Either way, it's the same idea — the least upper bound.

Definition — Infimum Symmetrically, the infimum of a set bounded below, inf A, is the greatest lower bound: the largest m such that m is still a valid lower bound of A.

A working test for "is s really the supremum?"

Condition (ii) above — "s ≤ every other upper bound" — is awkward to check directly, since it quantifies over every possible upper bound at once. There's an equivalent, more usable version:

Lemma — Epsilon Characterization of Supremum Let A be bounded above and let s be an upper bound of A. Then s = sup A if and only if: for every ε > 0, there exists some a in A with a > s − ε.
Proof (⇒) Suppose s = sup A and let ε > 0. Then s − ε < s, so s − ε is not an upper bound of A (since s is the least upper bound). "Not an upper bound" means some element of A exceeds it — so some a in A satisfies a > s − ε. (⇐) Suppose instead that s is an upper bound and, for every ε > 0, some a in A satisfies a > s − ε. Suppose toward contradiction that s is not the least upper bound — so some smaller upper bound M < s exists. Take ε = s − M > 0. By assumption, some a in A satisfies a > s − ε = M. But that contradicts M being an upper bound of A. So no smaller upper bound exists, and s = sup A. ∎
Why this version is the one actually used in proofs "For every ε > 0, some element of A gets within ε of s" is a statement about getting arbitrarily close — precisely the kind of claim this course's proof techniques are built to handle. Nearly every supremum argument from here forward uses this ε-characterization rather than the original definition directly.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What distinguishes the supremum from any other upper bound of a set?

Any number at least as large as every set element is an upper bound; the supremum is specifically the least one.

2. For A = (0, 1), why is sup A = 1 even though 1 is not an element of A?

A supremum only needs to be the least upper bound — it can sit just outside the set entirely, as it does here.

3. In the epsilon characterization lemma, what does "for every ε > 0, some a in A satisfies a > s − ε" actually guarantee?

This is exactly what rules out any smaller candidate upper bound — some element of A always beats it, which is what makes s the least one.

4. Fill in the blank: the smallest upper bound of a set is called its ___.

Supremum, or equivalently "least upper bound" — the term this entire lesson defines.

5. Fill in the blank: the largest lower bound of a set is called its ___.

Infimum is the mirror-image concept of supremum, for lower bounds.
