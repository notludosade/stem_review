Consequences of Completeness: The Archimedean Property and Density of ℚ

The first two real theorems this course proves — both direct payoffs of the axiom from Lesson 5, and both quietly assumed throughout ordinary calculus.

← Lesson 5 Unit 2, Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Completeness was stated in Lesson 5 as an abstract guarantee: bounded-above sets have suprema. This lesson gets the first two concrete theorems out of it — one about how the natural numbers sit inside ℝ, and one about how the rationals sit inside ℝ.

The Archimedean Property

Theorem — Archimedean Property For every real number x, there exists a natural number n such that n > x.

Informally: no matter how large a real number is, the natural numbers eventually pass it. This sounds too obvious to need a proof — until you notice that "obvious" isn't a valid justification in this course, and that the proof genuinely needs completeness.

Proof Suppose, toward contradiction, that the Archimedean Property is false: some real number x exists with n ≤ x for every natural number n. Then the set ℕ = {1, 2, 3, …} is bounded above (by x). ℕ is nonempty, so by the completeness axiom, s = sup ℕ exists in ℝ. Since s is the least upper bound, s − 1 is not an upper bound of ℕ (it's smaller than the least one). So some natural number m satisfies m > s − 1, which rearranges to m + 1 > s. But m + 1 is itself a natural number, and it exceeds s — contradicting that s is an upper bound of ℕ at all. So no such x exists, and the Archimedean Property holds. ∎
Why this proof needed completeness specifically The entire argument hinges on ℕ having a supremum in the first place — a step that only the completeness axiom licenses. This is the first concrete evidence that completeness isn't just abstract bookkeeping: without it, this proof has no way to get started, and the Archimedean Property itself could conceivably fail.

Density of ℚ in ℝ

The second consequence answers a natural question: given two different real numbers, however close together, is there always a rational number squeezed in between?

Theorem — Density of ℚ in ℝ For any real numbers a < b, there exists a rational number q with a < q < b.
Proof Since a < b, b − a > 0. By the Archimedean Property, choose a natural number n with n > 1/(b − a), which rearranges to 1/n < b − a. Now consider the set of integers m with m > na. By the Archimedean Property again, such integers exist (and, using it on −na as well, this set is bounded below), so it has a least element — call it m. By minimality, m − 1 ≤ na, so m ≤ na + 1. Combining with m > na: na < m ≤ na + 1. Divide through by n: a < m/n ≤ a + 1/n. Since 1/n < b − a, this gives m/n ≤ a + 1/n < a + (b − a) = b. So a < m/n < b, and q = m/n is a rational number strictly between a and b. ∎
Worked Example Take a = 1.41421 and b = 1.41422 (values squeezing √2). The proof's construction: pick n > 1/(b − a) = 100,000, say n = 200,000, then find the right integer m — concretely, m = 282,843 gives m/n = 1.414215, which does sit strictly between a and b. The proof guarantees such a rational always exists; it doesn't require finding the "nicest" one.
Why density matters going forward Density means the rationals are packed infinitely tightly throughout the real line — between any two reals, no matter how close, a rational always fits. Combined with Lesson 1's observation that irrationals like √2 fill genuine gaps between rationals, this paints the full picture: ℚ is dense but incomplete, while ℝ is both dense in itself and complete. Density will resurface directly in Unit 2, when it guarantees that sequences of rationals can approximate any real number to any desired accuracy.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does the Archimedean Property guarantee?

The Archimedean Property says the naturals are unbounded — they eventually exceed any given real number, which is the opposite of the natural numbers being bounded above.

2. Why does the proof of the Archimedean Property use a proof by contradiction rather than a direct argument?

Assuming the negation gives a bounded-above set (ℕ) to apply completeness to directly — the supremum that results is then shown to be contradictory, which is what makes the contradiction approach work here.

3. In the density proof, what was the purpose of choosing n > 1/(b − a) using the Archimedean Property?

Making 1/n smaller than the gap between a and b is exactly what guarantees some multiple m/n can land strictly inside that gap.

4. Fill in the blank: the ___ Property guarantees that the natural numbers are not bounded above by any real number.

The Archimedean Property is the theorem this lesson proves first, directly from completeness.

5. Fill in the blank: because a rational number can always be found strictly between any two distinct real numbers, ℚ is said to be ___ in ℝ.

Density is the name for exactly this property — rationals packed arbitrarily tightly throughout the real line.
