Why Real Analysis Exists

Calculus tells you what's true. Real analysis proves it — and in the process exposes exactly where calculus's usual hand-waving can quietly fail.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

AP Calculus BC taught you to compute limits, derivatives, and integrals fluently. It also relied, constantly, on definitions that were never fully nailed down: a limit is when a function "gets arbitrarily close" to a value; a continuous function has "no gaps or jumps." Those descriptions are good enough to build intuition and pass an exam. They are not good enough to prove anything, because "arbitrarily close" and "no gaps" aren't yet precise claims — they're descriptions of a picture.

Why "it looks continuous" isn't a proof A picture can mislead. There exist functions that are continuous at exactly one point and nowhere else, sequences that look like they're converging but aren't, and reasoning that feels airtight but silently assumes something that turns out to be false. Real analysis exists to replace "it looks true" with a definition precise enough that a claim can be checked mechanically, step by step, with no picture required.

A concrete case where intuition needs help

Consider building the number √2 one rational decimal digit at a time:

1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, 1.4142, 1.41421, …

Every term in this list is a rational number. The list is increasing (each term is bigger than the last), and it's bounded above — by 2, say. Intuitively, an increasing sequence that never passes a fixed ceiling has to settle down and converge to something. That "something" is √2.

The problem √2 is not a rational number. So if the only numbers that existed were the rationals — no reals, just fractions — this perfectly reasonable, increasing, bounded sequence of rational numbers would have nowhere to converge to. Its limit would fall through a gap in the number line that the rationals simply don't fill.

That gap is not a minor technicality. It means a statement as basic as "every increasing sequence bounded above converges" is false if you're only working with rational numbers — and true once you're working with real numbers. The difference between ℚ and ℝ is exactly the difference between a number line full of gaps and one without any. Naming precisely what "without gaps" means is one of the first jobs of this course, in Lesson 5's completeness axiom.

What "rigor" actually buys you

This course rebuilds calculus's core claims — limits, continuity, the Intermediate Value Theorem, derivatives, integrals — from a small set of axioms about the real numbers, so that every later claim traces back to something already proven, all the way down. The payoff isn't just certainty for its own sake:

Example The Intermediate Value Theorem says a continuous function that goes from negative to positive must cross zero somewhere. That's obviously true for a smooth hand-drawn curve. But why is it true — what property of the real numbers actually forces it? The answer, precisely, is completeness: the same gap-free property that lets √2's approximating sequence actually converge. Once you can name that connection, you understand why IVT holds, not just that it does.

The road ahead

This course builds in one direction, each unit resting on the last: the real number system and its completeness axiom (Unit 1), rigorous limits of sequences (Unit 2), the basic topology those limits live in (Unit 3), limits and continuity of functions (Unit 4), differentiation (Unit 5), and finally the Riemann integral and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Unit 6) — arriving, by the end, at a fully rigorous proof of the theorem AP Calculus BC asked you to simply use.
