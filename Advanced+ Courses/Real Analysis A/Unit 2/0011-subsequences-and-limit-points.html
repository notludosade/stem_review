Subsequences and Limit Points

What's left to talk about when a sequence doesn't converge as a whole, but part of it still settles down.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 7's example aₙ = (−1)ⁿ doesn't converge. But it isn't formless chaos either — every other term is exactly 1, and every other term is exactly −1. This lesson names that structure precisely.

Subsequences

Definition — Subsequence Given a sequence (aₙ), a subsequence is a sequence formed by choosing a strictly increasing list of indices n₁ < n₂ < n₃ < … and taking (an₁, an₂, an₃, …). A subsequence keeps infinitely many of the original terms, in their original order, but is allowed to skip terms — it can never reorder them or repeat an index.
Example For aₙ = (−1)ⁿ, taking every even index (n = 2, 4, 6, …) gives the subsequence 1, 1, 1, … — constant, and convergent to 1. Taking every odd index gives −1, −1, −1, … — convergent to −1. Neither subsequence is the original sequence, but both converge, even though the original sequence as a whole does not.

A sequence's own limit is inherited by every subsequence

Theorem If lim aₙ = L, then every subsequence of (aₙ) also converges to L.
Proof Let (anₖ) be a subsequence, and let ε > 0. Since lim aₙ = L, there exists N such that |aₙ − L| < ε for all n > N. Because n₁ < n₂ < n₃ < … is strictly increasing, nₖ ≥ k for every k (an easy induction: n₁ ≥ 1, and each subsequent index is strictly larger than the last, so it increases by at least 1 each step). So for k > N, nₖ ≥ k > N, meaning anₖ satisfies the same bound: |anₖ − L| < ε. So the subsequence also converges to L. ∎
Why this gives a fast test for divergence Contrapositively: if a sequence has two subsequences converging to different limits, the original sequence cannot converge at all — if it did, both subsequences would be forced to converge to that same single limit, a contradiction. This is exactly what makes aₙ = (−1)ⁿ's divergence easy to certify rigorously: its even and odd subsequences converge to 1 and −1 respectively, which are different, so the whole sequence must diverge.

Limit points

Definition — Limit Point of a Sequence A real number x is a limit point (or subsequential limit) of a sequence (aₙ) if some subsequence of (aₙ) converges to x.

A convergent sequence has exactly one limit point — its own limit, since every subsequence converges to that same value (the theorem above). A divergent sequence can have several limit points at once, or none, depending on its behavior.

Example aₙ = (−1)ⁿ has exactly two limit points: 1 and −1. A sequence like aₙ = n (increasing without bound) has none — no subsequence of it converges to any real number, since every subsequence is itself unbounded.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is required of the indices n₁, n₂, n₃, … used to form a subsequence?

Strictly increasing indices is the one requirement — a subsequence can skip terms and must preserve order, but can't reorder or repeat.

2. If a sequence converges to L, what does every subsequence of it do?

This is the theorem proved in this lesson — a convergent sequence forces every one of its subsequences to the same limit.

3. Why does the existence of two subsequences converging to different limits prove a sequence diverges?

This is the contrapositive of the lesson's main theorem, and exactly how (−1)ⁿ's divergence gets certified rigorously.

4. Fill in the blank: a sequence formed by keeping infinitely many terms of an original sequence, in order but possibly skipping some, is called a ___.

Subsequence is the term this lesson defines and builds its central theorem around.

5. Fill in the blank: a value that some subsequence of a sequence converges to is called a ___ of that sequence.

Limit point (or subsequential limit) is exactly this concept — (−1)ⁿ has two of them, 1 and −1.
