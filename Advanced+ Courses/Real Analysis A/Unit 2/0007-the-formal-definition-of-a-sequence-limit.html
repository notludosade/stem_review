The Formal Definition of a Sequence Limit

Turning "gets arbitrarily close" into a statement with quantifiers, checkable step by step with no picture required.

← Unit 1, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 1 built the real numbers themselves — axioms, bounds, completeness. Unit 2 puts that structure to work on the first genuinely calculus-flavored idea: what it actually means for a sequence to converge. A sequence is just an ordered, infinite list of real numbers a₁, a₂, a₃, … — formally, a function from ℕ to ℝ, written (aₙ).

The definition

Definition — Limit of a Sequence A sequence (aₙ) converges to a real number L, written lim aₙ = L, if: for every ε > 0, there exists a natural number N such that |aₙ − L| < ε for every n > N.

Read it in pieces. ε ("epsilon") is a challenge — any margin of error, however small, that someone else picks. N is your response — an index past which every single term of the sequence lands within that margin. The definition says: no matter how small a margin you're challenged with, a point exists after which the sequence stays inside it, forever. That's what "gets arbitrarily close, and stays there" means, made checkable.

Why the order of the quantifiers is everything N is allowed to depend on ε — and in every real example, it has to. A tiny ε (a tight margin) generally forces a much larger N (you have to wait longer for the sequence to squeeze in that tight) than a generous ε does. The definition's structure — "for every ε, there exists an N" — bakes in that dependency: ε is chosen first, then N is found in response to it, never the other way around.

Reading the definition against a picture

Picture the number line with a band of width 2ε centered on L — from L − ε to L + ε. The definition says: for any such band, however thin, only finitely many terms of the sequence (a₁ through a_N) are allowed to fall outside it. Every term after that has to land inside.

A sequence that does not converge

Example Let aₙ = (−1)ⁿ, so the sequence is −1, 1, −1, 1, … forever. This does not converge to any L. Sketch why: whatever L you propose, pick ε = 1. The terms alternate between −1 and 1, which are 2 apart — so at least one of them is always at least 1 away from L (they can't both be within 1 of the same L, since that would force L to be within 1 of both −1 and 1, impossible since |1 − (−1)| = 2 > 1 + 1 only if... actually more directly: no single band of width 2 centered anywhere can contain both −1 and 1 permanently past any N, since both values recur infinitely often). No N works, for any candidate L.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the definition of a sequence limit, what is N allowed to depend on?

N is chosen in response to ε — that dependency is exactly what the quantifier order "for every ε, there exists an N" encodes.

2. What does |aₙ − L| < ε for every n > N actually say?

The condition applies to every n greater than N, not just one term — the whole tail of the sequence must stay inside the band.

3. Why does aₙ = (−1)ⁿ fail to converge to any L?

The sequence is bounded (between −1 and 1) but still diverges — boundedness alone doesn't guarantee convergence, only the permanent oscillation matters here.

4. Fill in the blank: in the limit definition, ___ represents the margin of error, chosen first, that the sequence must eventually squeeze inside.

Epsilon is the challenge value — chosen arbitrarily small, with N found in response.

5. Fill in the blank: a sequence that satisfies the epsilon-N definition for some L is said to ___ to L.

Converges is the term for a sequence satisfying this definition — the central object of study for the rest of this unit.
