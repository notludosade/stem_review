The Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem

Boundedness alone — with no monotonicity, no known limit — is still enough to guarantee a convergent piece exists somewhere inside.

← Lesson 11 Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

The Monotone Convergence Theorem (Lesson 10) needed both boundedness and monotonicity. Many bounded sequences aren't monotone at all — aₙ = (−1)ⁿ/n, say, which oscillates while shrinking. This lesson shows boundedness by itself is still enough to guarantee a convergent subsequence exists, even with no monotonicity in sight.

Theorem — Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

The proof idea: repeated bisection

The proof builds a convergent subsequence by trapping infinitely many terms of the original sequence inside intervals that shrink to a single point.

Proof Since (aₙ) is bounded, all its terms lie in some closed interval I₀ = [−M, M]. Bisect I₀ into two closed halves. At least one half must contain aₙ for infinitely many n (if both halves only contained finitely many terms, the whole sequence would be finite, which it isn't). Call that half I₁, and pick any term an₁ from (aₙ) that lies in I₁. Now bisect I₁ the same way; again, one half contains infinitely many terms of the original sequence — call it I₂, and pick an₂ from within I₂ with n₂ > n₁ (possible since I₂ contains infinitely many terms, so one with a large enough index is always available). Repeating this process forever produces a nested sequence of intervals I₀ ⊇ I₁ ⊇ I₂ ⊇ … , each half the width of the last, and a subsequence (anₖ) with anₖ ∈ Ik for every k. Since the interval widths shrink to 0, the nested intervals converge to a single point x, and since anₖ is trapped inside Ik at every step, anₖ → x. ∎
Why "infinitely many terms in at least one half" is the key step This is a pigeonhole-style argument: infinitely many terms have to go somewhere, and splitting into only two halves means at least one half absorbs infinitely many of them. Repeating this indefinitely is what builds an infinite subsequence, one shrinking interval at a time — no monotonicity anywhere in the argument, only boundedness and this repeated halving.
Example aₙ = (−1)ⁿ/n is bounded (between −1 and 1) but not monotone (it oscillates in sign while shrinking in size). Bolzano-Weierstrass guarantees a convergent subsequence regardless — and in fact the whole sequence converges to 0 here, so every subsequence does. A more telling example is aₙ = (−1)ⁿ itself: not convergent as a whole, but Bolzano-Weierstrass still guarantees a convergent subsequence, and indeed the even-indexed terms (Lesson 11) supply one.

Why boundedness alone isn't enough for full convergence

Why Bolzano-Weierstrass stops at "a subsequence," not "the sequence" (−1)ⁿ is bounded and has convergent subsequences, but never converges itself — proving the theorem can't be strengthened to guarantee convergence of the whole sequence from boundedness alone. What Bolzano-Weierstrass buys is weaker but still powerful: a guaranteed foothold (some convergent piece) to reason about, even when the full sequence refuses to settle down. This foothold is exactly what Lesson 13 needs to characterize convergence a different way, via Cauchy sequences.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the only hypothesis the Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem requires of a sequence?

Boundedness alone is the entire hypothesis — no monotonicity is required, unlike the Monotone Convergence Theorem.

2. In the bisection proof, why must at least one half of a bisected interval contain infinitely many terms of the sequence?

This is a pigeonhole-style argument: infinitely many terms split between only two halves forces at least one half to contain infinitely many.

3. Why does the theorem stop at guaranteeing a convergent subsequence, rather than convergence of the whole sequence?

(−1)ⁿ is the exact counterexample showing boundedness alone cannot be strengthened to guarantee full convergence.

4. Fill in the blank: the Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem states that every ___ sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

Bounded is the single hypothesis needed — the theorem's entire content is that this alone is enough.

5. Fill in the blank: the proof builds a convergent subsequence using repeated ___ of a bounded interval, always keeping the half containing infinitely many terms.

Bisection — repeatedly halving the interval — is the mechanism that traps a subsequence into a single limiting point.
