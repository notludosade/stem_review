Limit Laws for Sequences

Prove sum, product, and quotient rules once, from the definition — then never write an epsilon-N proof for a combined sequence again.

← Lesson 8 Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 8's technique works, but writing a fresh epsilon-N proof for every new sequence — especially a sequence built out of simpler ones by adding or multiplying — would get unmanageable fast. This lesson proves the rules once, so from here on, combined limits can be computed algebraically instead.

The sum rule

Theorem — Algebraic Limit Theorem (Sum) If lim aₙ = A and lim bₙ = B, then lim (aₙ + bₙ) = A + B.
Proof Let ε > 0. Since lim aₙ = A, there exists N₁ such that |aₙ − A| < ε/2 for all n > N₁ (apply the definition with ε/2 in place of ε — a valid choice, since ε/2 is still positive). Since lim bₙ = B, there exists N₂ such that |bₙ − B| < ε/2 for all n > N₂. Let N = max(N₁, N₂). For any n > N, both bounds hold simultaneously, so by the triangle inequality (Unit 1, Lesson 3): |(aₙ + bₙ) − (A + B)| = |(aₙ − A) + (bₙ − B)| ≤ |aₙ − A| + |bₙ − B| < ε/2 + ε/2 = ε. So lim (aₙ + bₙ) = A + B. ∎
Why split ε into ε/2 and ε/2 Two separate error sources (aₙ's distance from A, and bₙ's distance from B) both need to be controlled at once, and the triangle inequality is what lets their individual errors add up rather than compound. Budgeting ε/2 to each piece in advance guarantees the total, once added, lands exactly at the ε the proof needs — this "splitting the error budget" trick reappears constantly whenever a proof needs to control more than one quantity simultaneously.

The product rule

Theorem — Algebraic Limit Theorem (Product) If lim aₙ = A and lim bₙ = B, then lim (aₙ bₙ) = AB.
Proof sketch Write aₙbₙ − AB = aₙbₙ − aₙB + aₙB − AB = aₙ(bₙ − B) + B(aₙ − A). Since (aₙ) converges, it's bounded (a convergent sequence's terms can't run off to infinity — a fact proved directly from the definition), say |aₙ| ≤ M for all n. Then |aₙbₙ − AB| ≤ |aₙ||bₙ − B| + |B||aₙ − A| ≤ M|bₙ − B| + |B||aₙ − A|. Both |bₙ − B| and |aₙ − A| can be made arbitrarily small (by the individual limit definitions), and M and |B| are fixed constants — so the whole right-hand side can be forced below any ε using the same split-budget technique as the sum rule. ∎
Why "convergent implies bounded" was needed here Without a bound M on |aₙ|, the term |aₙ||bₙ − B| couldn't be controlled — an unbounded aₙ could blow up even a tiny |bₙ − B|. This is the first place in the course where boundedness of a sequence (not just convergence) does real work, foreshadowing Lesson 12's Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem, where boundedness is the entire hypothesis.

Quotient rule, and using the laws together

Theorem — Algebraic Limit Theorem (Quotient) If lim aₙ = A, lim bₙ = B, and B ≠ 0 (with bₙ ≠ 0 for all n), then lim (aₙ/bₙ) = A/B.
Worked Example Compute lim ((3n² + 2n)/(n² + 1)) using the laws directly instead of an epsilon-N proof: divide numerator and denominator by n², giving (3 + 2/n)/(1 + 1/n²). Since lim (2/n) = 0 and lim (1/n²) = 0 (both provable the same way as Lesson 8's 1/n → 0), the sum rule gives numerator → 3 and denominator → 1, and the quotient rule (with B = 1 ≠ 0) gives the whole expression → 3/1 = 3.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the sum rule's proof, why was ε split into ε/2 and ε/2 rather than used directly for both aₙ and bₙ?

Budgeting ε/2 to each piece means their sum, bounded by the triangle inequality, lands at exactly ε — the proof's target.

2. Why did the product rule's proof need the fact that a convergent sequence is bounded?

The bound M on |aₙ| is exactly what lets M|bₙ − B| be forced small — without it, that term could grow without limit.

3. In the worked example computing lim ((3n² + 2n)/(n² + 1)), what was the purpose of dividing numerator and denominator by n²?

Rewriting in terms of known-convergent pieces is exactly what lets the algebraic limit laws replace a full epsilon-N proof.

4. Fill in the blank: the product rule's proof relies on the fact that a convergent sequence is always ___.

Boundedness of convergent sequences is the fact that makes the product rule's proof work, and it resurfaces directly in the Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem.

5. Fill in the blank: the sum rule's proof combines the two individual error bounds using the ___ inequality from Unit 1.

The triangle inequality is exactly what lets |(aₙ − A) + (bₙ − B)| be bounded by the sum of the two individual error terms.
