Monotone Sequences and the Monotone Convergence Theorem

The first theorem in this course that proves a sequence converges without ever having to guess its limit first.

← Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Every proof so far has required already knowing the candidate limit L before checking the epsilon-N definition against it. This lesson proves the first result that sidesteps that requirement entirely — exactly resolving Lesson 1's original example, the increasing sequence of rational approximations to √2, without ever needing to know in advance that the answer is √2.

Monotone sequences

Definition — Monotone Sequence A sequence (aₙ) is increasing if aₙ ≤ aₙ₊₁ for every n, and decreasing if aₙ ≥ aₙ₊₁ for every n. A sequence that is either increasing or decreasing is called monotone.

The theorem

Theorem — Monotone Convergence Theorem If (aₙ) is increasing and bounded above, then (aₙ) converges — specifically, to sup{aₙ : n ∈ ℕ}. (Symmetrically, a decreasing sequence bounded below converges to its infimum.)
Proof Let A = {aₙ : n ∈ ℕ}, the set of terms of the sequence. A is nonempty and bounded above (by hypothesis), so by the completeness axiom (Unit 1, Lesson 5), s = sup A exists in ℝ. Claim: lim aₙ = s. Let ε > 0. By the epsilon characterization of supremum (Unit 1, Lesson 4), some term a_N in A satisfies a_N > s − ε. Since (aₙ) is increasing, every n > N satisfies aₙ ≥ a_N > s − ε. Also, since s is an upper bound of A, every aₙ ≤ s < s + ε. Combining: s − ε < aₙ < s + ε for all n > N, i.e. |aₙ − s| < ε. So lim aₙ = s. ∎
Why this proof is doing something genuinely new Every previous convergence proof in this course started with a proposed limit L in hand. Here, s = sup A is produced by the completeness axiom before anything about convergence is shown — completeness supplies the candidate limit, and monotonicity plus the epsilon-characterization lemma is what shows the sequence actually reaches it. This is completeness doing real, structural work, not just ruling out a hypothetical gap as in Unit 1.

Resolving Lesson 1's example

Example The sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, … from Lesson 1 is increasing and bounded above by 2. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, it converges — to some real number, guaranteed to exist, without first needing to identify it as √2. (Showing that limit specifically equals √2 takes an additional short argument using continuity of x², covered once Unit 4 introduces continuous functions — but convergence itself is now settled immediately.)

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does the Monotone Convergence Theorem require of a sequence in order to guarantee convergence?

Monotonicity plus a bound in the matching direction (above for increasing, below for decreasing) is exactly the hypothesis — no need to know the limit beforehand.

2. In the proof, what specifically does the sequence converge to?

s = sup{aₙ} is exactly the limit the proof identifies and confirms — supplied directly by the completeness axiom.

3. Which earlier result does the proof rely on to conclude that some term a_N satisfies a_N > s − ε?

The epsilon characterization of supremum is precisely what guarantees a term of the sequence gets within ε of s — the crux of the whole proof.

4. Fill in the blank: a sequence that is either entirely increasing or entirely decreasing is called ___.

Monotone is the umbrella term covering both increasing and decreasing sequences.

5. Fill in the blank: the Monotone Convergence Theorem's proof depends directly on the ___ axiom to guarantee the supremum s exists at all.

Without completeness, there would be no guarantee that sup A exists in the first place — this theorem is a direct structural consequence of it.
