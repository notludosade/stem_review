1. For a bounded function f and partition P = {x₀,…,xₙ}, how is the lower sum L(f,P) defined?

The lower sum uses the infimum of f on each subinterval, guaranteeing every rectangle sits entirely at or below the curve — the largest such sum is exactly the greatest lower bound any Riemann sum on that partition could produce.

2. Let P′ be a refinement of P (P′ ⊇ P). Answer both parts:

(a) Which chain of inequalities always holds?

(b) Fill in the blank: to compare two unrelated partitions P₁ and P₂, you take their ___, P₁ ∪ P₂.

(a) Adding points to a partition can only shrink the sup on each smaller piece and grow the inf, so the lower sum rises (or stays put) and the upper sum falls (or stays put). (b) P₁ ∪ P₂ refines both P₁ and P₂ at once, letting the Refinement Lemma be chained twice to compare them.

3. Fill in the blank: f is Riemann integrable on [a,b] if the upper integral U(f) equals the ___ integral L(f).

When the best squeeze from above (U(f), the inf of all upper sums) meets the best squeeze from below (L(f), the sup of all lower sums), that shared value is defined as the Riemann integral.

4. Let f(x) = x on [0, 1], partitioned into 4 equal subintervals of width 0.25. Answer both parts:

(a) By the Cauchy Criterion, f is integrable on [a,b] if and only if for every ε > 0 there exists a partition P with:

(b) Fill in the blank: for this partition, U(f,P) − L(f,P) = ___.

(a) The Cauchy Criterion only requires one sufficiently fine partition per ε, not a direct computation of U(f) and L(f) as a sup/inf over all partitions. (b) Since f(x)=x is increasing, on each width-0.25 subinterval the sup and inf differ by 0.25, so U(f,P) − L(f,P) = 4 × (0.25 × 0.25) = 0.25.

5. Continuous functions on [a,b] are integrable because continuity on a compact set upgrades to which property, giving one δ that works across the whole partition at once?

A single δ must control the oscillation of f on every subinterval of the partition simultaneously — exactly the guarantee uniform continuity (Unit 4, Lesson 24) provides and ordinary continuity does not.

6. Suppose ∫01 f = 4 and ∫01 g = 5. Answer both parts:

(a) By linearity, ∫01 (3f + 2g) equals:

(b) Fill in the blank: so ∫01 (3f + 2g) = ___.

(a) Linearity splits both the sum and the scalar multiples apart. (b) 3(4) + 2(5) = 12 + 10 = 22.

7. Suppose f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x ∈ [0,2], with ∫02 f = 3 and ∫02 g = 7. Answer both parts:

(a) What must be true of ∫02f and ∫02g?

(b) Fill in the blank: if additionally |f(x)| ≤ 5 on [0,2], then |∫02f| ≤ ___.

(a) Every subinterval's sup of f is ≤ the sup of g, so U(f,P) ≤ U(g,P) for every partition, giving ∫f ≤ ∫g. (b) The bound corollary gives |∫02f| ≤ K(b−a) = 5 × 2 = 10.

8. If f is continuous on [a,b] and F(x) = ∫ax f, what does FTC Part 1 conclude?

FTC Part 1 shows differentiating the "integral so far" function F exactly recovers f — differentiation undoes integration.

9. Consider ∫13 2x dx, using the antiderivative G(x) = x². Answer both parts:

(a) By FTC Part 2, ∫13 2x dx = G(3) − G(1). What is this value?

(b) Fill in the blank: this evaluation works because F(x) = ∫1x 2t dt and G(x) = x² share the same derivative, 2x, so by Unit 5's corollary they differ by a ___.

(a) G(3) − G(1) = 9 − 1 = 8. (b) Two functions sharing a derivative differ by exactly one constant (Unit 5, Lesson 29), which is exactly what lets a value at one endpoint pin down the integral's value.

10. Fill in the blank: the capstone lesson traces nearly every existence theorem in this course — a supremum existing, a limit existing, a maximum being attained — back to the single axiom of ___.

Completeness (Unit 1, Lesson 5) is the one axiom distinguishing ℝ from ℚ, and every existence result built afterward — including the sup and inf underlying upper and lower sums themselves — ultimately cashes out to an invocation of it.
