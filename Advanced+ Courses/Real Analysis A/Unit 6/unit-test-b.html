1. For a bounded function f and partition P, what guarantees that Mi = sup{f(x): x ∈ [xi−1,xi]} actually exists for every subinterval?

Completeness is exactly what guarantees a sup (and inf) exists for any bounded nonempty set — the foundation the entire upper/lower sum construction rests on.

2. Refining a partition P by adding more points to get P′. Answer both parts:

(a) What effect can this refinement have on the gap U(f,P) − L(f,P)?

(b) Fill in the blank: two arbitrary partitions P₁ and P₂ can always be compared to each other by taking their ___.

(a) Adding a point can only shrink the sup, or grow the inf, on the pieces it splits — so U(f,P′) ≤ U(f,P) and L(f,P′) ≥ L(f,P), meaning the gap never grows. (b) The common refinement P₁ ∪ P₂ is what lets the Refinement Lemma be applied to two otherwise-unrelated partitions.

3. Fill in the blank: f is Riemann integrable exactly when its upper integral and ___ integral coincide.

L(f), the sup of all lower sums, must equal U(f), the inf of all upper sums, for the Riemann integral to be well-defined as that shared value.

4. Let f(x) = x on [0, 2], partitioned into 4 equal subintervals of width 0.5. Answer both parts:

(a) The Cauchy Criterion lets you avoid computing which quantity directly?

(b) Fill in the blank: for this partition, U(f,P) − L(f,P) = ___.

(a) Directly computing U(f) and L(f) as a sup/inf over every partition is unwieldy; the Cauchy Criterion only asks for one sufficiently fine partition. (b) Since f(x)=x is increasing, each width-0.5 subinterval contributes a sup−inf gap of 0.5, so U(f,P) − L(f,P) = 4 × (0.5 × 0.5) = 1.

5. Which theorem from Unit 4 is the essential ingredient in proving that continuous functions are Riemann integrable?

The proof needs one δ to control every subinterval's oscillation at once — exactly the guarantee that continuity on a compact set upgrades to uniform continuity, from Lesson 24.

6. Suppose ∫01 f = 6 and ∫01 g = 2. Answer both parts:

(a) By linearity, ∫01 (2f − 3g) equals:

(b) Fill in the blank: so ∫01 (2f − 3g) = ___.

(a) Linearity splits the combination into separate scalar multiples of each integral. (b) 2(6) − 3(2) = 12 − 6 = 6.

7. Suppose f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x ∈ [0,4]. Answer both parts:

(a) What must be true of ∫04f and ∫04g?

(b) Fill in the blank: if additionally |f(x)| ≤ 3 on [0,4], then |∫04f| ≤ ___.

(a) The pointwise inequality lifts to every upper sum, hence to the upper integrals themselves. (b) The bound corollary gives |∫04f| ≤ K(b−a) = 3 × 4 = 12.

8. In FTC Part 1's proof, why must f be continuous specifically at the point x, not merely integrable overall?

The estimate hinges on f(t) staying close to f(x) for t near x — precisely the epsilon-delta statement of continuity, used here to control the average value of f over a shrinking interval around x.

9. Consider ∫02 3x² dx, using the antiderivative G(x) = x³. Answer both parts:

(a) By FTC Part 2, ∫02 3x² dx = G(2) − G(0). What is this value?

(b) Fill in the blank: this works because F(x) = ∫0x 3t² dt and G(x) = x³ share the same derivative, 3x², so by Unit 5's corollary they differ by a ___.

(a) G(2) − G(0) = 8 − 0 = 8. (b) Sharing a derivative forces F and G to differ by exactly one constant, which is exactly what makes the evaluation formula work.

10. Fill in the blank: the one axiom that ℝ has and ℚ lacks, underlying nearly every existence theorem across all six units of this course, is ___.

This single extra axiom — every nonempty bounded-above set has a least upper bound — is what the capstone lesson traces the entire course's chain of existence theorems back to.
