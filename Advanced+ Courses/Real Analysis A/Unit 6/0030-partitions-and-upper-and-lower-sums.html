Partitions and Upper and Lower Sums

Before the integral can be a number, it needs bracketing sums that squeeze it from above and below — built from nothing but sup and inf.

← Unit 5, Lesson 29 Lesson 31 → Contents Glossary →

AP Calc BC introduced Riemann sums as left/right/midpoint rectangle approximations, and the definite integral as their limit "as the number of rectangles goes to infinity" — informally. Unit 6 replaces that limit with a rigorous construction, built entirely from tools already in hand: bounded sets, sup, and inf (Unit 1).

Definition — Partition A partition P of [a, b] is a finite set of points a = x₀ < x₁ < ⋯ < xₙ = b. It divides [a,b] into subintervals [xi−1, xi] for i = 1, …, n.
Definition — Upper and Lower Sums Let f be bounded on [a, b] and P = {x₀, …, xₙ} a partition. Let Mi = sup{f(x) : x ∈ [xi−1, xi]} and mi = inf{f(x) : x ∈ [xi−1, xi]} (both exist by the Completeness Axiom, Unit 1, since f is bounded on each subinterval). Define U(f, P) = Σi=1n Mi(xi − xi−1)    L(f, P) = Σi=1n mi(xi − xi−1)
Why sup and inf, not a chosen sample point AP Calc BC's Riemann sums picked one sample point per subinterval (left endpoint, right endpoint, midpoint) and got a different number each time. Using the sup and inf of f over each subinterval instead removes that arbitrary choice: Mi and mi are the largest and smallest values any sample point in that subinterval could produce, so U(f,P) and L(f,P) bracket every possible Riemann sum for that partition from above and below.

How refining a partition affects the sums

Lemma — Refinement If P′ is a refinement of P (P′ ⊇ P, i.e. P′ adds more points), then L(f, P) ≤ L(f, P′) ≤ U(f, P′) ≤ U(f, P).
Proof sketch It suffices to check adding one point y to a single subinterval [xi−1, xi], splitting it into [xi−1, y] and [y, xi]. The inf of f over each smaller piece is ≥ the inf over the whole piece [xi−1,xi] (inf over a smaller set is never smaller), so the lower sum can only increase or stay the same. Symmetrically, the sup over each smaller piece is ≤ the sup over the whole piece, so the upper sum can only decrease or stay the same. L(f,P′) ≤ U(f,P′) always holds since each mi ≤ Mi. Repeating one point at a time extends this to any refinement. ∎
Corollary — Any Lower Sum ≤ Any Upper Sum For any two partitions P₁, P₂ of [a,b] (not necessarily one refining the other), L(f, P₁) ≤ U(f, P₂).
Why this needs the common refinement trick P₁ and P₂ may be entirely unrelated partitions, so the Refinement Lemma doesn't directly compare them. But P = P₁ ∪ P₂ (the common refinement) refines both. So L(f,P₁) ≤ L(f,P) ≤ U(f,P) ≤ U(f,P₂), chaining the Refinement Lemma twice. This corollary is exactly what will let Lesson 31 define the integral as the number squeezed between every lower sum and every upper sum at once.

Check your recall

1. What guarantees that Mi and mi exist for each subinterval?

Completeness (Unit 1) is exactly what guarantees sup and inf exist for any bounded set — the foundation the whole upper/lower sum construction rests on.

2. Why do upper and lower sums avoid the arbitrariness of AP Calc BC's left/right/midpoint Riemann sums?

Using sup and inf sidesteps the sample-point choice entirely, since they represent the extremes any choice could produce.

3. Why does comparing L(f,P₁) and U(f,P₂) for two unrelated partitions require a common refinement?

The common refinement P₁ ∪ P₂ is what bridges two otherwise-unrelated partitions, via two applications of the Refinement Lemma.

4. Fill in the blank: a partition P′ that contains all the points of P (and possibly more) is called a ___ of P.

Refinement is the term for a partition with extra points added, central to the Refinement Lemma.

5. Fill in the blank: comparing two unrelated partitions P₁ and P₂ requires taking their ___, P₁ ∪ P₂.

Common refinement is the trick that lets any two partitions be compared via the Refinement Lemma.
