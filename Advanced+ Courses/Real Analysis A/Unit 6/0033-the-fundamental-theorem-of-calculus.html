The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Differentiation and integration, proved — at last, rigorously — to be inverse operations.

← Lesson 32 Lesson 34 → Contents Glossary →

Everything in this course has been building toward this lesson. Unit 5's derivative and this unit's integral were built entirely independently — one from epsilon-delta limits of difference quotients, the other from sup/inf of upper and lower sums. This lesson proves they are inverse operations, the single fact AP Calc BC was named after.

Theorem — FTC, Part 1 Let f be continuous on [a,b]. Define F(x) = ∫ax f for x ∈ [a,b] (well-defined, since f continuous on [a,x] implies f integrable there, Lesson 31). Then F is differentiable on [a,b], with F′(x) = f(x) for every x.
Proof Fix x ∈ [a,b]. For h ≠ 0 small, (F(x+h) − F(x))/h = (1/h)∫xx+h f (by additivity, Lesson 32). Since f is continuous at x, given ε > 0 there is δ > 0 with |f(t) − f(x)| < ε for |t−x| < δ. For 0 < |h| < δ: |(1/h)∫xx+h f − f(x)| = |(1/h)∫xx+h (f(t) − f(x)) dt| ≤ (1/|h|)·ε·|h| = ε (using the bound corollary from Lesson 32 on the integral of f(t)−f(x), which is bounded by ε in absolute value on this stretch). So (F(x+h)−F(x))/h → f(x) as h → 0, meaning F′(x) = f(x). ∎
Why continuity of f at x is exactly what's needed The whole estimate hinges on f(t) staying within ε of f(x) for t near x — precisely the epsilon-delta statement of continuity (Unit 4, Lesson 20), applied here to control the average value of f over a shrinking interval around x, rather than the value of f at a single point.
Theorem — FTC, Part 2 (Evaluation) Let f be continuous on [a,b], and let G be any antiderivative of f on [a,b] (i.e. G′ = f). Then ∫ab f = G(b) − G(a).
Proof Let F(x) = ∫ax f, as in Part 1, so F′ = f = G′ on [a,b]. Then (F − G)′ = 0 everywhere on [a,b], so by Unit 5, Lesson 29's corollary (zero derivative implies constant), F − G = k for some constant k. Evaluating at x = a: F(a) − G(a) = k, and F(a) = ∫aa f = 0 (an empty interval), so k = −G(a). At x = b: F(b) − G(b) = k = −G(a), so F(b) = G(b) − G(a). Since F(b) = ∫ab f, this gives ∫ab f = G(b) − G(a). ∎
Why Unit 5's corollary is the linchpin of Part 2 Part 2 reduces entirely to Part 1 plus one fact from Unit 5: that a zero derivative forces a function to be constant. Without that corollary, there would be no way to conclude F and G (which merely share a derivative) are related by anything more specific than "both are antiderivatives of f" — the corollary is what pins their difference down to a single constant, letting a value at one point (x = a) fix it everywhere.

1. What does FTC Part 1 establish?

Part 1 shows differentiating the "integral so far" function F recovers f exactly — differentiation undoes integration.

2. In Part 1's proof, what role does continuity of f at x play?

Continuity is exactly the epsilon-delta guarantee that lets the average of f over a shrinking window converge to f(x) itself.

3. Why does FTC Part 2's proof need Unit 5's "zero derivative implies constant" corollary?

Two functions sharing a derivative only differ by a constant thanks to that Unit 5 corollary — the exact fact that lets Part 2's evaluation formula fall out.

4. Fill in the blank: FTC Part 2 says the integral of f equals G(b) − G(a) for any ___ G of f.

Antiderivative is the term for any function whose derivative is f — the object FTC Part 2's evaluation formula uses.

5. Fill in the blank: the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus shows differentiation and integration are ___ operations of one another.

Inverse is the relationship FTC establishes — the central insight of the entire theorem.
