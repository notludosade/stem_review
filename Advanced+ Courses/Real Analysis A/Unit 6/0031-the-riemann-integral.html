The Riemann Integral

The definite integral, defined at last as a single squeezed number — and a clean test for when that squeeze actually pins one down.

← Lesson 30 Lesson 32 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 30's corollary showed every lower sum is ≤ every upper sum. That means the set of all lower sums is bounded above (by any upper sum) and the set of all upper sums is bounded below (by any lower sum) — so both have a sup and inf, by Completeness (Unit 1). This lesson uses those two numbers to define the integral.

Definition — Upper and Lower Integrals Let f be bounded on [a,b]. The upper integral is U(f) = inf{U(f,P) : P a partition of [a,b]}, and the lower integral is L(f) = sup{L(f,P) : P a partition of [a,b]}.
Fact L(f) ≤ U(f) always (an immediate consequence of Lesson 30's corollary: since every lower sum is ≤ every upper sum, the sup of the lower sums is ≤ the inf of the upper sums).
Definition — Riemann Integrability f is Riemann integrable on [a,b] if L(f) = U(f). In that case, the Riemann integral is defined as ∫ab f = L(f) = U(f).
Why this matches the AP Calc BC picture L(f) is the best possible lower bound achievable by inscribed rectangles, and U(f) is the best possible upper bound achievable by circumscribed rectangles. When these two "best squeezes" meet at the same number, that shared number is unambiguously "the area under the curve" — no matter which sample points, partitions, or limiting process would have been used informally.

A workable test for integrability

Theorem — Cauchy Criterion for Integrability f (bounded on [a,b]) is Riemann integrable if and only if for every ε > 0, there exists a partition P such that U(f,P) − L(f,P) < ε.
Proof sketch (⇐) If such P exists for every ε, then U(f) − L(f) ≤ U(f,P) − L(f,P) < ε for every ε > 0, forcing U(f) − L(f) = 0. (⇒) If L(f) = U(f), then since U(f) is an inf, some P₁ has U(f,P₁) < U(f) + ε/2; since L(f) is a sup, some P₂ has L(f,P₂) > L(f) − ε/2. Let P = P₁ ∪ P₂ (common refinement, Lesson 30). Then U(f,P) − L(f,P) ≤ U(f,P₁) − L(f,P₂) < (U(f)+ε/2) − (L(f)−ε/2) = ε (using L(f)=U(f)). ∎
Why this criterion is the one actually used in practice Directly computing U(f) and L(f) as a sup/inf over all partitions is unwieldy. The Cauchy Criterion instead asks only for one good-enough partition per ε — a direct parallel to Unit 2's Cauchy sequences, where you never needed the limit itself, just eventual closeness of terms to each other. Here, "closeness" means the upper and lower sums for a single partition are close to each other.

Continuous functions are integrable

Theorem If f is continuous on [a,b], then f is Riemann integrable on [a,b].
Proof Since f is continuous on the compact set [a,b], f is uniformly continuous (Unit 4, Lesson 24). Given ε > 0, uniform continuity gives δ > 0 such that |x − y| < δ implies |f(x) − f(y)| < ε/(b−a). Choose a partition P with every subinterval of length < δ. On each subinterval [xi−1,xi], the Extreme Value Theorem (Unit 4, Lesson 23) guarantees f attains its sup Mi and inf mi at points within that subinterval, so those two points are within δ of each other, giving Mi − mi < ε/(b−a). Then U(f,P) − L(f,P) = Σ(Mi−mi)(xi−xi−1) < [ε/(b−a)]·Σ(xi−xi−1) = [ε/(b−a)]·(b−a) = ε. By the Cauchy Criterion, f is integrable. ∎
Why this proof needed uniform, not just ordinary, continuity A single δ had to work for every subinterval of the partition simultaneously — exactly the "one δ serves the whole domain" guarantee uniform continuity provides and ordinary continuity does not (Unit 4, Lesson 24). This is precisely why that lesson's extra work, proving continuous-on-compact implies uniformly continuous, pays off here.

1. When is a bounded function f Riemann integrable on [a,b]?

Integrability means the sup of all lower sums and the inf of all upper sums coincide at a single shared value.

2. What does the Cauchy Criterion for Integrability let you avoid computing directly?

The criterion trades a hard sup/inf-over-all-partitions computation for finding just one sufficiently fine partition per ε.

3. Why does the continuous-implies-integrable proof specifically need uniform continuity rather than ordinary continuity?

Uniform continuity's one-δ-fits-all-of-[a,b] guarantee is exactly what lets a single partition control every subinterval's oscillation simultaneously.

4. Fill in the blank: the ___ for Integrability tests integrability via a single partition with small U(f,P) − L(f,P), rather than computing sup/inf over all partitions.

Cauchy Criterion is the practical test proved and used in this lesson.

5. Fill in the blank: continuous functions on [a,b] are integrable because continuity on a compact set upgrades to ___, giving one δ that works across the whole partition.

Uniform continuity, from Unit 4 Lesson 24, is the exact property that makes the integrability proof go through.
