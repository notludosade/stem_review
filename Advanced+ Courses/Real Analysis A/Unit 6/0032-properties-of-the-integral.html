Properties of the Integral

Linearity, additivity over subintervals, and monotonicity — the algebra rules AP Calc BC used freely, now derived from the Cauchy Criterion.

← Lesson 31 Lesson 33 → Contents Glossary →

Every property in this lesson has the same shape: reduce a claim about the integral to a Cauchy-Criterion-style estimate (Lesson 31) involving upper and lower sums. Once the pattern clicks for one property, the rest follow the same template.

Theorem — Linearity If f, g are integrable on [a,b] and k ∈ ℝ, then f + g and kf are integrable, with ∫ab (f+g) = ∫ab f + ∫ab g and ∫ab kf = k∫ab f.
Proof sketch — additivity in the integrand Given ε > 0, the Cauchy Criterion gives partitions P₁, P₂ with U(f,P₁) − L(f,P₁) < ε/2 and U(g,P₂) − L(g,P₂) < ε/2. Let P = P₁ ∪ P₂ (common refinement). On each subinterval, sup(f+g) ≤ sup(f) + sup(g) and inf(f+g) ≥ inf(f) + inf(g) (sup/inf of a sum is controlled by, though not always equal to, the sums of the sups/infs), so U(f+g, P) − L(f+g, P) ≤ [U(f,P) − L(f,P)] + [U(g,P) − L(g,P)] < ε. By the Cauchy Criterion, f+g is integrable, and squeezing U(f+g,P) between the sums of the upper/lower bounds for f and g pins the integral's value at ∫f + ∫g. ∎
Theorem — Additivity Over Subintervals If f is integrable on [a,b] and a < c < b, then f is integrable on [a,c] and [c,b], and ∫ab f = ∫ac f + ∫cb f.
Why including c in every partition is the key step Given any partition P of [a,b], refining it to include the point c (Lesson 30's Refinement Lemma guarantees this only decreases U(f,P) − L(f,P) or keeps it the same) splits P into a partition of [a,c] and a partition of [c,b]. The Cauchy Criterion estimate for [a,b] then splits cleanly into the sum of the two estimates, giving integrability on each piece and additivity of the integral's value.
Theorem — Monotonicity If f, g are integrable on [a,b] and f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x ∈ [a,b], then ∫ab f ≤ ∫ab g.
Proof For any partition P, each subinterval's sup of f is ≤ the sup of g (since f ≤ g pointwise), so U(f,P) ≤ U(g,P) for every P. Taking the inf over all P on both sides, U(f) ≤ U(g), i.e. ∫f ≤ ∫g (using integrability to identify U(f) = ∫f and U(g) = ∫g). ∎
Corollary — A Bound on the Integral If |f(x)| ≤ K for all x ∈ [a,b], then |∫ab f| ≤ K(b−a). (Apply monotonicity to −K ≤ f(x) ≤ K, using linearity to compute ∫ab K = K(b−a).)

1. What proof technique underlies all three properties in this lesson (linearity, additivity, monotonicity)?

Every property in this lesson traces back to controlling U(f,P) − L(f,P), the same Cauchy Criterion estimate from Lesson 31.

2. In the additivity-over-subintervals proof, why is refining a partition to include the point c the key move?

Adding c gives exactly the split needed to treat [a,c] and [c,b] as separate integrability problems whose estimates sum correctly.

3. Why does f(x) ≤ g(x) for all x imply ∫f ≤ ∫g?

The pointwise inequality lifts to an inequality of every upper sum, and then to the upper integrals themselves.

4. Fill in the blank: the property ∫(f+g) = ∫f + ∫g and ∫(kf) = k∫f together are called ___ of the integral.

Linearity is the combined additive-and-scalar property proved at the start of this lesson.

5. Fill in the blank: the property that f ≤ g pointwise implies ∫f ≤ ∫g is called ___ of the integral.

Monotonicity is the order-preserving property proved via comparing upper sums directly.
