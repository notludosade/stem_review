STEM+ · Real Analysis A

Real Analysis A

The fundamentals of real analysis — from the field and order axioms, through sequences, topology, and continuity, to a rigorous proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Requires AP Calculus BC.

Unit 1 — Foundations
Lesson 1

Why Real Analysis Exists

Calculus tells you what's true. Real analysis proves it — and in the process exposes exactly where calculus's usual hand-waving can quietly fail.

 Lesson 2

Field and Order Axioms

The short list of rules that + and · and < have to obey — everything else in this course is proved from these, plus one more axiom still to come.

 Lesson 3

Absolute Value and the Triangle Inequality

The single inequality that every "gets arbitrarily close" argument in this course will secretly be built from.

 Lesson 4

Bounds, Supremum, and Infimum

The vocabulary needed to finally state, precisely, what it means for the real numbers to have "no gaps."

 Lesson 5

The Completeness Axiom

The one axiom ℝ has and ℚ doesn't — precisely naming the "no gaps" property Lesson 1 promised.

 Lesson 6

Consequences of Completeness: The Archimedean Property and Density of ℚ

The first two real theorems this course proves — both direct payoffs of the axiom from Lesson 5, and both quietly assumed throughout ordinary calculus.
Unit 2 — Sequences and Limits
Lesson 7

The Formal Definition of a Sequence Limit

Turning "gets arbitrarily close" into a statement with quantifiers, checkable step by step with no picture required.

 Lesson 8

Proving Limits Directly from the Definition

The scratch-work-then-proof pattern behind every epsilon-N argument you'll write in this course.

 Lesson 9

Limit Laws for Sequences

Prove sum, product, and quotient rules once, from the definition — then never write an epsilon-N proof for a combined sequence again.

 Lesson 10

Monotone Sequences and the Monotone Convergence Theorem

The first theorem in this course that proves a sequence converges without ever having to guess its limit first.

 Lesson 11

Subsequences and Limit Points

What's left to talk about when a sequence doesn't converge as a whole, but part of it still settles down.

 Lesson 12

The Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem

Boundedness alone — with no monotonicity, no known limit — is still enough to guarantee a convergent piece exists somewhere inside.

 Lesson 13

Cauchy Sequences

A way to test for convergence using only the sequence's own terms — no candidate limit required, ever.
Unit 3 — Topology of the Real Line
Lesson 14

Open and Closed Sets

The two basic shapes a subset of ℝ can have — and why they are not opposites of each other.

 Lesson 15

Limit Points and Closure

Extending Unit 2's "limit point of a sequence" to sets in general — and the operation that seals up any leftover gaps.

 Lesson 16

Compactness

The property that will make continuous functions in Unit 4 behave as well as possible — attaining maximums, not just approaching them.

 Lesson 17

The Heine-Borel Theorem

A second, completely different-looking definition of compactness — proved equivalent to the sequential one from Lesson 16.

 Lesson 18

Connectedness and the Real Line

The topological property behind "no gaps to jump across" — and the hidden engine of the Intermediate Value Theorem.
Unit 4 — Limits and Continuity of Functions
Lesson 19

The Epsilon-Delta Definition of a Functional Limit

AP Calc BC's "as x approaches a" made precise — and connected directly to Unit 2's sequence limits.

 Lesson 20

Continuity

"You can draw it without lifting your pen" made precise, plus a second, sequential definition that turns out to say exactly the same thing.

 Lesson 21

Combinations of Continuous Functions

Why "it's a sum of continuous functions" has been a fully legitimate proof step all along.

 Lesson 22

The Intermediate Value Theorem

AP Calc BC's most-used existence theorem, finally proved from first principles instead of appealed to as "obvious."

 Lesson 23

The Extreme Value Theorem

Why a continuous function on a closed, bounded interval doesn't just approach a maximum — it actually attains one.

 Lesson 24

Uniform Continuity

The closing lesson of Unit 4: a single δ that works everywhere at once — and why compactness is again exactly what makes that possible.
Unit 5 — Differentiation
Lesson 25

The Derivative as a Rigorous Limit

The difference quotient, finally built on Unit 4's epsilon-delta functional limit instead of an intuitive "instantaneous rate of change."

 Lesson 26

Differentiability, Continuity, and Rules of Differentiation

Why every differentiable function is continuous, why the reverse fails, and the product/quotient rules proved from the definition.

 Lesson 27

Rolle's Theorem

If a function starts and ends at the same height, it must have a flat spot somewhere in between — the special case that unlocks the Mean Value Theorem.

 Lesson 28

The Mean Value Theorem

Tilt Rolle's Theorem's picture, and the "flat spot" becomes "a tangent parallel to the secant line" — one algebraic trick away.

 Lesson 29

Applications of the Mean Value Theorem

The closing lesson of Unit 5: proving, at last, why a positive derivative actually implies an increasing function.
Unit 6 — Riemann Integration
Lesson 30

Partitions and Upper and Lower Sums

Before the integral can be a number, it needs bracketing sums that squeeze it from above and below — built from nothing but sup and inf.

 Lesson 31

The Riemann Integral

The definite integral, defined at last as a single squeezed number — and a clean test for when that squeeze actually pins one down.

 Lesson 32

Properties of the Integral

Linearity, additivity over subintervals, and monotonicity — the algebra rules AP Calc BC used freely, now derived from the Cauchy Criterion.

 Lesson 33

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Differentiation and integration, proved — at last, rigorously — to be inverse operations.

 Lesson 34

Capstone — From Axioms to the Fundamental Theorem

The whole course, traced as one dependency chain: how eleven field and order axioms end up proving that differentiation undoes integration.
