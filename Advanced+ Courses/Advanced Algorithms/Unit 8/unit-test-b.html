1. "Does graph G have a Hamiltonian cycle?" has no known polynomial-time algorithm, yet the problem is in NP. What makes it a member of NP?

NP membership only requires that a "yes" instance's certificate be checkable quickly — walking a proposed cycle and confirming it visits every vertex once is an O(n) check, regardless of how hard finding the cycle is.

2. A polynomial-time reduction A ≤ₚ B transforms every instance of A into an instance of B in polynomial time, preserving the yes/no answer. Answer both parts:

(a) What does a reduction A ≤ₚ B never do?

(b) Fill in the blank: if A ≤ₚ B and B turns out to have a polynomial-time algorithm, then chaining it after the reduction gives A one too. Reading this contrapositively is how a reduction transfers ___ from a problem already known to lack an efficient algorithm onto a new one.

(a) The reduction only transforms the input — solving either problem outright would defeat the entire purpose of comparing their difficulty without an algorithm for either. (b) If A has no polynomial-time algorithm, B can't either, or chaining B's (hypothetical) algorithm after the reduction would solve A too — a contradiction, which is exactly how hardness transfers.

3. A problem has been proven NP-hard, but it has not been shown to be in NP itself. What keeps it from also being called NP-complete?

NP-hardness alone only says every NP problem reduces to it; being NP-complete additionally requires the problem to sit inside NP as well, with its own polynomial-time-checkable certificates.

4. Cook and Levin proved SAT is NP-complete. Answer both parts:

(a) What does the Cook-Levin construction directly encode as a Boolean formula?

(b) Fill in the blank: because this is a one-time foundational construction, Cook-Levin is described as the ___ every later NP-completeness proof builds from, directly or through a chain of reductions.

(a) Cook-Levin's proof doesn't reduce from one other named problem — it mechanically encodes any NP verifier's computation, at every time slice, into one Boolean formula satisfiable exactly when some certificate makes the verifier accept. (b) Once SAT is confirmed NP-hard this way, every later proof only needs to reduce from SAT or an already-confirmed NP-complete problem, never repeating the machine-simulation argument.

5. In the 3-SAT ≤ₚ Independent Set construction, cross-triangle edges connect a literal (like x₁ in one clause's triangle) to its negation (¬x₁ in another clause's triangle). What role do these specific edges play?

Without these cross-triangle edges, an independent set could pick both x₁ (in one clause) and ¬x₁ (in another) — the edges rule this out, so a valid size-k independent set corresponds to a consistent variable assignment.

6. Vertex Cover's NP-completeness required no new from-scratch gadget construction once Independent Set was shown NP-complete. Answer both parts:

(a) Which already-established fact, combined with Independent Set's newly-proven NP-hardness, makes Vertex Cover NP-hard?

(b) Fill in the blank: Vertex Cover is also confirmed to be in NP, since a candidate cover is a ___ checkable in polynomial time by confirming every edge has at least one chosen endpoint.

(a) Transitivity of reductions lets Vertex Cover inherit hardness through the existing Independent Set ≤ₚ Vertex Cover reduction, without any new construction. (b) Being both NP-hard and in NP (via this easily-checkable certificate) is exactly what makes Vertex Cover NP-complete, not merely NP-hard.

7. In the Hamiltonian Cycle ≤ₚ TSP reduction, edges of the original graph G get weight 1 and all other edges of the completed graph get weight 2. Answer both parts:

(a) Why can no tour on this constructed graph ever have total weight less than |V|?

(b) Fill in the blank: this reduction resolves the claim, first deferred when bitmask dynamic programming solved TSP exactly but only in exponential time, that TSP is ___-hard.

(a) Weight is summed over exactly |V| edges no matter which tour is chosen, so no combination of weight-1-or-greater edges can total below |V|. (b) Since Hamiltonian Cycle is already NP-complete and reduces to TSP's decision version in polynomial time, TSP inherits NP-hardness — the precise reason bitmask DP's exponential runtime was never a contradiction.

8. For a maximization problem, how does the definition of a c-approximation differ from the minimization case?

The inequality direction flips for maximization: a c-approximation still runs in polynomial time, but guarantees a value of at least OPT/c rather than a cost of at most c·OPT.

9. For the greedy Vertex Cover algorithm's matching M of picked edges, OPT ≥ |M| always holds. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does covering all of M's edges require at least |M| distinct vertices in any vertex cover, including the optimal one?

(b) Fill in the blank: the algorithm's own cover has size exactly ___ |M|, since it adds both endpoints of every edge it picks — which is why |C| ≤ 2·OPT.

(a) Since no two edges in M share a vertex, "covering" all of them one distinct vertex at a time is the fewest any valid cover could possibly manage. (b) |C| = 2|M|, and since OPT ≥ |M|, this gives |C| ≤ 2·OPT — a 2-approximation from nothing more than a matching's disjointness.

10. According to the course's closing capstone, which three proof techniques recur across the entire course in different "costumes," from Unit 1 through Unit 8?

Exchange arguments (Unit 3's greedy correctness, Huffman coding), potential functions (Unit 1's amortized analysis, Union-Find, KMP), and reductions (Unit 8's entire final act) are the three techniques the capstone identifies as recurring in different forms throughout the course.
