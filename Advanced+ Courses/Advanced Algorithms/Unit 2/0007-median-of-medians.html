Median of Medians

Quickselect finds the k-th smallest element in expected O(n) time, but a bad pivot makes it O(n²) in the worst case. Median-of-medians fixes that by spending extra work choosing a pivot that's provably good — worst-case O(n), not just expected.

Finding the k-th smallest element of an unsorted array (the selection problem) can be done in Θ(n log n) by sorting first, but that's more work than necessary — you don't need the whole order, just one position in it. Quickselect (a close relative of quicksort) partitions around a random pivot and recurses into only the side containing the k-th element, giving expected O(n) time. But like quicksort, an adversarial or unlucky sequence of pivots makes quickselect degrade to Θ(n²). This lesson builds an algorithm that chooses its pivot deliberately, guaranteeing Θ(n) worst-case — at the cost of a cleverer, more expensive-looking pivot selection step that turns out not to hurt the asymptotics at all.

The algorithm

Algorithm — median of medians (select) 
function select(arr, k):
    if length(arr) <= 5:
        return k-th smallest of arr by direct sort
    // 1. Divide into groups of 5, find each group's median
    groups = partition arr into ceil(n/5) groups of 5 (last group may be smaller)
    medians = [median of each group, found by direct sort of that group of ≤5]
    // 2. Recursively find the median of the medians — this is the pivot
    pivot = select(medians, length(medians)/2)
    // 3. Partition arr around pivot, then recurse into the correct side
    (less, equal, greater) = partition arr around pivot
    if k < length(less):
        return select(less, k)
    else if k < length(less) + length(equal):
        return pivot
    else:
        return select(greater, k - length(less) - length(equal))
Why groups of exactly 5 Groups of 5 are the smallest odd group size that makes the following guarantee work out to a favorable constant (shown below): at least roughly 3/10 of all elements are provably ≤ the pivot, and at least roughly 3/10 are provably ≥ it — guaranteeing the partition step never recurses into more than about 7/10 of the array. Groups of 3 give a guarantee too weak to produce a shrinking recurrence at all; groups of 7 or more work but add unnecessary overhead in the direct-sort step. 5 is the sweet spot the original 1973 paper (Blum, Floyd, Pratt, Rivest, Tarjan) landed on.
Proving at least 3n/10 elements are ≤ the pivot Consider the ⌈n/5⌉ group medians. By construction, the pivot is the median of these medians, so at least half of the group medians (roughly ⌈n/5⌉/2) are ≤ pivot. For each such group, since its median is ≤ pivot, at least 2 more elements in that same group of 5 (the two below its own median) are also ≤ its median, hence ≤ pivot. So each of those groups contributes at least 3 elements (the median plus 2 below it) that are ≤ pivot. Multiplying: at least 3 · (⌈n/5⌉/2) ≈ 3n/10 elements of the whole array are ≤ pivot. The symmetric argument gives at least 3n/10 elements ≥ pivot.
Runtime — solving the recurrence The 3n/10 guarantee means the partition step recurses into at most n − 3n/10 = 7n/10 elements. The full recurrence has two recursive calls: T(n) ≤ T(n/5) [finding the median of medians] + T(7n/10) [recursing into the larger side] + Θ(n) [group medians, partitioning]. This doesn't fit the Master Theorem's single-branching-factor form (Lesson 2's territory, not Lesson 3's) — solve it by guessing T(n) ≤ cn and verifying by substitution: T(n) ≤ c(n/5) + c(7n/10) + Θ(n) = c(9n/10) + Θ(n). Since 9/10 < 1, the coefficient on n shrinks each substitution, and the Θ(n) term can be absorbed into a large-enough c — confirming T(n) = O(n). Worst-case linear time, with no randomness required anywhere in the argument.
Why this matters beyond selection itself Median-of-medians is this course's first example of a technique that trades a small constant-factor overhead (the extra pass to compute group medians) for a worst-case guarantee that a simpler algorithm (quickselect) can only offer in expectation. That trade — accept more work per step, in exchange for eliminating an adversarial worst case — recurs throughout the rest of this course, most visibly when balanced trees (Unit 6) trade rebalancing overhead for a worst-case height guarantee that an unbalanced binary search tree cannot offer.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What guarantee does median-of-medians provide that plain quickselect (random pivot) does not?

Quickselect's O(n) is only an expectation over random pivots; median-of-medians deliberately chooses a provably good pivot, eliminating the adversarial worst case entirely.

2. Why does each group with a median ≤ pivot contribute at least 3 elements (not just 1) that are ≤ pivot?

Within a group of 5, the two elements below the group's own median are automatically ≤ that median, and hence ≤ pivot whenever the group's median itself is ≤ pivot — giving 3 elements per such group, not just the median alone.

3. Why can't the recurrence T(n) = T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + Θ(n) be solved directly with the Master Theorem?

Just like the T(n/3)+T(2n/3)+n example from Lesson 2, unevenly-sized recursive calls fall outside the Master Theorem's form — here solved instead by guess-and-verify substitution.

4. Fill in the blank: median-of-medians divides the array into groups of ___, the smallest odd size that makes the 3n/10 guarantee work out favorably.

Groups of 5 are the classic choice from the original Blum-Floyd-Pratt-Rivest-Tarjan paper, balancing a strong-enough guarantee against the overhead of directly sorting each group.
