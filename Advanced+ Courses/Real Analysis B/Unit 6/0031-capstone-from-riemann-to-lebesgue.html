Capstone — From Riemann to Lebesgue

The whole course, traced as one dependency chain: how relaxing "uniform" to "pointwise," then abandoning Riemann's integral altogether, builds a theory strong enough to integrate through any pointwise limit.

← Lesson 30 Contents Glossary →

This closing lesson adds no new theorems. Instead, it retraces the entire logical chain built across 31 lessons — showing how two seemingly separate projects, generalizing convergence and rebuilding the integral, converge on the same destination: a body of analysis where limits and integrals can be swapped under hypotheses far weaker than Real Analysis A ever needed.

The dependency chain

Unit 1 — Uniform Convergence Pointwise convergence (Lesson 1) is too weak to preserve continuity or integrability; the Weierstrass M-Test (Lesson 2) gives a practical tool for proving the stronger notion, uniform convergence. Uniform convergence preserves continuity (Lesson 3) and lets a limit pass through a Riemann integral (Lesson 4) — but Lesson 4 flagged a "moving bump" counterexample as beyond Riemann's reach to construct, and differentiation (Lesson 5) showed uniform convergence of fₙ alone isn't even enough there; f'ₙ must converge uniformly too.
Unit 2 — Power Series Radius and interval of convergence (Lesson 6) locate where a power series converges at all; uniform convergence of power series (Lesson 7) on compact subintervals is what makes term-by-term differentiation and integration (Lesson 8) valid, applying Unit 1's machinery directly. Taylor series and analytic functions (Lesson 9) push this further — until Lesson 10 shows even infinite smoothness doesn't guarantee a Taylor series converges to its own function, the sharpest limitation uncovered before Unit 5's deeper structural failures.
Unit 3 — Metric Spaces Definitions and examples (Lesson 11) generalize ℝ's distance structure to arbitrary sets; open and closed sets (Lesson 12), convergence and completeness (Lesson 13), and continuity (Lesson 14) all restate Real Analysis A's ℝ-specific machinery in this general language. Compactness (Lesson 15) proves the harder sequentially-compact-implies-compact direction by citing total boundedness plus completeness — the abstract version of Real Analysis A's Heine-Borel — and connectedness (Lesson 16) generalizes the Intermediate Value Theorem's engine.
Unit 4 — Multivariable Differentiation Partial derivatives (Lesson 17) generalize the one-variable derivative naively, but the total derivative (Lesson 18) is what actually deserves the name — a single linear map capturing the best affine approximation, built on Unit 3's metric-space continuity. The Jacobian and Chain Rule (Lesson 19) and Clairaut's Theorem on mixed partials (Lesson 20) extend Real Analysis A's calculus rules rigorously; the Inverse and Implicit Function Theorems (Lesson 21) close the unit by citing the Contraction Mapping Theorem for their harder direction, the same "prove what's reachable, cite the rest" pattern this course uses throughout.
Unit 5 — Lebesgue Measure Lesson 22 is the hinge of the entire course: Dirichlet's function isn't Riemann integrable at all, and worse, a pointwise limit of Riemann integrable functions can fail to be integrable — the exact failure mode Unit 1's uniform-convergence machinery was built to avoid, now shown to be unavoidable within Riemann's framework. Outer measure (Lesson 23) gives every subset of ℝ a notion of size; the Carathéodory criterion (Lesson 24) isolates exactly the measurable sets where that size is countably additive (Lesson 25); and measurable functions (Lesson 26) close the unit by proving the exact repair Lesson 22 needed — pointwise limits of measurable functions stay measurable, unconditionally.
Unit 6 — The Lebesgue Integral The integral is built from the ground up: simple functions first (Lesson 27), where Dirichlet's function finally integrates to 0 exactly because m(ℚ)=0; then general measurable functions via positive/negative parts (Lesson 28), with a proof that the new integral agrees with Riemann's everywhere the old one applied — so nothing already known is lost. The Monotone Convergence Theorem (Lesson 29) is the tool both of Lesson 28's deferred debts needed, and the Dominated Convergence Theorem (Lesson 30) finally builds Lesson 4's moving bump in full, showing exactly why domination — not uniformity — is the right hypothesis for swapping limits and integrals.
Why this chain is the actual point of the course Real Analysis A proved that Riemann's integral and derivative are inverse operations, built from eleven axioms and completeness. This course took two routes past that foundation. Units 1–4 generalized the machinery — from uniform convergence, to power series, to arbitrary metric spaces, to several variables — without ever questioning the Riemann integral itself. Units 5–6 questioned it directly, and found it structurally incomplete: no amount of uniform convergence can rescue a theory where a pointwise limit of integrable functions escapes integrability altogether. The Lebesgue integral is not a generalization for its own sake — it is the exact repair that Unit 1's "moving bump" and Unit 2's Taylor-series failures were both, in hindsight, symptoms of. MCT and DCT replace "uniform convergence" with "monotone" or "dominated," strictly weaker hypotheses, because the integral underneath them is finally strong enough to bear the weight.
