The Lebesgue Integral and Comparison with Riemann

Lesson 27 integrated nonnegative functions. This lesson allows negative values, pins down exactly which functions are integrable, and asks the reassuring question: does this new integral still agree with the old one, wherever the old one worked?

← Lesson 27 Lesson 29 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Positive and Negative Parts For measurable f: ℝ → ℝ, let f⁺ = max(f,0) and f⁻ = max(−f,0). Both are nonnegative and measurable, f = f⁺ − f⁻, and |f| = f⁺ + f⁻.
Definition — Lebesgue Integrability f is Lebesgue integrable on a measurable set E if ∫_Ef⁺dm < ∞ and ∫_Ef⁻dm < ∞. In that case, define

∫_Ef dm = ∫_Ef⁺dm − ∫_Ef⁻dm.
Lemma — Additivity Over a Domain Partition If g ≥ 0 is measurable and A is a measurable set, then ∫_Ag dm + ∫_{Aᶜ}g dm = ∫_ℝ g dm.
Proof (≤): let φ, ψ be simple with φ ≤ g·χ_A and ψ ≤ g·χ_{Aᶜ}. Then φ+ψ is simple, and φ+ψ ≤ g everywhere (on A, φ+ψ = φ ≤ g, since ψ=0 is forced off Aᶜ... more precisely, since φ≤gχ_A means φ=0 off A, and ψ≤gχ_{Aᶜ} means ψ=0 off Aᶜ, so on A: φ+ψ=φ≤g, and on Aᶜ: φ+ψ=ψ≤g). By Lesson 27's simple-function linearity, ∫φdm+∫ψdm = ∫(φ+ψ)dm ≤ ∫gdm, since φ+ψ is a valid candidate in the supremum defining ∫gdm. Taking the supremum over all such φ, then over all such ψ, gives ∫_Agdm + ∫_{Aᶜ}gdm ≤ ∫gdm.

(≥): let χ be any simple function with χ ≤ g. Set φ = χ·χ_A and ψ = χ·χ_{Aᶜ}, both simple, with φ ≤ g·χ_A and ψ ≤ g·χ_{Aᶜ} (since χ≤g), and χ = φ+ψ (A, Aᶜ partition the domain). By simple-function linearity, ∫χdm = ∫φdm + ∫ψdm ≤ ∫_Agdm + ∫_{Aᶜ}gdm, since φ, ψ are valid candidates in their respective suprema. This holds for every simple χ ≤ g, so taking the supremum over χ gives ∫gdm ≤ ∫_Agdm + ∫_{Aᶜ}gdm.

Combining both directions gives equality. ∎
Why this lemma is the elementary case, not the hard one This lemma splits one function g over a partition of the domain into A and Aᶜ — both directions reduce directly to gluing or restricting simple functions, which Lesson 27 already handles exactly. The genuinely hard fact — that ∫(f+g)dm = ∫fdm + ∫gdm for two different nonnegative measurable functions f and g over the same domain — cannot be proved this elementarily; it is a corollary of the Monotone Convergence Theorem, next lesson.
Theorem — Integrability via |f|, and the Triangle Inequality f is Lebesgue integrable on E if and only if ∫_E|f|dm < ∞, and in that case |∫_Ef dm| ≤ ∫_E|f| dm.
Proof Let A = {x∈E : f(x)≥0}, so f⁺ = |f|·χ_A and f⁻ = |f|·χ_{Aᶜ} exactly (pointwise: on A, f⁺=f=|f| and f⁻=0; on Aᶜ, f⁺=0 and f⁻=−f=|f|). By the lemma above (applied with g=|f|·χ_E), ∫_Ef⁺dm + ∫_Ef⁻dm = ∫_E|f|dm.

If f is integrable, both terms on the left are finite, so their sum ∫_E|f|dm is finite. Conversely, if ∫_E|f|dm < ∞, then since 0≤f⁺≤|f| and 0≤f⁻≤|f|, monotonicity (Lesson 27) gives ∫_Ef⁺dm ≤ ∫_E|f|dm < ∞ and likewise for f⁻, so both are finite and f is integrable.

For the triangle inequality: |∫_Efdm| = |∫_Ef⁺dm − ∫_Ef⁻dm| ≤ ∫_Ef⁺dm + ∫_Ef⁻dm = ∫_E|f|dm. ∎
Lemma — Bounded Functions on Finite-Measure Sets Are Integrable If f is measurable, |f| ≤ M on E, and m(E) < ∞, then f is Lebesgue integrable on E.
Proof The constant function M·χ_E is simple with ∫_E(M·χ_E)dm = M·m(E) < ∞ (finite since m(E)<∞). Since |f| ≤ M·χ_E on E, monotonicity gives ∫_E|f|dm ≤ M·m(E) < ∞. By the theorem above, f is integrable. ∎
Theorem (stated) — Riemann Integrable Implies Lebesgue Integrable, with Equal Value If f is Riemann integrable on [a,b], then f is Lebesgue integrable on [a,b], and ∫_a^b f(x)dx (Riemann) = ∫_{[a,b]} f dm (Lebesgue).
Why this theorem is stated here but proved next lesson Riemann integrability makes f bounded, and [a,b] has finite measure b−a, so the lemma above already shows f is Lebesgue integrable — that much is immediate. Equality of the two integral values, though, comes from sandwiching f between increasing lower step functions φₙ and decreasing upper step functions ψₙ built from a refining sequence of partitions (exactly Lesson 23's interval-splitting idea, now applied function-wise), and passing to the limit inside the integral. That limit-passing step — "the integral of an increasing limit is the limit of the integrals" — is precisely the Monotone Convergence Theorem, proved next lesson. The full proof resumes immediately once that theorem is in hand.
Why this comparison matters going forward Every Riemann integral computed in prerequisite calculus, and every Riemann integral proved in Real Analysis A, remains valid and unchanged under the Lebesgue integral — nothing is lost. What's gained is everything Riemann integration couldn't reach: Dirichlet's function (Lesson 27), and, starting next lesson, integrals of limits that Riemann's framework couldn't even discuss.

1. When is f Lebesgue integrable on E?

Both the positive and negative parts of f must have finite integral for f itself to be integrable.

2. Why doesn't the Domain-Partition Additivity Lemma need the Monotone Convergence Theorem?

Splitting a single function over a domain partition is elementary; combining two different functions over the same domain is the harder fact needing MCT.

3. What does the Riemann-Lebesgue comparison theorem's proof still need, deferred to next lesson?

The step-function sandwich reduces the comparison to exactly the limit-interchange the Monotone Convergence Theorem supplies.

4. Fill in the blank: a measurable function bounded by M on a set of ___ measure is automatically Lebesgue integrable.

Boundedness plus finite measure bounds ∫|f|dm by M times that finite measure.

5. Fill in the blank: the theorem needed to finish the Riemann-Lebesgue comparison proof is the ___ Convergence Theorem.

The Monotone Convergence Theorem is exactly what lets the limit of the step-function integrals equal the integral of the limit.
