The Jacobian and the Chain Rule

Once differentiability means "best linear approximation," composing two differentiable functions should compose their linear approximations — and it does, exactly, as a matrix product.

← Lesson 18 Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

Theorem — The Multivariable Chain Rule Let g: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ be differentiable at a, and let f: ℝᵐ → ℝᵏ be differentiable at g(a). Then f∘g is differentiable at a, and

D(f∘g)(a) = Df(g(a)) · Dg(a),

a product of a k×m matrix and an m×n matrix, giving the k×n Jacobian of f∘g.
Proof Write b = g(a), L = Dg(a), M = Df(b). By differentiability of g at a and f at b: g(a+h) = g(a) + Lh + |h|·ε₁(h), and f(b+k) = f(b) + Mk + |k|·ε₂(k), where ε₁(h) → 0 as h → 0 and ε₂(k) → 0 as k → 0.

Let k = k(h) = g(a+h) − g(a) = Lh + |h|·ε₁(h), so k(h) → 0 as h → 0 (g is differentiable, hence continuous at a, by Lesson 18). Then

f(g(a+h)) = f(b + k(h)) = f(b) + M·k(h) + |k(h)|·ε₂(k(h)) = f(b) + M(Lh + |h|ε₁(h)) + |k(h)|·ε₂(k(h)).

So f(g(a+h)) − f(g(a)) − MLh = M(|h|ε₁(h)) + |k(h)|·ε₂(k(h)). Dividing by |h|: the first term's norm is bounded by |M|·|ε₁(h)| → 0. For the second term, |k(h)|/|h| ≤ |L| + |ε₁(h)|, a bounded quantity as h → 0, while ε₂(k(h)) → 0 since k(h) → 0 — so this term also vanishes relative to |h|. Hence [f(g(a+h)) − f(g(a)) − MLh] / |h| → 0, meaning f∘g is differentiable at a with total derivative ML = Df(g(a))Dg(a). ∎
Why n = m = k = 1 recovers single-variable calculus When every space involved is ℝ, the Jacobian "matrices" Df(g(a)) and Dg(a) are 1×1 — ordinary numbers f'(g(a)) and g'(a) — and matrix multiplication is ordinary multiplication. The theorem above becomes (f∘g)'(a) = f'(g(a))·g'(a): the single-variable chain rule, now understood as the special case of composing linear approximations in one dimension.
Example: computing a Jacobian product Let g(r,θ) = (r cos θ, r sin θ) (polar to Cartesian), and let f(x,y) = x² + y². Then

Dg(r,θ) = [[cos θ, −r sin θ], [sin θ, r cos θ]], and Df(x,y) = [2x, 2y] (a 1×2 row matrix).

At a point (r,θ), Df(g(r,θ)) = [2r cos θ, 2r sin θ]. The chain rule gives D(f∘g)(r,θ) = Df(g(r,θ))·Dg(r,θ) = [2r cos²θ + 2r sin²θ, −2r² sin θ cos θ + 2r² sin θ cos θ] = [2r, 0] — matching the direct computation that f(g(r,θ)) = r², whose partial derivative with respect to r is 2r and with respect to θ is 0.

1. What does the Multivariable Chain Rule say D(f∘g)(a) equals?

The Jacobian of the outer function, evaluated at g(a), is multiplied by the Jacobian of the inner function at a — order matters, since matrix multiplication is not commutative.

2. In the proof, why does |k(h)|/|h| stay bounded as h → 0?

This bound is exactly what's needed so that ε₂(k(h)), which vanishes as k(h)→0, still forces the whole second error term to vanish relative to |h|.

3. What does the chain rule reduce to when n = m = k = 1?

In one dimension, the Jacobian matrices are just numbers, and matrix multiplication becomes ordinary multiplication — recovering the familiar chain rule exactly.

4. Fill in the blank: the matrix representing Df(a) via the partial derivatives is called the ___ matrix.

Jacobian is the standard name for this matrix of partial derivatives.

5. Fill in the blank: D(f∘g)(a) = Df(g(a))·Dg(a) is the multivariable ___ rule.

This is the chain rule, generalized to composition of differentiable maps between Euclidean spaces.
