Clairaut's Theorem and Higher-Order Partials

Differentiating twice in two different orders looks like it must give the same answer — and usually does. This lesson pins down exactly when that's guaranteed, and shows the guarantee can genuinely fail.

← Lesson 19 Lesson 21 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Second-Order Partial Derivatives Given f: ℝⁿ → ℝ, the second-order partial derivative ∂²f/∂xᵢ∂xⱼ is defined as ∂/∂xᵢ (∂f/∂xⱼ) — differentiate with respect to xⱼ first, then with respect to xᵢ.

A natural question: is ∂²f/∂x∂y always the same as ∂²f/∂y∂x? Surprisingly, the answer is no — not without an extra hypothesis.

Example (computation omitted): mixed partials can disagree Let f(x,y) = xy(x²−y²)/(x²+y²) for (x,y) ≠ (0,0), and f(0,0) = 0. A direct (if tedious) computation from the definitions gives ∂²f/∂x∂y(0,0) = 1 and ∂²f/∂y∂x(0,0) = −1 — the two mixed partial derivatives exist at the origin but are not equal. The full computation is in the source reading below.
Why this is possible at all Both second-order partials exist at the origin in this example — the failure isn't that a limit fails to exist, but that two limits which look like they should coincide simply don't, because neither mixed partial is continuous at the origin. This is the exact hypothesis the next theorem adds back.
Theorem — Clairaut's Theorem (Equality of Mixed Partials) If ∂²f/∂x∂y and ∂²f/∂y∂x both exist in a neighborhood of a point and are continuous at that point, then ∂²f/∂x∂y = ∂²f/∂y∂x there.
Proof sketch Fix a point (a,b) and consider the second difference Δ(h,k) = f(a+h,b+k) − f(a+h,b) − f(a,b+k) + f(a,b) for small h, k ≠ 0. Viewing Δ(h,k) as φ(a+h) − φ(a) for φ(x) = f(x,b+k) − f(x,b), the single-variable Mean Value Theorem gives Δ(h,k) = h·[∂f/∂x(a+θh,b+k) − ∂f/∂x(a+θh,b)] for some θ ∈ (0,1); applying the Mean Value Theorem once more, this time in y, gives Δ(h,k) = hk·∂²f/∂y∂x(a+θh, b+ψk) for some ψ ∈ (0,1). Running the identical argument with the roles of x and y swapped instead produces Δ(h,k) = hk·∂²f/∂x∂y(a+θ'h, b+ψ'k) for possibly different θ', ψ' ∈ (0,1). Dividing both expressions by hk and letting h, k → 0, continuity of both mixed partials at (a,b) forces both right-hand sides to converge to the value of the corresponding mixed partial exactly at (a,b) — and since both expressions equal the same quantity Δ(h,k)/hk, the two limits must agree. So ∂²f/∂x∂y(a,b) = ∂²f/∂y∂x(a,b). The full accounting of the two applications of the Mean Value Theorem is in the source reading below.
Why continuity is exactly the missing ingredient The proof genuinely needs continuity of the mixed partials at (a,b) to pass from "Δ(h,k)/hk equals the mixed partial at some nearby point" to "equals the mixed partial exactly at (a,b)" as h, k → 0. In the xy(x²−y²)/(x²+y²) example, both mixed partials exist everywhere but are discontinuous at the origin — exactly where they fail to agree.
Example f(x,y) = x³y² has ∂f/∂x = 3x²y², so ∂²f/∂y∂x = 6x²y; and ∂f/∂y = 2x³y, so ∂²f/∂x∂y = 6x²y. Both mixed partials are polynomials, hence continuous everywhere, and indeed agree — consistent with Clairaut's Theorem.

1. What can cause the two mixed partial derivatives of a function to disagree at a point?

The xy(x²−y²)/(x²+y²) example has both mixed partials existing everywhere but discontinuous at the origin, exactly where they disagree.

2. What hypothesis does Clairaut's Theorem require to guarantee equal mixed partials?

Continuity of both mixed partials at the point is exactly the hypothesis the proof uses to force the two limits to coincide.

3. What tool does the proof sketch of Clairaut's Theorem apply twice?

Applying the Mean Value Theorem in x then y (or y then x) produces two expressions for Δ(h,k)/hk that must agree in the limit.

4. Fill in the blank: the theorem guaranteeing equal mixed partial derivatives, given continuity, is ___'s Theorem.

Clairaut's Theorem is the standard name for the equality-of-mixed-partials result.

5. Fill in the blank: mixed partials can exist yet disagree at a point where they fail to be ___.

Discontinuity of the mixed partials at a point is exactly what allows them to disagree there.
