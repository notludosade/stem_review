Partial Derivatives and Multivariable Functions

Units 1–3 rebuilt single-variable analysis on abstract spaces. Unit 4 goes the other direction: functions of several real variables, where a genuinely new phenomenon appears immediately — the single most natural generalization of the derivative turns out not to be strong enough.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Partial Derivative Let f: ℝⁿ → ℝ and let a ∈ ℝⁿ. The partial derivative of f with respect to xᵢ at a is

∂f/∂xᵢ(a) = limh→0 [f(a + h·eᵢ) − f(a)] / h,

where eᵢ is the i-th standard basis vector — provided this limit exists.
Why this is just single-variable differentiation in disguise Freezing every coordinate except xᵢ turns f into an ordinary function of one real variable, and ∂f/∂xᵢ(a) is exactly that function's derivative at aᵢ, in the sense of ordinary single-variable calculus. Nothing new is being defined here — only where to apply an old definition.
Example Let f(x,y) = x²y. Freezing y and differentiating in x: ∂f/∂x = 2xy. Freezing x and differentiating in y: ∂f/∂y = x². At (a,b) = (3,2): ∂f/∂x(3,2) = 12, ∂f/∂y(3,2) = 9.

A natural guess is that a function is "differentiable" at a point exactly when all its partial derivatives exist there. The next example shows this guess fails badly — badly enough that it doesn't even guarantee continuity.

Example: partial derivatives can exist where the function isn't even continuous Let f(x,y) = xy/(x²+y²) for (x,y) ≠ (0,0), and f(0,0) = 0.

∂f/∂x(0,0) = limh→0 [f(h,0) − f(0,0)] / h = limh→0 [0 − 0] / h = 0, since f(h,0) = h·0/(h²+0) = 0 for every h ≠ 0. By the identical computation with the roles of x and y swapped, ∂f/∂y(0,0) = 0 as well. Both partial derivatives exist at the origin.

But along the line y = x: f(t,t) = t²/(2t²) = 1/2 for every t ≠ 0, while f(0,0) = 0. So f(t,t) does not approach f(0,0) as t → 0 — f is not continuous at the origin, despite both partial derivatives existing there.
Why this is a genuine surprise In single-variable analysis, differentiability at a point always implies continuity there — a fact used constantly throughout Real Analysis A. The example above shows the naive multivariable analogue of differentiability — "all partial derivatives exist" — carries no such guarantee. The partials only see how f behaves along the n coordinate directions through a; the discontinuity above is invisible to both partials because it only shows up along the diagonal direction y = x, a direction neither partial ever probes. A stronger notion of differentiability, sensitive to every direction of approach at once, is needed — the subject of Lesson 18.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is ∂f/∂xᵢ(a), in terms of single-variable calculus?

Fixing all coordinates but one reduces f to a one-variable function, and the partial derivative is exactly that function's ordinary derivative.

2. What does the example f(x,y) = xy/(x²+y²) demonstrate?

Both partials vanish at the origin, yet approaching along y = x shows f is not continuous there — partials alone miss this diagonal behavior.

3. Which single-variable fact fails to generalize to "all partial derivatives exist"?

In one variable, differentiability always forces continuity; the existence of all partials in several variables does not.

4. Fill in the blank: freezing all but one variable and differentiating gives the ___ derivative.

This is exactly the definition of a partial derivative.

5. Fill in the blank: xy/(x²+y²) has both partial derivatives at the origin equal to zero, yet the function itself is ___ there.

Approaching along y = x gives a limit of 1/2, not f(0,0) = 0, so f is discontinuous at the origin.
