Uniform Convergence of Power Series

Every power series converges uniformly on any closed interval strictly inside its radius of convergence — which is exactly what unlocks Unit 1's continuity, integration, and differentiation machinery for free.

← Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 6 established where a power series converges. This lesson upgrades that to how it converges — and the answer connects power series directly back to Unit 1's Weierstrass M-test.

Theorem — Uniform Convergence on Compact Subintervals Let Σaₙ(x − c)ⁿ have radius of convergence R > 0 (possibly ∞). Then for every r with 0 < r < R, the series converges uniformly on the closed interval [c − r, c + r].
Proof Since r < R, choose x₀ with r < |x₀ − c| < R (possible since R is either ∞ or strictly greater than r). The series converges absolutely at x₀ (Lesson 6), so its terms are bounded: |aₙ(x₀ − c)ⁿ| ≤ M for some M and all n. For any x ∈ [c − r, c + r], let ρ = r/|x₀ − c| < 1 (a fixed number, not depending on x). Then |aₙ(x − c)ⁿ| = |aₙ(x₀ − c)ⁿ| · (|x − c|/|x₀ − c|)ⁿ ≤ M · ρⁿ, since |x − c| ≤ r. Set Mₙ = M·ρⁿ; then ΣMₙ is a convergent geometric series (ρ < 1), and Mₙ bounds |aₙ(x−c)ⁿ| independently of x ∈ [c−r, c+r]. By the Weierstrass M-Test (Unit 1, Lesson 2), the power series converges uniformly on [c − r, c + r]. ∎
Why this unlocks all of Unit 1 for power series, one closed subinterval at a time Combined with Unit 1's theorems, this means: the sum function of a power series is continuous at every point strictly inside (c−R, c+R) (Lesson 3, applied on some [c−r, c+r] containing that point), and can be integrated term by term over any [a,b] ⊆ (c−R, c+R) (Lesson 4). Both consequences hold at every interior point, even though — as the next box shows — the convergence is not uniform on the whole open interval at once.
Why the theorem stops at "any r < R," not "all of (c−R, c+R)" Consider Σxⁿ, with sum s(x) = 1/(1−x) and R = 1. Its partial sums are sₙ(x) = (1 − xⁿ⁺¹)/(1 − x), so |sₙ(x) − s(x)| = |xⁿ⁺¹|/|1 − x|. For any fixed n, as x → 1⁻ within (−1,1), this quantity → ∞ — so supx∈(−1,1)|sₙ(x) − s(x)| is not even finite, let alone shrinking to 0. The convergence is uniform on every [−r, r] with r < 1, but genuinely fails to be uniform on the full open interval (−1, 1) itself — the obstruction concentrates arbitrarily close to the endpoint, exactly where "any r < R" stops protecting you.
Example: continuity of Σxⁿ/n! everywhere Σxⁿ/n! has R = ∞ (Lesson 6), so for any x₀ ∈ ℝ, choosing r = |x₀| + 1 gives a closed interval [−r, r] containing x₀ on which the series converges uniformly. By Unit 1, Lesson 3, the sum function is continuous on [−r, r], hence continuous at x₀. Since x₀ was arbitrary, the sum function (which Unit 2 will soon identify as eˣ) is continuous on all of ℝ — built entirely from local uniform convergence, one closed interval at a time.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does this lesson's theorem guarantee, for 0 < r < R?

The theorem gives uniform convergence on any closed subinterval strictly inside the radius, not necessarily on the whole open interval.

2. What tool from Unit 1 does the proof rely on?

The proof bounds each term by an x-independent Mₙ forming a convergent geometric series, exactly the hypothesis the Weierstrass M-Test needs.

3. Why does Σxⁿ fail to converge uniformly on the full open interval (−1, 1)?

The gap |sₙ(x) − s(x)| = |xⁿ⁺¹|/|1−x| grows without bound as x approaches 1 within the interval, for any fixed n, ruling out uniform convergence there.

4. Fill in the blank: a power series converges uniformly on any ___ subinterval strictly inside its radius of convergence.

Closed (compact) subintervals [c−r, c+r] with r < R are exactly where uniform convergence is guaranteed.

5. Fill in the blank: the proof of uniform convergence on [c−r, c+r] applies the ___ M-Test from Unit 1.

The Weierstrass M-Test is the direct tool that converts the geometric bound into uniform convergence.
