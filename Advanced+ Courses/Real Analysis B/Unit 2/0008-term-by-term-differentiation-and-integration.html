Term-by-Term Differentiation and Integration

Calculus BC differentiated and integrated power series term by term as a computational shortcut. This lesson proves the shortcut is exactly correct — and along the way, uncovers what a power series's coefficients really are.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Lemma — Differentiating Preserves the Radius of Convergence If f(x) = Σaₙ(x−c)ⁿ has radius of convergence R, then the term-by-term differentiated series Σn·aₙ(x−c)ⁿ⁻¹ also has radius of convergence R.
Proof Fix any r < R and choose r' with r < r' < R. Since Σaₙr'ⁿ converges absolutely, its terms go to 0, hence are bounded: |aₙ|r'ⁿ ≤ M for all n. Let q = r/r' < 1. Then |n·aₙ·rⁿ⁻¹| = (|aₙ|r'ⁿ) · n·(r/r')ⁿ⁻¹ / r' ≤ (M/r')·n·qⁿ⁻¹. The series Σn·qⁿ⁻¹ converges by the Ratio Test (Real Analysis A): the ratio of consecutive terms is (n+1)qⁿ/(n·qⁿ⁻¹) = q·(n+1)/n → q < 1. So by the Comparison Test, Σ|n·aₙ·rⁿ⁻¹| converges — the differentiated series converges absolutely for every r < R, so its radius is at least R. Conversely, for n ≥ 1, |aₙrⁿ| = (r/n)·|n·aₙ·rⁿ⁻¹| ≤ r·|n·aₙ·rⁿ⁻¹|, so absolute convergence of the differentiated series at r forces absolute convergence of the original series there too — the differentiated series's radius is at most R as well. Both directions together give exactly R. ∎
Theorem — Term-by-Term Differentiation and Integration Let f(x) = Σaₙ(x−c)ⁿ have radius of convergence R > 0. Then f is differentiable on (c−R, c+R), with f′(x) = Σn·aₙ(x−c)ⁿ⁻¹, and for any [a,b] ⊆ (c−R, c+R), ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = Σaₙ∫ₐᵇ(x−c)ⁿ dx — the series can be differentiated and integrated term by term throughout its radius of convergence.
Proof sketch Fix x ∈ (c−R, c+R) and choose r with |x − c| < r < R. By the Lemma, the differentiated series has the same radius R, so by Lesson 7 both Σaₙ(t−c)ⁿ and its term-by-term derivative Σn·aₙ(t−c)ⁿ⁻¹ converge uniformly on [c−r, c+r]. Let sₙ be the partial sums of f; then (sₙ′) converges uniformly on [c−r, c+r] to the derivative series, and (sₙ(c)) converges trivially (sₙ(c) = a₀ for every n). By the Differentiable Limit Theorem (Unit 1, Lesson 5), f is differentiable on [c−r, c+r] with f′ equal to the derivative series — and x was an arbitrary point of (c−R, c+R), so this holds throughout. For integration: since Σaₙ(x−c)ⁿ already converges uniformly on any [a,b] ⊆ (c−R, c+R) by Lesson 7, the Uniform Convergence and Integration theorem (Unit 1, Lesson 4) applies directly to the partial sums, giving term-by-term integration with no extra work. ∎
Why this reveals what the coefficients aₙ really are Applying the differentiation half of the theorem repeatedly: f′(x) = Σn·aₙ(x−c)ⁿ⁻¹, f″(x) = Σn(n−1)aₙ(x−c)ⁿ⁻², and in general f(k)(x) = Σn(n−1)⋯(n−k+1)aₙ(x−c)ⁿ⁻ᵏ — each differentiation preserves the radius R by the Lemma, so f is infinitely differentiable throughout (c−R, c+R). Evaluating f(k) at x = c kills every term except n = k (every lower power of (x−c) is already differentiated away, every higher power still has a factor of (x−c) that vanishes at x=c), leaving f(k)(c) = k!·aₖ. So aₖ = f(k)(c)/k! — the coefficients of a power series are forced to be exactly its own Taylor coefficients at the center. This is not a coincidence to be checked case by case; it's a direct consequence of term-by-term differentiation being valid at all.
Example: differentiating the geometric series f(x) = Σn=0∞ xⁿ = 1/(1−x) has R = 1. Term-by-term differentiation gives f′(x) = Σn=1∞ n·xⁿ⁻¹, which by the theorem must equal the ordinary derivative of 1/(1−x), namely 1/(1−x)². So Σn=1∞ n·xⁿ⁻¹ = 1/(1−x)² for |x| < 1 — an identity for a whole new series, obtained with no extra convergence work beyond this lesson's theorem.

1. What does the Lemma establish about differentiating a power series term by term?

Both directions of the comparison in the proof show the differentiated series converges absolutely exactly when the original does, for the same radius R.

2. Which two Unit 1 theorems does the proof of term-by-term differentiation combine?

Lesson 7 supplies uniform convergence of both the series and its derivative series on a compact subinterval, exactly the hypothesis the Differentiable Limit Theorem needs.

3. What does f(k)(c) = k!·aₖ reveal about a power series's coefficients?

Since term-by-term differentiation is valid, evaluating the k-th derivative at the center isolates aₖ exactly as k!·aₖ, forcing aₖ = f^(k)(c)/k!.

4. Fill in the blank: differentiating a power series term by term produces a new series with the ___ radius of convergence as the original.

The Lemma proves the differentiated series's radius equals the original's radius exactly.

5. Fill in the blank: because differentiation can be repeated arbitrarily, a power series is ___ differentiable throughout its radius of convergence.

Since the radius never shrinks under differentiation, the process can be repeated forever, making the sum function infinitely (C^∞) differentiable.
