Radius and Interval of Convergence

A power series is a series of functions of a very special kind — and that special structure forces its set of convergence to always be an interval, never anything more exotic.

← Lesson 5 Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

A power series centered at c is a series of the form Σn=0∞ aₙ(x − c)ⁿ, for fixed coefficients a₀, a₁, a₂, …. AP Calculus BC used the ratio test to find where such series converge; this lesson proves, from the definitions built in Real Analysis A, exactly what shape that set of convergence must take.

Theorem — Existence of the Radius of Convergence For any power series Σaₙ(x − c)ⁿ, exactly one of the following holds: (a) the series converges only at x = c; (b) the series converges absolutely for every x ∈ ℝ; or (c) there is a number R > 0 such that the series converges absolutely whenever |x − c| < R and diverges whenever |x − c| > R. In cases (a) and (b), R is taken to be 0 and ∞ respectively; in every case R is called the radius of convergence.
Proof Suppose the series converges at some x₁ ≠ c (if it converges nowhere but c, that's case (a)). Since Σaₙ(x₁−c)ⁿ converges, its terms go to 0, so they're bounded: |aₙ(x₁−c)ⁿ| ≤ M for some M and all n. Now take any x with |x − c| < |x₁ − c|, and let r = |x−c|/|x₁−c| < 1. Then |aₙ(x−c)ⁿ| = |aₙ(x₁−c)ⁿ| · rⁿ ≤ M·rⁿ. Since ΣM·rⁿ is a convergent geometric series (0 ≤ r < 1), the Comparison Test (Real Analysis A) shows Σ|aₙ(x−c)ⁿ| converges — so the original series converges absolutely at x. This shows: convergence at any one point x₁ ≠ c forces absolute convergence at every point strictly closer to c. Let R = sup{|x₁ − c| : the series converges at x₁} (possibly ∞, if no such supremum is finite). By the argument just given, the series converges absolutely for every |x − c| < R, and by definition of supremum, it cannot converge for any |x − c| > R. ∎
Why the comparison to a geometric series is the key move The whole proof reduces the unknown power series to a familiar geometric series ΣMrⁿ purely by bounding |aₙ(x₁−c)ⁿ| — the boundedness that convergence at x₁ automatically supplies. This is why the set of convergence can only ever be an interval (or all of ℝ, or a single point): geometric decay radiates outward from c symmetrically, with no way to converge at some far point but skip a nearer one.
Why R alone doesn't decide what happens at the endpoints The theorem is silent about x = c ± R exactly — and genuinely so, since different power series behave differently there. The interval of convergence is the full set where the series converges: (c−R, c+R), plus whichever of the two endpoints (if any) the series happens to converge at, checked separately by the ordinary series tests from Real Analysis A (comparison, alternating series, etc.).
Example: three series, same radius, different endpoint behavior Σxⁿ, Σxⁿ/n, and Σxⁿ/n² all have radius of convergence R = 1 (each is checked by the Ratio Test: the ratio of consecutive coefficients' reciprocals tends to 1). But their intervals of convergence differ: Σxⁿ diverges at both x = ±1 (terms don't even go to 0), giving (−1, 1). Σxⁿ/n diverges at x = 1 (harmonic series) but converges at x = −1 (alternating harmonic series, conditionally), giving [−1, 1). Σxⁿ/n² converges absolutely at both endpoints (comparison to the convergent p-series Σ1/n²), giving the closed interval [−1, 1].
Example: R = ∞ and R = 0 Σxⁿ/n! has radius R = ∞ — the Ratio Test ratio |xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)!| / |xⁿ/n!| = |x|/(n+1) → 0 for every fixed x, so the series converges absolutely everywhere (this series is, in fact, eˣ — Unit 2's next lessons make that identification precise). By contrast, Σn!xⁿ has radius R = 0: the same ratio is (n+1)|x| → ∞ for any x ≠ 0, so the series diverges everywhere except at x = 0.

1. What does the theorem guarantee about a power series's set of convergence?

The proof forces the convergence set to radiate symmetrically from c, so it must be an interval — degenerating to a point or expanding to all of ℝ in the extreme cases.

2. What is the key comparison used in the proof?

Boundedness of |aₙ(x₁−c)ⁿ| from convergence at x₁, combined with a ratio r < 1, gives a geometric-series bound that the Comparison Test can use directly.

3. Σxⁿ, Σxⁿ/n, and Σxⁿ/n² all share radius of convergence R = 1. What differs between them?

Same radius, but different endpoint behavior — from both endpoints diverging, to one converging, to both converging.

4. Fill in the blank: the number R such that a power series converges absolutely for |x−c| < R and diverges for |x−c| > R is called the ___ of convergence.

Radius of convergence names the distance from the center within which the series is guaranteed to converge absolutely.

5. Fill in the blank: the proof of the radius of convergence theorem compares the power series to a convergent ___ series.

A geometric series with ratio r < 1 is what the Comparison Test needs to conclude absolute convergence.
