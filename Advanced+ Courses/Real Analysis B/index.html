Real Analysis B

Real Analysis A proved calculus rigorous on ℝ. This course pushes past it in two directions — generalizing convergence, metric spaces, and differentiation, then rebuilding integration itself from measure theory. Requires Real Analysis A.

Unit 1 — Uniform Convergence
Lesson 1

Pointwise vs. Uniform Convergence

Real Analysis A studied sequences of numbers. Now the terms of the sequence are themselves functions — and it turns out there are two genuinely different ways for a sequence of functions to converge.

 Lesson 2

The Weierstrass M-Test

Checking sup|fₙ(x) − f(x)| → 0 by hand is often hopeless for an infinite series of functions. The M-test turns that check into a single ordinary series of numbers.

 Lesson 3

Uniform Convergence and Continuity

The whole reason to care about uniform convergence: it's exactly strong enough to guarantee the limit of continuous functions is itself continuous.

 Lesson 4

Uniform Convergence and Integration

Continuity survived a uniform limit. Integrability does too — and uniform convergence is exactly the hypothesis that lets a limit and an integral be swapped.

 Lesson 5

Uniform Convergence and Differentiation

Continuity and integration both survived a uniform limit for free. Differentiation is the exception — and the reason why closes out this unit.

Unit 2 — Power Series
Lesson 6

Radius and Interval of Convergence

A power series is a series of functions of a very special kind — and that special structure forces its set of convergence to always be an interval, never anything more exotic.

 Lesson 7

Uniform Convergence of Power Series

Every power series converges uniformly on any closed interval strictly inside its radius of convergence — which is exactly what unlocks Unit 1's continuity, integration, and differentiation machinery for free.

 Lesson 8

Term-by-Term Differentiation and Integration

Calculus BC differentiated and integrated power series term by term as a computational shortcut. This lesson proves the shortcut is exactly correct — and along the way, uncovers what a power series's coefficients really are.

 Lesson 9

Taylor Series and Analytic Functions

Lesson 8 showed a power series's coefficients are forced to be its Taylor coefficients. This lesson runs the construction the other way — starting from an infinitely differentiable function, and asking honestly whether its Taylor series converges back to it.

 Lesson 10

Where Taylor Series Can Fail

eˣ and sin(x) were both analytic everywhere — it would be easy to walk away thinking every C^∞ function is. It isn't, and the counterexample closes this unit by showing exactly how the remainder term can refuse to vanish.

Unit 3 — Metric Spaces
Lesson 11

Metric Spaces: Definitions and Examples

Real Analysis A did everything with |x − y|. This unit asks what actually made those arguments work — and the answer turns out to need almost nothing about ℝ at all.

 Lesson 12

Open and Closed Sets

A metric alone is enough to build the entire vocabulary of open sets, closed sets, and limit points — with no reference to numbers, only to distance.

 Lesson 13

Convergence and Completeness

Sequences and Cauchy sequences carry over to any metric space unchanged. Whether every Cauchy sequence actually converges, though, depends entirely on the space — and the answer for C[a,b] turns out to already be sitting in Unit 1.

 Lesson 14

Continuity in Metric Spaces

The ε–δ definition of continuity generalizes to any two metric spaces by simply replacing |·| with d. What's surprising is how far it can be pushed: continuity turns out to be expressible with no numbers at all — only open sets.

 Lesson 15

Compactness

Real Analysis A proved compact and "closed and bounded" coincide for subsets of ℝ, and flagged that this was special. This lesson shows exactly how special — and gives a concrete function space where it genuinely fails.

 Lesson 16

Connectedness

Real Analysis A defined connectedness with closures and proved it means "interval" — for ℝ only. This lesson generalizes the definition, resolves a fact Lesson 12 deliberately left open, and proves the theorem that makes the Intermediate Value Theorem an instance of something much bigger.

Unit 4 — Multivariable Differentiation
Lesson 17

Partial Derivatives and Multivariable Functions

Units 1–3 rebuilt single-variable analysis on abstract spaces. Unit 4 goes the other direction: functions of several real variables, where a genuinely new phenomenon appears immediately — the single most natural generalization of the derivative turns out not to be strong enough.

 Lesson 18

The Total Derivative

Lesson 17 showed partial derivatives can miss a function's behavior entirely. This lesson defines the notion that doesn't: differentiability as the existence of a single best linear approximation, sensitive to every direction at once.

 Lesson 19

The Jacobian and the Chain Rule

Once differentiability means "best linear approximation," composing two differentiable functions should compose their linear approximations — and it does, exactly, as a matrix product.

 Lesson 20

Clairaut's Theorem and Higher-Order Partials

Differentiating twice in two different orders looks like it must give the same answer — and usually does. This lesson pins down exactly when that's guaranteed, and shows the guarantee can genuinely fail.

 Lesson 21

The Inverse and Implicit Function Theorems

Single-variable calculus says a function with nonzero derivative is locally invertible. This capstone lesson generalizes that fact with the Jacobian in place of the derivative — and shows implicit differentiation, a technique from prerequisite calculus, was quietly leaning on it the whole time.

Unit 5 — Lebesgue Measure
Lesson 22

Why Riemann Integration Breaks Down

Real Analysis A already flagged one bounded function the Riemann integral can't handle. This lesson diagnoses exactly why, and shows the damage is worse than one bad example — even a limit of perfectly well-behaved functions can escape integrability entirely.

 Lesson 23

Outer Measure on the Real Line

Lesson 22 asked for a notion of "size" general enough to assign ℚ a length of zero. This lesson builds the first candidate — outer measure — and proves it does exactly that, while still agreeing with ordinary length on intervals.

 Lesson 24

Measurable Sets and Sigma-Algebras

Outer measure is defined on every subset of ℝ, but Lesson 23 warned it doesn't add up correctly on all of them. This lesson isolates exactly the sets where it does — and shows they form a rich, robust collection.

 Lesson 25

Countable Additivity and Measure-Zero Sets

Lesson 24 isolated the measurable sets. This lesson proves what makes them worth isolating: on this collection, outer measure becomes genuinely, countably additive — and the language of "almost everywhere" falls directly out of it.

 Lesson 26

Measurable Functions

Sets are only half the story. This capstone lesson names the functions the new integral will act on — and proves the exact structural gap that broke Riemann integration in Lesson 22 no longer exists.

Unit 6 — The Lebesgue Integral
Lesson 27

The Lebesgue Integral of Simple Functions

Unit 5 built measure. This lesson starts spending it: defining an integral for the simplest measurable functions, then extending it to every nonnegative measurable function by approximation — closing the loop opened three lessons ago.

 Lesson 28

The Lebesgue Integral and Comparison with Riemann

Lesson 27 integrated nonnegative functions. This lesson allows negative values, pins down exactly which functions are integrable, and asks the reassuring question: does this new integral still agree with the old one, wherever the old one worked?

 Lesson 29

The Monotone Convergence Theorem

Lesson 28 needed exactly one missing tool twice: to prove general additivity of the integral, and to finish comparing Lebesgue's integral with Riemann's. This lesson proves that tool, then spends it on both debts at once.

 Lesson 30

The Dominated Convergence Theorem

MCT needed monotonicity. This lesson drops that requirement entirely — replacing it with a single dominating integrable function — and finally builds, in full, the counterexample Lesson 4 called "beyond this course's scope."

 Lesson 31

Capstone — From Riemann to Lebesgue

The whole course, traced as one dependency chain: how relaxing "uniform" to "pointwise," then abandoning Riemann's integral altogether, builds a theory strong enough to integrate through any pointwise limit.
