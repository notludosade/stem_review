Uniform Convergence and Integration

Continuity survived a uniform limit. Integrability does too — and uniform convergence is exactly the hypothesis that lets a limit and an integral be swapped.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Given fₙ → f, when is limn→∞ ∫ₐᵇ fₙ = ∫ₐᵇ (limn→∞ fₙ) = ∫ₐᵇ f? This "interchange of limit and integral" is not automatic — but uniform convergence is exactly strong enough to guarantee it, and to guarantee the limit function is even integrable in the first place.

Theorem — Uniform Convergence and the Riemann Integral Suppose fₙ → f uniformly on [a, b], and each fₙ is Riemann integrable on [a, b]. Then f is Riemann integrable on [a, b], and ∫ₐᵇ f = limn→∞ ∫ₐᵇ fₙ.
Proof — f is integrable Fix ε > 0. By uniform convergence, choose N such that n ≥ N implies |fₙ(x) − f(x)| < ε/(4(b−a)) for every x ∈ [a, b]. Fix such an n. Since fₙ is integrable, the Cauchy Criterion for Integrability (Real Analysis A, Lesson 31) gives a partition P with U(fₙ, P) − L(fₙ, P) < ε/2. Because f is within ε/(4(b−a)) of fₙ everywhere, on every subinterval of P the supremum of f is at most ε/(4(b−a)) above the supremum of fₙ, and the infimum of f is at most ε/(4(b−a)) below the infimum of fₙ. Summing over all subintervals of total length (b−a): U(f, P) ≤ U(fₙ, P) + ε/4 and L(f, P) ≥ L(fₙ, P) − ε/4. So U(f, P) − L(f, P) ≤ [U(fₙ, P) − L(fₙ, P)] + ε/2 < ε/2 + ε/2 = ε. By the Cauchy Criterion, f is integrable. ∎
Proof — the integrals converge |∫ₐᵇ f − ∫ₐᵇ fₙ| = |∫ₐᵇ (f − fₙ)| ≤ ∫ₐᵇ |f − fₙ| ≤ (b − a) · supx∈[a,b] |f(x) − fₙ(x)|, using the standard bound that an integral is at most (length of interval) × (bound on the integrand). Since fₙ → f uniformly, the right-hand side → 0 as n → ∞, forcing ∫ₐᵇ fₙ → ∫ₐᵇ f. ∎
Why uniformity, not just pointwise convergence, drives both halves Both proofs hinge on a single bound on |fₙ(x) − f(x)| that holds at every x ∈ [a,b] simultaneously — used to bound the whole interval's worth of upper/lower sums in the first proof, and the whole integral in the second. A pointwise bound, potentially different at every x, could not be summed or integrated into a single small number the way a uniform bound can. This is the same shape of argument as Lesson 3's continuity proof: uniform convergence supplies one number that controls the entire domain at once.
Example: integrating Σ sin(nx)/n² term by term Lesson 2 showed s(x) = Σk=1∞ sin(kx)/k² is a uniform limit of its partial sums sₙ(x) = Σk=1n sin(kx)/k² on ℝ, and Lesson 3 showed s is continuous (hence integrable) on any [a,b]. By this theorem, ∫ₐᵇ s(x) dx = limn→∞ ∫ₐᵇ sₙ(x) dx = limn→∞ Σk=1n ∫ₐᵇ sin(kx)/k² dx — the integral of the infinite sum equals the infinite sum of the integrals, term by term, justified entirely by uniform convergence.
Why pointwise convergence isn't enough for this theorem A classic counterexample (beyond this course's scope to construct in full) is a "moving bump" sequence: fₙ is a triangular spike of height n and width 2/n centered near 0, so that fₙ(x) → 0 pointwise everywhere, yet ∫₀¹ fₙ = 1 for every n (the spike's area stays fixed at 1 as its width shrinks and height grows to compensate). Here lim ∫fₙ = 1 ≠ 0 = ∫(lim fₙ). The convergence is pointwise but not uniform — the height of the spike, growing without bound, is exactly the obstruction to a single N working across the whole domain — and the interchange of limit and integral genuinely fails.

1. If fₙ → f uniformly on [a,b] and each fₙ is Riemann integrable, what does the theorem guarantee?

Uniform convergence of integrable functions guarantees both integrability of the limit and convergence of the integrals to the limit's integral.

2. In the proof that f is integrable, what role does the uniform bound |fₙ(x) − f(x)| < ε/(4(b−a)) play?

The uniform bound controls U(f,P)−L(f,P) in terms of U(fₙ,P)−L(fₙ,P), which is already small by the Cauchy Criterion applied to fₙ.

3. What does the "moving bump" example show?

The moving bump converges to 0 pointwise but keeps constant integral 1, showing the theorem's uniform-convergence hypothesis genuinely cannot be weakened to pointwise.

4. Fill in the blank: swapping a limit and an integral, term by term, is justified when the convergence of the sequence or series is ___.

Uniform convergence is exactly the hypothesis this theorem needs to justify interchanging the limit and the integral.

5. Fill in the blank: a uniform limit of Riemann ___ functions on [a,b] is itself Riemann integrable on [a,b].

The theorem's first half guarantees integrability of the limit, not just convergence of integrals.
