Uniform Convergence and Continuity

The whole reason to care about uniform convergence: it's exactly strong enough to guarantee the limit of continuous functions is itself continuous.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 showed pointwise convergence alone can turn a sequence of continuous functions into a discontinuous limit. This lesson proves the payoff for the stronger hypothesis: uniform convergence rules that out completely.

Theorem — Uniform Limit of Continuous Functions Suppose fₙ → f uniformly on S, and each fₙ is continuous at x₀ ∈ S. Then f is continuous at x₀.
Proof Fix ε > 0. By uniform convergence, choose N such that n ≥ N implies |fₙ(x) − f(x)| < ε/3 for every x ∈ S — this single N works everywhere, which is exactly what uniform (rather than pointwise) convergence supplies. Fix any such n ≥ N. Since fₙ is continuous at x₀, choose δ > 0 such that |x − x₀| < δ implies |fₙ(x) − fₙ(x₀)| < ε/3. Now for any x ∈ S with |x − x₀| < δ:

|f(x) − f(x₀)| ≤ |f(x) − fₙ(x)| + |fₙ(x) − fₙ(x₀)| + |fₙ(x₀) − f(x₀)| < ε/3 + ε/3 + ε/3 = ε.

The first and third terms are each below ε/3 by the uniform-convergence choice of n, applied at the two specific points x and x₀; the middle term is below ε/3 by fₙ's continuity at x₀. So f is continuous at x₀. ∎
Why uniformity is exactly what the first and third terms need Both |f(x) − fₙ(x)| and |fₙ(x₀) − f(x₀)| must be small at the same fixed n — one bound at the arbitrary nearby point x, the other at the fixed point x₀. Uniform convergence supplies a single N that works at every point of S, so one choice of n handles both simultaneously. Pointwise convergence alone would only guarantee an N for x₀ specifically; there would be no guarantee that same n makes |f(x) − fₙ(x)| small too, since x ranges over a whole neighborhood of x₀, each point with potentially its own required N.
Corollary — A Discontinuity Test for Non-Uniform Convergence If (fₙ) is a sequence of continuous functions converging pointwise to a discontinuous f, then the convergence cannot be uniform. (This is the contrapositive of the theorem above.)
Example: revisiting fₙ(x) = xⁿ on [0, 1] Each fₙ(x) = xⁿ is continuous, and (Lesson 1) the pointwise limit is f(x) = 0 on [0,1) with f(1) = 1 — discontinuous at x = 1. By the corollary, this alone proves the convergence is not uniform on [0, 1], without needing the direct supremum computation from Lesson 1. The corollary is often the fastest way to rule out uniform convergence.
Example: Σ sin(nx)/n² is continuous on all of ℝ Lesson 2's M-test showed Σ sin(nx)/n² converges uniformly on ℝ. Each partial sum sₙ(x) = Σk=1n sin(kx)/k² is a finite sum of continuous functions, hence continuous. By this lesson's theorem, the uniform limit — the full sum s(x) = Σk=1∞ sin(kx)/k² — is therefore continuous on all of ℝ, even though it's an infinite sum with no elementary closed form.

Uniform convergence of continuous functions is exactly strong enough to guarantee the limit function is continuous.

2. In the ε/3 proof, why must the same n work for both |f(x) − fₙ(x)| and |fₙ(x₀) − f(x₀)|?

Uniform convergence's N works at every point of S, so a single fixed n bounds the error at both x and x₀ at once — pointwise convergence would not guarantee this.

3. What does the corollary let you conclude about fₙ(x) = xⁿ on [0, 1]?

Since every xⁿ is continuous but the pointwise limit is discontinuous at 1, the corollary rules out uniform convergence immediately.
