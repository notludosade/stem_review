Uniform Convergence and Differentiation

Continuity and integration both survived a uniform limit for free. Differentiation is the exception — and the reason why closes out this unit.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Lessons 3 and 4 both had the same shape: fₙ → f uniformly, plus some property of each fₙ, guarantees f inherits that property (and, for integrals, that the limiting values converge too). It's tempting to guess differentiation works the same way. It doesn't — and the counterexample shows exactly why.

Counterexample: uniform convergence of fₙ says nothing about fₙ′ Let fₙ(x) = sin(nx)/√n. Since |fₙ(x) − 0| = |sin(nx)|/√n ≤ 1/√n for every x, and 1/√n → 0, (fₙ) converges uniformly to f = 0 on all of ℝ. But fₙ′(x) = √n·cos(nx), which oscillates with amplitude √n → ∞ — it doesn't converge at any x (except where cos(nx) = 0 infinitely often by coincidence). So fₙ → f = 0 uniformly, yet (fₙ′) doesn't converge to f′ = 0 — it doesn't converge at all.
Why differentiation breaks the pattern of Lessons 3 and 4 Continuity and integration are both "averaging" operations — they only ever look at values of a function nearby or across an interval, so a uniformly small difference in function values directly bounds the difference in the property. Differentiation is the opposite: it depends on the rate of change, which can be wildly different even when two functions are everywhere close together. Dividing by √n in the counterexample kept the function's amplitude going to 0, but multiplying by the same √n when differentiating sin(nx) sent the derivative's amplitude to infinity — closeness in fₙ carries no information about closeness in fₙ′.

The fix is to stop asking about fₙ directly, and instead impose uniform convergence on the derivatives themselves.

Theorem — The Differentiable Limit Theorem Let (fₙ) be a sequence of differentiable functions on [a, b] such that (fₙ′) converges uniformly on [a, b] to some function g. If (fₙ(x₀)) converges for at least one point x₀ ∈ [a, b], then (fₙ) converges uniformly on [a, b] to a differentiable function f, and f′ = g.
Proof sketch Step 1 — (fₙ) converges uniformly. Apply the Mean Value Theorem to fₙ − fₘ on the interval between any x ∈ [a,b] and x₀: (fₙ−fₘ)(x) − (fₙ−fₘ)(x₀) = (fₙ′−fₘ′)(c)·(x−x₀) for some c between x and x₀. Rearranging and bounding, |fₙ(x) − fₘ(x)| ≤ |fₙ(x₀) − fₘ(x₀)| + (b−a)·sup|fₙ′ − fₘ′|. As m, n → ∞, the first term → 0 (since (fₙ(x₀)) converges, hence is Cauchy) and the second → 0 (since (fₙ′) converges uniformly, hence is uniformly Cauchy) — and this bound holds for every x at once. By the Uniform Cauchy Criterion (Lesson 2), (fₙ) converges uniformly to some f.

Step 2 — f′ = g. Fix x ∈ [a,b] and consider the difference quotients φₙ(t) = (fₙ(t) − fₙ(x))/(t − x) for t ≠ x. A second Mean-Value-Theorem argument, structurally identical to Step 1, shows (φₙ) converges uniformly in t to φ(t) = (f(t) − f(x))/(t − x). Since each φₙ(t) → fₙ′(x) as t → x, and this convergence is uniform in n, the order of the two limits (t → x and n → ∞) can be swapped — the same kind of interchange justified by uniform convergence in Lesson 3. Swapping gives f′(x) = limt→x φ(t) = limn→∞ fₙ′(x) = g(x). ∎
Example: a scaling that satisfies the theorem's hypothesis Let fₙ(x) = x + sin(nx)/n² on [0, 1]. Then fₙ′(x) = 1 + cos(nx)/n, and supx|cos(nx)/n| ≤ 1/n → 0, so (fₙ′) converges uniformly to g(x) = 1. Also fₙ(0) = 0 for every n, so the theorem's single-point hypothesis holds trivially at x₀ = 0. The theorem now guarantees (fₙ) converges uniformly to some differentiable f with f′ = 1. Direct inspection confirms it: fₙ(x) → x uniformly (since |sin(nx)/n²| ≤ 1/n² → 0), so f(x) = x and indeed f′(x) = 1 = g(x) — contrast the healthy 1/n² scaling here with the earlier 1/√n scaling, which was too weak to tame the derivative.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does the fₙ(x) = sin(nx)/√n example show?

fₙ = sin(nx)/√n → 0 uniformly, but fₙ′ = √n·cos(nx) has amplitude growing without bound, so it doesn't converge at all.

2. What does the Differentiable Limit Theorem require to converge uniformly?

The theorem's hypothesis is on the derivatives (uniform convergence) plus convergence of the original sequence at a single point — from which full uniform convergence of (fₙ) is derived, not assumed.

3. In the Step 1 proof, what does the Mean Value Theorem relate?

The Mean Value Theorem applied to fₙ − fₘ links the difference at any x back to the difference at x₀, plus a derivative term evaluated at some intermediate point.

4. Fill in the blank: the Differentiable Limit Theorem requires uniform convergence of the sequence of ___, not the original functions.

Uniform convergence must be imposed on (fₙ′), since uniform convergence of (fₙ) itself gives no information about the derivatives.

5. Fill in the blank: the theorem only requires (fₙ(x₀)) to converge at ___ point x₀, not the whole sequence (fₙ), to conclude uniform convergence of (fₙ) overall.

A single point of convergence, combined with uniform convergence of the derivatives, is enough to force uniform convergence of the entire sequence via the Mean Value Theorem bound.
