Mathematics Capstone

An open-ended proof, not a quiz — this project exists to see whether you've actually proved yourself on Precalculus, AP Calculus BC, Real Analysis A, and Real Analysis B.

← Mathematics Pathway All Projects →

The Brief

Pick one calculus fact you used without proof in AP Calculus BC — the power rule, the Extreme Value Theorem, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, term-by-term differentiation of a power series, swapping a limit with an integral, or anything similar — and reconstruct a rigorous proof of it using only the tools Real Analysis A and B actually built. Every step should be justified by a named definition or theorem, not "it's obviously true."

Deliverables

  • A precisely stated result: an if-then statement, not "a calculus fact"
  • A full proof using only Real Analysis A/B-level tools (epsilon-delta, sequences, compactness, uniform convergence, measure — whichever apply), each step labeled by the theorem or definition that licenses it
  • At least one place where you explicitly note what breaks if a hypothesis (continuity, boundedness, uniform convergence) is dropped
  • A short "why we get away with it in Calc BC" paragraph — what Precalculus and AP Calculus BC quietly assumed that Real Analysis actually proves

Reflection

Answer in your own words. There's no grading here — just save your answers below.

1. What result did you pick, and where in AP Calculus BC did you first use it without seeing a proof? What did the course tell you to just accept?

2. Which specific Real Analysis A definition or theorem did the heavy lifting in your proof (an epsilon-delta argument, a compactness argument, the Extreme Value Theorem, etc.)? Explain it well enough that someone who's only taken Calc BC could follow it.

3. Find or construct an example where one hypothesis of your theorem fails and the conclusion actually fails too. What does that example teach you that the proof alone doesn't?

4. If your result involves swapping a limit with a sum or integral, what condition from Real Analysis B (uniform convergence, dominated convergence) makes that legal — and what's a case where swapping them silently gives the wrong answer? If it doesn't apply here, describe a case from Real Analysis B where it would.

5. Before this project, would you have said you "understood" this result? What changed about that understanding now that you've had to write the proof yourself instead of reading someone else's?
