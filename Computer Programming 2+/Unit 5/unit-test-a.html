1. What is the one ordering rule a min-heap actually enforces?

Unlike a BST, there's no rule relating a node to its whole subtree or to its siblings — only to its direct children.

2. Fill in the blank: a binary tree where every parent is smaller (or larger) than both of its children is called a ___.

This is the name for the entire structure Lesson 15 introduces.

3. What is the time complexity of building a heap from an existing unsorted collection (heapify)?

This is a common surprise — heapify is asymptotically cheaper than a full sort.

4. Why is a min-heap used to find the kth largest element, rather than a max-heap?

Once the heap holds exactly the k largest values seen so far, its smallest member is the kth largest overall.

5. Fill in the blank: an alternative approach that averages O(n) time by partitioning the array like quicksort, at the cost of worse worst-case behavior, is called ___.

It trades a better average case for a worse worst case and the inability to handle a data stream, unlike the heap approach.

6. Trace the bounded min-heap (capped at size k=2) for nums = [3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4] as each element is pushed left to right. Answer both parts:

(a) After pushing 1 (the third element), the heap exceeds size 2 and must pop. What gets popped, and what does the heap contain afterward?

(b) Fill in the blank: continuing this process through the rest of the array, the final heap holds {5, 6}, so the 2nd largest element is ___.

(a) Pushing 1 grows the heap to {1, 2, 3}, past the cap of 2, so the smallest value (1) is popped, leaving {2, 3}. (b) Continuing to push 5, 6, and 4 (each time popping the current smallest once the cap is exceeded) leaves the heap holding exactly the two largest values seen, {5, 6}; its minimum, 5, is the 2nd largest element overall.

7. Answer both parts about the complexity of the hash map plus bounded heap approach to Top K Frequent Elements:

(a) What is the overall time complexity, where u is the number of unique values?

(b) Fill in the blank: an alternative O(n) approach exploits the fact that frequency is bounded by array length, using ___ sort.

(a) Counting costs O(n); maintaining the heap costs O(log k) per unique value, of which there are at most n. (b) This groups values into buckets indexed by their frequency count, avoiding the heap entirely.

8. For nums = [1,1,1,2,2,3] with k=2, frequencies are 1→3, 2→2, 3→1. Pairs are pushed in that order into a heap capped at size 2, ordered by frequency. Answer both parts:

(a) What happens when the pair (frequency=1, value=3) is pushed?

(b) Fill in the blank: after processing every value, the heap holds the pairs for values 1 and 2, so the top 2 frequent elements are ___ and 2.

(a) Pushing (1,3) brings the heap to three entries, past the cap of 2, so the pair with the lowest frequency — (1,3) — is popped, correctly discarding the least frequent value. (b) What remains, (3,1) and (2,2), are exactly the two most frequent values, 1 and 2.

9. A heap is stored as a plain array, not linked nodes. Answer both parts:

(a) For a node at index i, where do its two children live?

(b) Fill in the blank: this array-based storage means the tree structure is entirely ___ — no explicit left/right pointers are ever stored.

(a) Index i's children live at 2i+1 and 2i+2 — this is exactly the formula Lesson 15 uses to store a heap without pointers. (b) The parent-child relationships are computed from array indices rather than stored directly, which is what "implicit" means here.

10. Answer both parts about heap support across this course's four languages:

(a) Which language has no built-in heap or priority queue, requiring a hand-written MinHeap class instead?

(b) Fill in the blank: Lesson 17's version of the hand-written heap extends Lesson 15's by accepting a ___ function, letting it order pairs by frequency instead of by raw value.

(a) Python's heapq, Java's PriorityQueue, and C++'s std::priority_queue are all built in; only JavaScript requires implementing a heap by hand, as shown across Lessons 15 through 17. (b) Passing a comparator function is what lets the exact same MinHeap class be reused to order by frequency in Lesson 17, instead of only by raw value as in Lesson 15.
