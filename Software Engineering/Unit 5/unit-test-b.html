Unit 5 Test — Version B

Software Architecture. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A team is deciding between two things: whether new error messages should be capitalized, and whether the system's components should call each other in-process versus communicate over a network as separate services. Which decision is architectural?

Cost of reversal, not user-facing visibility, is the defining test for whether a decision is architectural.

2. Lesson 13's YAGNI principle says not to build speculative flexibility now, since it's cheap to add later. Why is that reasoning a poor excuse for skipping upfront thought about a system's architecture?

The high cost of reversing an architectural decision is exactly why architecture gets more upfront deliberation than an ordinary choice.

3. A consultant claims there's one "best practice" architecture every serious company should adopt, regardless of size or team structure. What does this lesson say is wrong with that claim?

Architecture is a trade-off decision serving specific constraints, not a universal best-practice checklist.

4. Fill in the blank: when every part of an application runs in the same process, is deployed together, and can call other parts directly as ordinary function calls, that's called a ___ architecture.

A monolith is the single-codebase, single-process, single-deployment architectural style.

5. Fill in the blank: splitting a system into microservices means components communicate over a ___ rather than direct function calls, so calls between them can now fail in ways an in-process call structurally cannot.

Network calls between separate services introduce failure modes an in-process function call never has.

6. A twenty-person engineering org has three separate teams whose work constantly collides inside one shared codebase, and one specific reporting module needs to scale independently of everything else. Answer both parts:

(a) According to the lesson's decision framework, what does this situation suggest?

(b) Fill in the blank: the right question is never which approach is more modern — it's which specific ___ the system actually faces right now.

Real, current constraints — not trend or popularity — should drive the monolith-versus-microservices trade-off.

7. Class A calls Class B's decrement(itemId) method and knows nothing else about how B stores or manages its data. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this describe?

(b) Fill in the blank: if Class A instead built a direct database connection and wrote raw SQL against B's exact table schema, A and B would be tightly ___.

Depending on another component's internal implementation details, rather than just a stable interface, is what tight coupling means.

8. A class called ReportManager formats PDF reports, sends push notifications, and manages user login sessions, all in the same class. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this reveal?

(b) Fill in the blank: splitting ReportManager into ReportFormatter, NotificationSender, and SessionManager gives each piece one clear, ___ purpose.

Splitting unrelated responsibilities into separate classes is what raises cohesion — each piece gets one clear purpose.

9. An engineer claims coupling and cohesion are just two unrelated vocabulary words that happen to appear in the same lesson, with no deeper connection. Answer both parts:

(a) What's the actual relationship, per the lesson?

(b) Fill in the blank: separation of concerns holds that each part of a system should be responsible for one distinct ___, with clear boundaries between them.

Coupling and cohesion are the concrete, checkable signals underneath the broader separation-of-concerns principle.

10. A monolith slowly became tightly coupled and low-cohesion over two years, and nobody ever discussed or decided to let that happen — it just accumulated unnoticed. Answer both parts:

(a) What kind of technical debt does this describe, and why?

(b) Fill in the blank: unlike this case, a shortcut a team names out loud, understands the cost of, and can plan to pay down later is called ___ debt.

Accidental debt accumulates unchosen and untracked; deliberate debt is a conscious, plannable decision.
