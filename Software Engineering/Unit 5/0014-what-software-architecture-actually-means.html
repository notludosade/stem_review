What Software Architecture Actually Means

The high-level structural decisions that are expensive to change later, and why they deserve more upfront care than the rest.

← Unit 4, Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 4 covered patterns — decisions about the shape of individual classes and small groups of them. Software architecture operates one level up: the high-level structural decisions about how a whole system is organized, decisions that are dramatically more expensive to change once made.

What makes a decision "architectural"

Why some decisions are architectural and most aren't Most decisions an engineer makes day to day — naming a variable, choosing between two ways to loop over a list — are cheap to change later, usually a small, local edit. An architectural decision is one that's expensive and disruptive to reverse once a system is built around it: how the system is split into major components, how those components communicate, and what technology each layer depends on. The defining test isn't how important a decision feels — it's how expensive it would be to undo.
Example Choosing whether a function returns a list or a generator is a normal, cheap-to-reverse implementation decision. Choosing whether a system's components communicate by calling each other's functions directly in the same process, or by sending messages over a network as separate services, is architectural — reversing it later means touching nearly every part of the system, not a single function.

Why architecture deserves more upfront care

Why "we'll fix it later" doesn't apply the same way here Lesson 13's YAGNI principle argued against overbuilding flexibility speculatively — and that advice still holds for most decisions. Architecture is the deliberate exception: because these specific decisions are so expensive to reverse, they're worth genuinely more upfront analysis than an ordinary implementation choice, precisely because "add it later, cheaply, with tests" — the safety net Lesson 13 relied on — doesn't apply nearly as well once a system's fundamental structure is already built around a different assumption.

Architecture serves the system's actual constraints

There is no universally "best" architecture Architectural decisions exist to serve specific, real constraints a system faces — expected scale, team size, how independently different parts need to be developed and deployed, reliability requirements. A structure that's exactly right for one system's constraints can be actively wrong for another's, which is why the next lesson, comparing monoliths and microservices, frames the choice entirely around trade-offs rather than picking a universal winner.

Cost of reversal, not perceived importance or code volume, is what actually distinguishes an architectural decision.

The high reversal cost is exactly why architecture gets more upfront deliberation than an ordinary implementation choice.

Scale, team size, and reliability needs vary by system, which is exactly why architecture is a trade-off decision, not a universal best-practice checklist.
