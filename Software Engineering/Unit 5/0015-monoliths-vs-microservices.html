Monoliths vs. Microservices

One deployable system versus many independent services — and why neither is a universal upgrade over the other.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 14 established that architecture serves a system's real constraints. The monolith-versus-microservices choice is the most cited example of exactly that trade-off in modern practice — and one of the most commonly misjudged, since microservices are often reached for as a default "best practice" rather than a fit for a specific situation.

Monolith: one codebase, one deployable unit

What a monolith is A monolithic architecture builds an entire application as a single, unified codebase, deployed and run as one unit. Every part of the system runs in the same process and can call other parts directly, as ordinary function calls.
Why monoliths are usually the right starting point A monolith is simpler to develop, test, and deploy: one codebase to check out, one thing to run locally, no network calls between components to reason about or fail. For a new product, especially with a small team and requirements that are still actively changing, this simplicity is a genuine advantage, not just a lack of ambition — most successful large systems, including many now run as microservices, started as monoliths.

Microservices: many small, independently deployable services

What microservices are A microservices architecture splits a system into multiple small, independent services, each responsible for one specific piece of functionality, communicating over a network (commonly via HTTP or message queues) rather than as direct in-process function calls.
Why large organizations adopt microservices Independent services can be developed, tested, deployed, and scaled separately by separate teams — a real advantage once an organization has grown large enough that many teams working in one shared monolith start blocking each other, or once one specific part of the system needs to scale independently from the rest.

The real cost of microservices

Why microservices aren't a free upgrade Splitting a system into services introduces real, non-optional complexity: network calls can fail in ways in-process function calls never do, data that used to live in one place is now spread across multiple services with no single shared database, and testing (Unit 3) needs meaningfully more integration-level coverage to verify services still work correctly together. None of this complexity disappears — it's traded for the independence benefit, and for a small team or an early-stage product, that trade is very often a net loss, not a win.
Example A five-person startup splits its product into twelve microservices from day one, expecting to "scale like a big tech company." Instead, the team spends most of its time debugging network failures between services and keeping a dozen separate deployments in sync — complexity a monolith would never have introduced, for a system that, at five people, has no actual scaling or team-independence problem yet to solve.

The actual decision framework

Ask the constraint question, not the trend question Per Lesson 14, the right question is never "which is more modern" — it's which specific constraints this system actually has right now: is more than one team genuinely blocking on the same shared codebase? Does one specific part of the system have a scaling need wildly different from the rest? If neither is true yet, a monolith remains the simpler, usually correct choice — and per Unit 4's YAGNI principle, a well-structured monolith (Lesson 16 covers exactly what "well-structured" means) can often be split into services later, once a real constraint actually appears.

Simplicity of development and deployment is the concrete advantage a monolith offers a small, early-stage team.

Real, current constraints — not trend or popularity — should drive this specific architectural trade-off.
