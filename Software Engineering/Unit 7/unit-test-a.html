1. The Agile Manifesto lists values like favoring working software over exhaustive documentation and responding to change over following a rigid upfront plan. What do Scrum and Kanban represent relative to these values?

Agile is the values layer; Scrum and Kanban are specific, concrete implementations of those values.

2. Fill in the blank: in Scrum, work is organized into fixed-length iterations called ___, commonly one or two weeks long, where the team commits to a specific set of backlog work upfront.

A sprint is Scrum's fixed-length, committed iteration of work.

3. In Scrum, one role is responsible for prioritizing the backlog, a different role facilitates the process itself, and the team also holds a recurring meeting to reflect on how the process went. Which roles and ceremony does this describe?

Scrum defines a Product Owner and Scrum Master as specific roles, and the retrospective as the ceremony reflecting on process.

4. Fill in the blank: a Kanban ___ limit caps how many items can be "In Progress" at once, forcing the team to finish existing work before pulling in new work.

A work-in-progress (WIP) limit forces the team to finish existing work before starting more.

5. A company's product team plans a new feature over the next month, while its on-call support team handles bug reports that arrive unpredictably at any time. Which framework fits each team, and why?

Predictable, plannable work suits Scrum's fixed sprints, while unpredictable, as-it-arrives work suits Kanban's continuous flow.

6. A product manager writes a ticket for a promo-code feature: "Users can apply a promo code at checkout." As written, the engineer isn't told what happens with an invalid code, whether codes can stack, or whether they expire. Answer both parts:

(a) What must happen before this ticket is considered ready to prioritize into a sprint?

(b) Fill in the blank: catching this kind of ambiguity before implementation is far cheaper than catching it ___, exactly as Lesson 3 warned.

Clarifying vague acceptance criteria before a ticket enters a sprint avoids far more expensive ambiguity caught later.

7. While building the promo-code feature, the engineer notices the checkout system already has a Strategy pattern used for shipping-cost calculation, and reuses that same shape for discount calculation instead of writing a new branching if/elif chain. They also confirm the feature fits inside the existing checkout module without needing a new service. Answer both parts:

(a) What kind of decision is being made here?

(b) Fill in the blank: no new architectural decision is being made here because nothing about this choice would be expensive or disruptive to ___ later.

Reusing an existing pattern within the current structure is a cheap-to-reverse implementation choice, not an architectural one.

8. Supporting multiple stacked promo codes turns out to be far more complex than expected, and isn't required by the ticket's acceptance criteria. The engineer scopes it out and writes a comment plus a follow-up ticket explicitly naming the decision. Answer both parts:

(a) Why write the follow-up ticket instead of just leaving the feature out silently?

(b) Fill in the blank: naming the shortcut out loud turns it into ___ debt instead of an accidental gap.

A named, tracked shortcut is deliberate technical debt, distinct from an accidental gap nobody chose.

9. A teammate reviews the promo-code pull request and leaves one comment about a missing null check on an edge case, plus two comments suggesting different variable names, clearly marked as optional. Answer both parts:

(a) How should the engineer treat these comments, per the severity distinction from Lesson 19?

(b) Fill in the blank: rather than silently complying, the engineer explains their reasoning on one nit — the same engaged, non-___ response Lesson 19 recommends.

Blocking issues must be fixed before merging; nits are optional, and engaging with reasoning beats silent compliance or defensiveness.

10. At the end of the sprint, the retrospective surfaces that the promo-code ticket took longer than estimated, and traces the delay back to requirements that weren't fully clarified upfront. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this retrospective finding confirm?

(b) Fill in the blank: tracing this one ordinary feature through every unit's practice is what separates writing correct code from software ___ — the distinction the course opened with in Lesson 1.

The retrospective's root cause directly confirms Lesson 3's warning, and the capstone as a whole shows the difference between programming and engineering in practice.
