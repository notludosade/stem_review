Unit 7 Test — Version B

Agile Development in Practice. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A new engineer says "our team does Scrum, so we're agile," as if Scrum and agile were interchangeable terms. What's the more accurate relationship between them?

Agile is the values layer; Scrum and Kanban are specific, concrete implementations of those values.

2. Fill in the blank: a Scrum ___ is a fixed-length iteration, commonly one or two weeks, that ends with a review of what shipped and a reflection on the process.

A sprint is Scrum's fixed-length, committed iteration of work.

3. A team holds a short daily status sync, a sprint planning session, a demo of what shipped, and a meeting to reflect on how the process itself went. Which framework's ceremonies does this describe, and what are the two defined roles that go with them?

These specific recurring meetings and defined roles belong to Scrum, not to Kanban or waterfall.

4. Fill in the blank: Kanban has no fixed sprints — instead, a work-in-progress, or ___, limit caps how many items can be "In Progress" at once.

A WIP limit forces the team to finish existing work before starting more, instead of relying on fixed sprints.

5. A team building a planned, month-long feature adopts fixed two-week sprints, while a different team at the same company handling incoming production incidents uses a continuous board with a WIP limit instead. What explains the difference?

The kind of work — predictable versus unpredictable — is what should drive the choice between Scrum and Kanban.

6. The ticket for a promo-code feature is written only as "users can apply a promo code at checkout," with no mention of invalid codes, stacking, or expiration. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does this ticket need more work before an engineer should start on it?

(b) Fill in the blank: this is exactly the kind of vague requirement Lesson 3 warned needs ___ criteria before it's actually ready to build.

Clarifying vague acceptance criteria before a ticket enters a sprint avoids far more expensive ambiguity caught later.

7. Instead of writing a new branching if/elif chain for discount calculation, the engineer reuses the existing Strategy pattern already used for shipping costs, and confirms the feature fits inside the current checkout module. Answer both parts:

(a) Why isn't this considered a new architectural decision?

(b) Fill in the blank: it isn't architectural because it would be cheap, not disruptive, to ___ later if it turned out to be the wrong call.

Reusing an existing pattern within the current structure is a cheap-to-reverse implementation choice, not an architectural one.

8. The engineer decides to leave stacked promo codes out of this release since it isn't required by the acceptance criteria, and writes a comment plus a follow-up ticket explaining exactly what was skipped and why. Answer both parts:

(a) What does writing that follow-up ticket accomplish?

(b) Fill in the blank: an explicitly named and tracked shortcut like this is ___ technical debt, distinct from the accidental kind.

A named, tracked shortcut is deliberate technical debt, distinct from an accidental gap nobody chose.

9. During review of the promo-code pull request, a teammate flags a missing null check as something that must be fixed, and separately leaves two lightly-marked suggestions about variable naming. Answer both parts:

(a) Which comment must be addressed before the pull request can merge?

(b) Fill in the blank: on one of the naming nits, the engineer explains their reasoning rather than silently complying — the engaged response Lesson 19 recommends over blind compliance or ___.

Blocking issues must be fixed before merging; nits are optional, and engaging with reasoning beats silent compliance or defensiveness.

10. The sprint retrospective for the promo-code feature concludes that the ticket ran over its estimate specifically because the requirements weren't nailed down before work began. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this retrospective finding illustrate?

(b) Fill in the blank: following one ordinary feature through every unit's practice is what shows the real difference between writing correct code and software ___.

The retrospective's root cause directly confirms Lesson 3's warning, and the capstone as a whole shows the difference between programming and engineering in practice.
