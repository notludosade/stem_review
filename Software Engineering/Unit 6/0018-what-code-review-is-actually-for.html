What Code Review Is Actually For

Catching bugs is only one of three reasons every serious engineering team requires it before merging.

← Unit 5, Lesson 17 Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 6 introduced the pull request as the mechanism that adds human review before a merge. This lesson looks at what that review is actually accomplishing — and it turns out catching bugs, while real, is only one of three distinct jobs code review does.

Job 1: catching bugs and mistakes

A second set of eyes on unfamiliar code The most obvious purpose of code review: a reviewer who didn't write the code, and isn't anchored to the author's assumptions, is more likely to notice an edge case, a logic error, or a missed requirement. This overlaps with, but doesn't replace, the automated tests from Unit 3 — a human reviewer can catch things no test was written to check for, like a requirement that was subtly misunderstood in the first place.

Job 2: spreading knowledge across the team

Why review prevents a codebase from having "single points of failure" Every review is a chance for someone besides the original author to understand a piece of the system. Without review, knowledge of any given part of the codebase concentrates in whoever wrote it — a real risk if that person leaves, goes on vacation, or simply forgets the details months later. Review spreads that understanding across the team continuously, as a side effect of the normal workflow.

Job 3: keeping the codebase consistent

A shared, enforced standard Review is where a team's shared conventions — naming, structure, when a pattern from Unit 4 actually fits (or is overkill, per Lesson 13), how much test coverage a change needs — actually get enforced in practice, rather than existing only as a document nobody reads. A codebase reviewed consistently by the same team tends to read like it was written by one disciplined author, even with dozens of contributors.

Why all three jobs matter more than any one alone

Why "review just to catch bugs" undersells the practice A team that treats review purely as bug-catching often skips it under time pressure, reasoning "the tests will catch anything important." That reasoning misses the other two jobs entirely — knowledge stays siloed, and consistency erodes — costs that don't show up immediately but compound exactly like the technical debt from Lesson 17. Recognizing all three purposes is what makes skipping review under pressure look like the real trade-off it actually is, not a harmless shortcut.
Example A senior engineer's pull request has no bugs and passes every test — but a reviewer still leaves a comment: "this duplicates logic that already exists in PricingService; worth reusing that instead?" No bug was caught, yet the review still did real work: catching duplication, spreading knowledge of PricingService's existence, and keeping the codebase's structure consistent.
