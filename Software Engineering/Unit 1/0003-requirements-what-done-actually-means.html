Requirements: What "Done" Actually Means

Functional vs. non-functional requirements, and why ambiguous acceptance criteria cause more rework than bad code.

← Lesson 2 Unit 2, Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 2 named requirements as the first SDLC phase, and the one where mistakes are cheapest to catch. This lesson looks at what a requirement actually is, and why a surprising amount of professional software's rework traces back not to bugs in the code, but to a requirement nobody pinned down clearly enough.

Functional vs. non-functional requirements

Functional requirements What the system must do — specific behaviors, features, and outputs. "Users can reset their password via email" and "the search bar returns results ranked by relevance" are both functional requirements: each describes an observable behavior you could write a test for.
Non-functional requirements How well the system must do it — introduced in Lesson 1 as properties like performance, reliability, security, and maintainability. "Password reset emails must arrive within 30 seconds" and "the system must support 10,000 concurrent users" are non-functional requirements: the feature could exist without them, but the system would fail its actual purpose if it didn't meet them.
Example "The app has a checkout button" is functional — it either exists and works, or it doesn't. "Checkout completes in under two seconds at peak holiday traffic" is non-functional — the button already works, but this requirement is about how well it holds up under real conditions. Missing either one is a real engineering failure, even though only the first would show up as a broken feature in a casual demo.

Acceptance criteria: turning a requirement into a test

Why a requirement needs a concrete, checkable definition of "done" "The login page should be user-friendly" is a requirement nobody can actually verify — two engineers could both believe they've satisfied it and disagree completely. Acceptance criteria are the specific, checkable conditions that turn a vague requirement into something a team can agree is either met or not met — ideally phrased so directly that they map almost one-to-one onto the tests from Unit 3.
Example Vague: "the login page should be user-friendly." With acceptance criteria: "a user can log in with a valid email and password in under three clicks; an invalid password shows a specific error message within one second; the page is usable via keyboard alone." The second version is something a tester, a designer, and an engineer can all check independently and reach the same verdict.

Why ambiguity here is expensive

Why this is the cheapest phase to get wrong, and the most expensive one to leave wrong A misunderstood requirement caught during a requirements conversation costs a few minutes of clarification. The same misunderstanding, if it survives into design, gets built into an architecture; if it survives into implementation, gets built into working code; if it survives into testing, might even get validated by a test that was written against the same wrong assumption. Each phase a bad requirement survives multiplies the cost of catching and fixing it — which is why experienced engineers spend real, deliberate time pushing back on vague requirements before writing a line of code.

Requirements change — and that's expected, not a failure

Requirements aren't fixed forever A requirement captured today can be genuinely correct today and wrong in six months, once the business changes, a competitor moves, or users behave differently than expected. Good requirements practice isn't about predicting the future perfectly — it's about writing requirements precisely enough to build against now, while accepting that some of them will need to be revisited later. Unit 7 covers how agile processes are specifically built around that expectation.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which of these is a functional requirement rather than a non-functional one?

Functional requirements describe a specific behavior or feature; the other three describe how well the system performs, which is non-functional.

2. What problem do acceptance criteria specifically solve?

Acceptance criteria exist so that "done" has one agreed-upon meaning instead of a vague, subjective one like "user-friendly."

3. Why does a misunderstood requirement get more expensive to fix the later it's caught?

A wrong assumption compounds as later phases build on it, so unwinding it later means unwinding everything built on top of it too.

4. Fill in the blank: ___ criteria are the specific, checkable conditions that turn a vague requirement into something a team can agree is either met or not met.

Acceptance criteria are what make a requirement testable instead of a matter of opinion.

5. Fill in the blank: good requirements practice accepts that requirements will ___ over time, rather than trying to predict the future perfectly up front.

Requirements that were correct when written can become outdated as the business or its users change — that's expected, not a planning failure.
