Unit 1 Test — Version B

The Software Development Lifecycle. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A weekend script that renames files on one laptop, for one person, only has to be correct once. A production billing system used by a company has to stay correct for years, across dozens of engineers. What does this contrast illustrate?

Programming targets correctness for a given input; engineering additionally targets correctness that survives time, teams, and changing requirements.

2. Which pair of dimensions does Lesson 1 name as turning shortcuts that are harmless in a one-off script into real costs in professional software?

Scale (other engineers) and lifetime (years of use) are exactly the two dimensions that turn shortcuts into real costs.

3. Which of these is described in Lesson 1 as a non-functional requirement rather than a matter of raw correctness?

Non-functional requirements — performance, reliability, security, maintainability — are properties separate from whether an output is correct.

4. Fill in the blank: writing the actual code that carries out a solution's design is the SDLC phase called ___ — the phase Computer Programming 1 and 2 focused on almost entirely.

Implementation is usually the smallest slice of a system's total lifetime cost, even though it's the most visible phase.

5. Fill in the blank: fixing bugs, patching security issues, and adapting a system to changing requirements for as long as it stays in use is the SDLC phase called ___.

Maintenance is usually most of a system's total lifetime and where most engineering hours across a career actually get spent.

6. A team's process requires that requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance happen exactly once, strictly in that order, with no phase ever revisited. Answer both parts:

(a) What is this rigid process called, and how do most real teams actually operate?

(b) Fill in the blank: maintenance often surfaces a new requirement, restarting the whole cycle at a smaller scale — showing the six phases describe kinds of ___ that exist, not a strict pipeline.

Waterfall is the strict once-through version of the SDLC; real teams routinely loop back once a later phase reveals a problem.

7. One requirement reads: "the system must remain available 99.9% of the time." A second reads: "users can reset their password via a link sent by email." Answer both parts:

(a) Which one is the functional requirement?

(b) Fill in the blank: "99.9% availability" is a ___ requirement, since it describes how well the system holds up rather than a specific feature.

Functional requirements describe a specific behavior; describing how well the system holds up instead makes a requirement non-functional.

8. Two engineers both believe they've satisfied the requirement "the login page should be easy to use," yet they built very different things. Answer both parts:

(a) What is missing from this requirement?

(b) Fill in the blank: rewriting it as "a user can log in with a valid password in under three clicks; an invalid password shows an error within one second" gives the requirement concrete ___ criteria.

Acceptance criteria are what let a tester, a designer, and an engineer all check a requirement independently and reach the same verdict.

9. A misunderstood requirement survives all the way into a shipped, tested feature before anyone notices the mistake. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does catching it this late cost more than catching it during the initial requirements conversation?

(b) Fill in the blank: a bad requirement that survives all the way into a written ___ might even get validated by a check written against the same wrong assumption.

Each phase a bad requirement survives multiplies the cost of catching and fixing it later.

10. A requirement that was completely correct when the team wrote it becomes outdated once a competitor forces the product to pivot. Answer both parts:

(a) What does Lesson 3 say this demonstrates?

(b) Fill in the blank: Unit 7's ___ processes are built specifically around the expectation that requirements will keep changing.

Requirements that were correct when written can become outdated as the business or its users change — that's expected, not a planning failure.
