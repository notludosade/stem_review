Balancing and Data-Driven Mods (Stats, Drop Tables, Config-Only Changes)

Unit 2, Lesson 6 introduced the config-file edit as the safest possible entry point to modding. This lesson scales that same idea up to entire systems — without adding a single new asset or line of script.

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

A data-driven mod changes nothing but the numbers and structures already exposed in a game's config files — no new textures, no new models, no new scripts. This is the category game balance mods live in almost entirely: adjusting weapon damage, enemy health, resource costs, or spawn rates until the game feels fair and fun again, purely by editing values the developer already made adjustable. Because it touches no copyrighted assets and requires no programming, it's simultaneously the lowest-legal-risk and lowest-technical-barrier category in this entire course — and, because a single well-chosen number can reshape how an entire game plays, one of the highest-impact.

Definition A drop table is the data structure defining what items an enemy, chest, or event can produce, and at what odds — a classic target for data-driven mods that want to change the game's economy or difficulty without touching any code.

Why balance changes need testing, not just intuition

A weapon's raw damage number in isolation says very little — what actually matters to a player is DPS (damage per second), which factors in attack speed too: a weapon dealing 10 damage at 2 attacks per second outputs the same DPS as one dealing 20 damage at 1 attack per second, even though the two numbers look completely different in the config file. Good balance modding means computing and comparing the numbers that actually matter to gameplay, not just eyeballing individual fields in isolation — a mistake even experienced modders make when a config file has many interacting values.

Why it matters here This lesson closes Unit 4 by returning to where Unit 2 started: a plain-text value. Every technique in between — asset formats, scripting, textures, audio — expands what a mod can touch, but data-driven changes remain the simplest, safest, and often most effective category of all.
