The Legal and Ethical Boundaries of Modding

Buying a game doesn't mean owning its code and assets. Every technique in this course operates inside limits set by a license you agreed to and a copyright you didn't write — this lesson is about seeing those limits clearly before using any of the tools that follow.

Buying a copy of a game gives a player a license to play it — not ownership of its code, art, music, or writing. That distinction sits underneath every mod ever made. Studios still hold copyright over their assets and code even after a player owns a disc, a download, or a Steam library entry, and every player agrees to a EULA (End User License Agreement) that spells out what that license does and doesn't permit, usually without reading it. Most EULAs say nothing explicit about modding at all; some explicitly permit it for personal, non-commercial use; a few explicitly forbid it. Which one applies to a specific game is a real question worth actually checking, not assuming.

Definition A EULA is the license agreement a player accepts when installing or playing a game, setting the legal terms under which the developer's copyrighted work may be used — separate from, and usually far more restrictive than, simply owning a copy.

What "fair use" actually covers

Fair use (in the U.S.; other countries have their own, differently-shaped doctrines like "fair dealing") is a legal defense that can excuse using copyrighted material without permission — for purposes like commentary, criticism, or parody — but it is not a blanket permission slip for modding. Courts weigh fair use case by case, looking at things like how much of the original was used and whether the new work harms the market for the original. A mod that adds new, original content while requiring the base game to run is on much safer ground than one that repackages and redistributes the original developer's assets wholesale. "I made a mod, so it must be fair use" is not how the doctrine actually works.

What's generally allowed versus generally not

In practice, across most commercial games: using a developer-provided modding tool or SDK as intended is almost always fine — the developer built it for exactly this. Personal, non-commercial mods that require owning the base game to run are usually tolerated even without an explicit SDK. What crosses a line more often: redistributing the original game's copyrighted assets outside of a mod that requires the base game, selling a mod built on someone else's IP without permission, and circumventing DRM or anti-cheat systems to make modding possible (Unit 5 covers exactly where that crosses into DMCA territory). None of this is a substitute for reading a specific game's actual EULA — it changes game to game.

Why it matters here Every technique in Units 2 through 6 — unpacking archives, scripting, swapping assets, reverse-engineering, publishing — is a technical capability, not a legal green light. Whether a specific use of that capability is allowed depends on the specific game's EULA and, sometimes, on national copyright law. This lesson is the filter every later lesson's techniques should pass through.
