Uniform Circular Motion and Centripetal Force

Constant speed, constantly changing velocity: moving in a circle is accelerating, even when the speedometer never budges.

← Lesson 5 Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Uniform circular motion is motion along a circular path at constant speed. "Constant speed" does not mean "constant velocity" — velocity is a vector (Unit 1, Lesson 5), and an object moving in a circle constantly changes direction, so its velocity vector is constantly changing even though its magnitude never does.

Changing direction is still acceleration Lesson 2 defined acceleration as any change in velocity — and a vector changes if its direction changes, even at fixed magnitude. A car rounding a curve at a steady 30 mph is accelerating, in the everyday-language sense of "not just going in a straight line," which is exactly circular motion's defining feature.

This acceleration — called centripetal acceleration — points toward the center of the circle at every instant, perpendicular to the velocity (which is always tangent to the circle). Its magnitude is:

ac = v² / r

where v is the (constant) speed and r is the circle's radius. By Newton's second law, this acceleration requires a net force pointing toward the center too — called the centripetal force:

Fc = mac = mv² / r
"Centripetal" names a direction, not a new kind of force Centripetal force is not a distinct physical force like gravity, tension, friction, or the normal force — it's a label for whichever real force (or combination of forces) happens to be supplying the center-pointing net force in a given problem. For a car turning on a flat road, it's friction; for a ball on a string, it's tension; for a satellite, it's gravity (Lesson 7). Every circular-motion problem asks "what force is playing the centripetal role here?"
Example: a ball on a string A 0.5 kg ball moves in a horizontal circle of radius 1.2 m at 4 m/s, held by a string. Find the tension. The string's tension is the only horizontal force, so it must supply the entire centripetal force: T = mv²/r = 0.5(4²)/1.2 = 6.67 N.
Example: a car on a flat curve A 1200 kg car rounds a flat curve of radius 50 m at the maximum speed before it starts to skid, where static friction is at its maximum μsN. Setting friction equal to the required centripetal force: μsmg = mv²/r → v = √(μsgr). With μs = 0.6: v = √(0.6 × 9.8 × 50) = 17.1 m/s — notice the car's mass cancels out entirely.

There is no "centrifugal force" pushing outward

The sensation of being "thrown outward" in a turning car is Newton's first law in disguise: your body's inertia wants to continue in a straight line, and the car (via the seat, the door, friction) is the one actually accelerating you inward, toward the center. What feels like an outward push is really your own inertia resisting the inward force — there is no real outward force acting on you at all. In an inertial (non-rotating) reference frame, "centrifugal force" doesn't appear in the force inventory.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. An object moves in a circle at constant speed. Which statement is correct?

Velocity's direction is constantly changing even at fixed speed, which is itself an acceleration — directed toward the circle's center, called centripetal acceleration.

2. In a ball-on-a-string horizontal circular motion problem, which force supplies the centripetal force?

Centripetal force isn't its own separate force — here, the string's tension is the real force providing the center-directed net force.

3. A passenger in a sharply turning car feels "thrown outward" against the door. What is actually happening?

There's no real outward force — the passenger's own inertia (Newton's first law) resists being turned, and the door supplies the actual inward force needed to make them turn with the car.

4. Fill in the blank: the acceleration of an object in uniform circular motion, directed toward the center, is called ___ acceleration.

Centripetal ("center-seeking") acceleration is the standard term, with magnitude v²/r.
