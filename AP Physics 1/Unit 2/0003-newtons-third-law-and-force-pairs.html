Newton's Third Law and Force Pairs

Every force is one half of a pair — and the two halves never act on the same object, which is exactly why they don't cancel each other out.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Newton's Third Law For every force one object exerts on a second object, the second object exerts a force on the first that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. These two forces are called an action-reaction pair.
Why the pair never cancels A common error: "if every force has an equal and opposite reaction, how does anything ever accelerate?" The answer is that the two forces in a pair act on different objects — Newton's second law only sums forces acting on one object at a time. When you push a wall, the wall pushes back on you with equal force — but that reaction force acts on you, not on the wall, and the wall's own forces (from the floor, from the rest of the building) are what keep it in equilibrium. The two paired forces never appear together in the same ΣF for any single object.
Example: walking Walking works because of a third-law pair. Your foot pushes backward on the ground (action); the ground pushes forward on your foot (reaction) — and that forward push on you is what accelerates you forward. Without friction to let your foot exert that backward push in the first place (ice, for instance), the reaction force vanishes too, and walking becomes impossible.
Example: rocket propulsion A rocket pushes exhaust gas backward out of its engines (action); the gas pushes the rocket forward with equal force (reaction). No air or ground to push against is needed — the pair is between the rocket and the exhaust gas itself, which is why rockets work in the vacuum of space.

Identifying a true third-law pair

Two forces form a genuine action-reaction pair only if they meet three conditions: they act on different objects, they are the same type of force (both gravitational, or both a contact push, matching what's producing them), and they occur simultaneously — one never exists without the other. This rules out common mix-ups: a book's weight (gravity, Earth pulling on the book) and the table's support force on the book (a contact force) are not a third-law pair, even though they're equal and opposite in Lesson 1's equilibrium example — they're different force types acting on the same object, which is a Lesson 1 fact, not a Lesson 3 one.

The classic mix-up, resolved Book on table: weight (Earth → book) and normal force (table → book) both act on the book and happen to be equal because the book is in equilibrium (Lesson 1). Weight's actual third-law partner is the book pulling up on the Earth with equal gravitational force. The normal force's actual partner is the book pushing down on the table with equal contact force. Four forces, two genuine pairs — not the two you'd first guess.

1. Two forces form a genuine Newton's third law pair only if they:

A true action-reaction pair acts on two different objects, is the same force type, and exists at the same time — this is what distinguishes it from two unrelated forces that merely happen to be equal.

2. A book rests on a table. The book's weight and the table's normal force on the book are equal in magnitude. Are they a Newton's third law pair?

Both forces act on the book itself — they're equal only because the book is in equilibrium (Newton's first law), not because they're a third-law pair, which requires acting on two different objects.

3. A rocket accelerates forward in the vacuum of space by expelling exhaust gas backward. This is possible because:

The action-reaction pair is between the rocket and the exhaust gas directly — no external medium like air or ground is needed for the third law to operate.

4. Fill in the blank: the two forces described by Newton's third law are together called an ___ pair.

Action-reaction pair is the standard name for these two equal-and-opposite forces acting on two different objects.
