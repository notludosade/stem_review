Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

The same force that drops an apple holds the Moon in orbit — one equation, one constant, for every pair of masses in the universe.

← Lesson 6 Unit 3, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Every object with mass attracts every other object with mass. Newton's insight — startling for its time — was that the force pulling an apple down and the force keeping the Moon in orbit are the exact same phenomenon, governed by one equation:

Fg = Gm1m2 / r²

where m1 and m2 are the two masses, r is the distance between their centers, and G is the universal gravitational constant, G ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² — the same tiny number, everywhere in the universe, for every pair of masses.

Why gravity is only noticeable with huge masses G's tiny value is why gravitational attraction between everyday objects — two people standing near each other — is utterly imperceptible; it only becomes noticeable when at least one mass is planet-sized. This is also why weight (W = mg, Lesson 2) works as a separate, simpler formula for objects near Earth's surface: mg is just Gm1m2/r² with m1 fixed as Earth's mass and r fixed as Earth's radius, bundled into the single constant g ≈ 9.8 m/s².

An inverse-square law

Because r appears squared in the denominator, gravitational force falls off sharply with distance: doubling the distance between two masses cuts the force to one-quarter, not one-half. Tripling the distance cuts it to one-ninth. This inverse-square pattern — recurring later for electric force in AP Physics 2 — means gravity, while acting over infinite range in principle, becomes negligible quickly as distance grows.

Example: force between Earth and a satellite A 500 kg satellite orbits at a distance of 7 × 10⁶ m from Earth's center (Earth's mass ≈ 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg). Fg = (6.67×10⁻¹¹)(5.97×10²⁴)(500) / (7×10⁶)² ≈ 4062 N.

Gravity as the centripetal force for orbits

Lesson 6 established that "centripetal force" is a role, not a distinct force — for any object in a stable circular orbit, gravity itself supplies that centripetal force. Setting Lesson 6's Fc = mv²/r equal to this lesson's Fg = GMm/r² (M the central body's mass, m the orbiting object's mass) gives the orbital speed needed for a circular orbit at radius r:

GMm/r² = mv²/r ⟹ v = √(GM/r)

Notice the orbiting object's own mass m cancels completely — every object at the same orbital radius, regardless of its own mass, needs the same speed to stay in a circular orbit. This is the same mass-cancellation seen in Unit 1's free fall: gravity's effect on motion doesn't depend on the mass being pulled.

Example: low Earth orbit speed For a satellite orbiting just above Earth's surface (r ≈ 6.4 × 10⁶ m, Earth's mass M ≈ 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg): v = √[(6.67×10⁻¹¹)(5.97×10²⁴) / (6.4×10⁶)] ≈ 7,900 m/s — nearly 8 km/s, regardless of the satellite's own mass.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. If the distance between two masses is tripled, the gravitational force between them becomes:

Gravitational force follows an inverse-square law: F ∝ 1/r², so tripling r divides force by 3² = 9.

2. Why does an object's own mass cancel out of the formula for the speed needed to maintain a circular orbit?

Setting GMm/r² = mv²/r, the orbiting mass m appears on both sides and divides out, leaving v = √(GM/r) — independent of the orbiting object's own mass.

3. Weight, W = mg, is best understood as:

W = mg is F_g = GMm/r² with M (Earth's mass) and r (Earth's radius) fixed constants, bundled together into g ≈ 9.8 m/s² for convenience near Earth's surface.

4. Fill in the blank: because force is proportional to 1/r², gravitation is described as an ___ law.

Inverse-square describes any quantity that falls off as 1/r² — gravitation is the first and most famous example in this course.
