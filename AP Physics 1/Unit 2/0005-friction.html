Friction

Every "frictionless" example so far was an idealization. Friction is the real-world force that opposes sliding — and it comes in two distinct flavors.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Friction is a contact force between two surfaces that opposes relative sliding (or the tendency to slide) between them. It arises from microscopic roughness and molecular adhesion between the surfaces, and always acts parallel to the contact surface — never perpendicular, which is the normal force's job.

Static vs. kinetic friction Static friction (fs) acts between surfaces that are not sliding relative to each other, and adjusts its magnitude — up to a maximum — to exactly prevent sliding from starting. Kinetic friction (fk) acts between surfaces that are sliding, with an approximately constant magnitude regardless of speed.
fs ≤ μsN (static, self-adjusting up to this maximum)
fk = μkN (kinetic, essentially constant while sliding)

μs and μk (the Greek letter mu) are the coefficients of static and kinetic friction — dimensionless numbers depending only on the two materials in contact, not on the surface area touching or the sliding speed. For essentially every pair of real surfaces, μs > μk: it takes more force to start something sliding than to keep it sliding once moving.

Why static friction is an inequality, not an equation Push gently on a heavy couch and it doesn't move — static friction has matched your push exactly, whatever it was, up to some point. Push harder and eventually it starts to slide: you've exceeded μsN, static friction's maximum available value. This is why fs ≤ μsN is written as an inequality with an equals-or-less-than sign: static friction supplies exactly what's needed to prevent sliding, capped at that maximum.
Example: does it slide? A 20 kg crate sits on a floor with μs = 0.4. Is a 60 N horizontal push enough to start it sliding? Maximum static friction: μsN = 0.4(20)(9.8) = 78.4 N. Since 60 N < 78.4 N, static friction matches the push (fs = 60 N) and the crate does not move.
Example: sliding at constant velocity Once sliding, the same 20 kg crate is kept moving at constant velocity by a horizontal push. Given μk = 0.3, kinetic friction is fk = μkN = 0.3(20)(9.8) = 58.8 N. For constant velocity (Lesson 1: zero net force), the applied push must exactly equal 58.8 N — no more, no less.

Friction on an incline

Combining Lesson 4's tilted-axis technique with friction: on an incline, the normal force is N = mg cos θ (from balancing perpendicular to the slope), so friction becomes f = μN = μmg cos θ, acting up the slope if the object tends to slide down, or down the slope if something is pushing it up. Whether an object placed on an incline stays put or slides depends on comparing mg sin θ (gravity's pull down the slope) to the maximum available static friction, μsmg cos θ.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which is generally true about the two friction coefficients for a given pair of surfaces?

It typically takes more force to start an object sliding than to keep it sliding, so the static coefficient is generally larger than the kinetic one.

2. A box is pushed with a force less than the maximum static friction available. The box:

Static friction self-adjusts to match the applied force exactly, up to its maximum value — as long as the push doesn't exceed that maximum, the box stays put.

3. The friction coefficients μs and μk primarily depend on:

μ is a dimensionless property of the pair of materials in contact — it does not depend on contact area, sliding speed, or the object's mass directly.

4. Fill in the blank: both friction formulas, f ≤ μsN and f = μkN, depend on N, the ___ force.

Friction is proportional to the normal force pressing the two surfaces together — more normal force means more available friction.
