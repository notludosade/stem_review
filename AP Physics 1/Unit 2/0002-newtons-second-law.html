Newton's Second Law

The single most-used equation in this entire course: net force causes acceleration, in direct proportion, inversely scaled by mass.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 established what happens when net force is zero. Newton's second law describes what happens the rest of the time — whenever forces don't cancel.

Newton's Second Law The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, in the same direction as that net force, and inversely proportional to its mass: ΣF = ma, or equivalently a = ΣF / m. ΣF (read "sum of F") denotes the net force — the vector sum of every force acting on the object.
Why mass is the resistance term Push a shopping cart and a loaded truck with the same force, and the cart accelerates far more. Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia from Lesson 1 — how strongly an object resists a change in its velocity. The second law makes that resistance precise: for the same net force, doubling the mass exactly halves the acceleration.

Working with ΣF = ma

Because force is a vector, ΣF = ma is really two separate scalar equations in disguise — one per axis: ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may. Every force acting on an object gets resolved into x- and y-components (Unit 1, Lesson 5's tool), then each axis's forces are summed and set equal to mass times that axis's acceleration, entirely independently of the other axis.

Example A 2 kg block is pushed across a frictionless floor by a horizontal force of 6 N. Find its acceleration. ΣF = ma → 6 = 2a → a = 3 m/s². Since the floor is frictionless and the push is purely horizontal, no other horizontal force needs to be considered.
Example: two forces, opposite directions A 5 kg box is pulled right with 20 N and simultaneously pulled left with 8 N. Net force: ΣF = 20 − 8 = 12 N to the right. Acceleration: a = 12/5 = 2.4 m/s², to the right — the same direction as the net force, per the second law.

Units: the newton, defined

The second law is where the unit of force comes from. A newton (N) is defined as exactly the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s²: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². Every force in this course — weight, tension, friction, the normal force, and more, all introduced across the rest of this unit — is measured in newtons because they all plug into this same equation.

Weight as an application of the second law

An object's weight is the gravitational force on it, and it follows directly from the second law applied to free fall (Unit 1, Lesson 4): since a falling object's acceleration is g, the force causing that acceleration must be W = mg. Weight is a force (measured in newtons), fundamentally different from mass (measured in kilograms) — mass is how much matter an object has and doesn't change from place to place; weight depends on the local gravitational acceleration and would be different on the Moon.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A constant net force is applied to two different masses, one twice as large as the other. Compared to the smaller mass, the larger mass's acceleration is:

a = ΣF/m: for the same net force, doubling the mass halves the acceleration — the second law's inverse proportionality.

2. A 4 kg object is pulled right with 15 N and left with 7 N at the same time. Its acceleration is:

Net force = 15 − 7 = 8 N right. a = 8/4 = 2 m/s², in the direction of the net force (right).

3. An astronaut's mass is 70 kg on Earth. On the Moon, where gravity is weaker, their mass is:

Mass measures the amount of matter (and inertia) an object has and is the same everywhere. Weight = mg changes with location because g changes; mass does not.

4. Fill in the blank: for a given net force, the quantity that determines how much acceleration results is the object's ___.

a = ΣF/m — mass is the term relating a given net force to the resulting acceleration.
