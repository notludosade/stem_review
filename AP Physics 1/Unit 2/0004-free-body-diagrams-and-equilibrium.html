Free-Body Diagrams and Equilibrium

The single most useful habit in all of dynamics: isolate one object, draw every force acting on it, and nothing else.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

A free-body diagram is a simplified sketch showing a single object, isolated from everything else in the scenario, with an arrow drawn for every force acting on it — never a force it exerts on something else (that belongs on a different object's diagram, per Lesson 3).

The normal force The normal force (N) is the contact force a surface exerts on an object, always directed perpendicular ("normal") to that surface, pushing away from it. It's not a fixed value — it adjusts to whatever is needed to prevent the object from passing through the surface, which is why N isn't simply equal to weight once other forces or angles are involved.
Tension Tension (T) is the pulling force transmitted through a stretched string, rope, or cable, directed along the rope, away from the object, toward whatever is pulling. An ideal (massless, inextensible) rope has the same tension throughout its length and at both ends.

The equilibrium method

Once every force is drawn and resolved into x- and y-components (Unit 1, Lesson 5), an object in equilibrium (Lesson 1: zero net force) must satisfy both ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 simultaneously. This turns a diagram into a solvable system of algebraic equations.

Example: hanging block A 5 kg block hangs at rest from a single vertical rope. Forces: tension T (up), weight W = mg (down). Equilibrium: T − mg = 0 → T = mg = 5(9.8) = 49 N.
Example: block on a frictionless incline A block rests on a frictionless ramp inclined at angle θ, held in place by a rope parallel to the ramp surface. Tilting the coordinate axes to align with the ramp (a standard trick), gravity splits into a component mg sin θ pulling the block down the slope and mg cos θ pressing it into the surface. Along the slope: T − mg sin θ = 0 → T = mg sin θ. Perpendicular to the slope: N − mg cos θ = 0 → N = mg cos θ. Tilting the axes turned two forces (gravity split two ways) plus tension plus normal force into two clean, independent equations.
Why tilt the axes on an incline Choosing x and y along the standard horizontal/vertical directions works, but forces the normal force and tension — both aligned with the ramp — into awkward components on both axes. Tilting the coordinate system to match the incline instead makes N and T fall entirely on one axis each, at the cost of only gravity needing to be split into two components. It's the same physics either way — just far less algebra.

1. A free-body diagram for a single object should include:

A free-body diagram isolates one object and shows every force acting on it — forces it exerts on other objects belong on those objects' own diagrams instead.

2. A 10 kg block hangs at rest from a single vertical rope. The rope's tension is:

For a block hanging at rest, equilibrium requires T = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N.

3. On a frictionless incline problem, why is it often easier to tilt the x- and y-axes to align with the slope?

Tilted axes are a computational convenience, not a change in physics — they minimize how many forces need to be split into components.

4. Fill in the blank: the contact force a surface exerts perpendicular to itself, pushing an object away, is called the ___ force.

The normal force is defined as always perpendicular to the contact surface, adjusting its magnitude to whatever prevents the object from passing through.
