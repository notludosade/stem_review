Newton's First Law and Inertia

Unit 1 described motion without asking why. Unit 2 opens with the most counterintuitive fact in mechanics: moving objects don't need a push to keep moving.

← Unit 1, Lesson 6 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Aristotle believed that objects need a continuous push to keep moving — that rest is the "natural" state and motion decays without effort. Everyday experience seems to confirm this: roll a ball across a floor and it eventually stops. Newton's first law says this intuition is backwards.

Newton's First Law An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity (constant speed and direction), unless acted on by a net external force. This property — resistance to a change in motion — is called inertia.
Why the ball actually stops The rolling ball doesn't stop because motion "runs out." It stops because friction and air resistance are forces acting on it the whole time, continuously slowing it down. Remove those forces — a puck on frictionless ice, a spacecraft in the vacuum of deep space — and the object coasts at constant velocity indefinitely. What looks like "motion needs a cause" is really "changing motion needs a cause," and friction was quietly supplying that cause all along.

Net force is what matters

The first law doesn't say "no forces act" — it says the net force (the vector sum of every force acting on the object) is zero. An object can have several forces acting on it simultaneously and still move at constant velocity, or sit at rest, as long as those forces cancel out. A book resting on a table has gravity pulling down and the table pushing up (Unit 2, Lesson 4 names this the normal force) — two real forces, summing to zero net force, which is exactly why the book doesn't accelerate.

Example A hockey puck sliding across frictionless ice moves at constant velocity forever, with zero net force acting on it (gravity and the ice's support force cancel vertically; nothing acts horizontally). The moment it reaches a rough patch, friction introduces a net horizontal force, and the puck's velocity starts to change — this is Newton's second law's territory, in Lesson 2.

Equilibrium and reference frames

An object with zero net force is said to be in equilibrium — this includes both objects at rest (static equilibrium) and objects moving at constant velocity (dynamic equilibrium). Newton's first law only holds exactly in an inertial reference frame — one that is itself not accelerating. Inside an accelerating frame (a braking car, a spinning carousel), objects appear to move as if pushed by forces that don't actually exist — a subtlety AP Physics 1 mostly sets aside, but worth knowing exists.

Check your recall

1. According to Newton's first law, an object moving at constant velocity in a straight line requires:

Constant velocity means zero acceleration, which by the first law means zero net force — individual forces may still be present, as long as they sum to zero.

2. A book sits motionless on a table. Which statement is correct?

Two real forces act — gravity down, the table's support force up — and they sum to zero net force, which is exactly why the book stays at rest.

3. A ball rolling across a real (non-frictionless) floor gradually slows down and stops. This happens because:

Friction supplies a real, continuous net force opposing motion — the first law still holds; it's simply not the case that zero net force is acting here.

4. Fill in the blank: an object's resistance to a change in its motion is called ___.

Inertia is exactly this property — the tendency of an object to keep doing whatever it's already doing unless a net force intervenes.
