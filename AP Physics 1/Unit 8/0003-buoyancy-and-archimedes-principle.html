Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

A steel ship floats for the same reason a rubber duck does — it's all about displaced water.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Archimedes' Principle The buoyant force on an object submerged (fully or partly) in a fluid equals the weight of the fluid it displaces: Fb = ρfluidVg, where V is the volume of fluid displaced. This buoyant force points upward, opposing gravity.
Where the buoyant force comes from Pressure increases with depth (Lesson 1), so the fluid pressure pushing up on the bottom of a submerged object is greater than the fluid pressure pushing down on its top — this pressure difference, summed over the object's surface, produces a net upward force. Since the pressure at any depth depends only on the fluid above it (Lesson 1's P = P₀ + ρgh) and not on what's submerged there, this net force works out to exactly the weight of the fluid that used to occupy that same volume.
Floating vs. Sinking An object floats if its average density is less than the fluid's density (it displaces its own weight in fluid before being fully submerged); it sinks if its average density is greater. A fully submerged floating object settles at the depth where the buoyant force exactly balances its weight.
Why a steel ship floats despite steel being denser than water Density that matters for floating is the object's average density, not the density of the material it's made from. A ship's hull encloses a large volume of air, so its total mass spread over its total volume (hull plus enclosed air) gives an average density less than water's — even though the steel itself is roughly 8 times denser than water. Punch a hole and let water in, replacing that air volume, and the average density rises above water's, which is exactly why a flooded ship sinks.
Example: buoyant force on a submerged block A block with volume 0.02 m³ is fully submerged in water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³). Fb = (1000)(0.02)(9.8) = 196 N — regardless of what the block itself is made of or how heavy it is; only the displaced water's weight matters for the buoyant force.
Example: determining whether an object floats A block of mass 5 kg and volume 0.006 m³ is placed in water. Its average density: ρ = 5/0.006 ≈ 833 kg/m³, less than water's 1000 kg/m³, so it floats — settling at whatever submerged depth displaces exactly 5 kg of water (0.005 m³), leaving the remaining 0.001 m³ above the surface.

1. Archimedes' principle states that the buoyant force on a submerged object equals:

Buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced fluid, F_b = ρ_fluid·V·g, regardless of the submerged object's own material or weight.

2. Why does a steel ship float even though steel itself is much denser than water?

What matters for floating is the object's average density including any enclosed air, not the density of the material alone.

3. A block fully submerged in water has volume 0.01 m³. What is the buoyant force on it?

F_b = ρVg = (1000)(0.01)(9.8) = 98 N.

4. Fill in the blank: the upward force on a submerged object, equal to the weight of fluid it displaces, is called the ___.

The buoyant force arises from the pressure difference between the top and bottom of a submerged object.
