Pascal's Principle

A small force on a small piston can lift a car — the physics behind every hydraulic lift.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Pascal's Principle A pressure change applied anywhere to an enclosed, incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every other point in the fluid and to the walls of its container. This is what makes hydraulic systems work: P1 = P2, so F1/A1 = F2/A2.
Why a small input force can produce a large output force Since pressure (not force) is what transmits equally throughout the fluid, a small force F1 over a small area A1 produces the same pressure as a much larger force F2 over a proportionally larger area A2: F2 = F1(A2/A1). This isn't free energy — the small piston must move a proportionally larger distance than the large piston moves, so the work done (force × distance) balances out, exactly as with a lever (Unit 5) or any other mechanical-advantage device.
Example: a car lift A hydraulic lift has a small piston of area 0.002 m² and a large piston of area 0.5 m². A mechanic applies 200 N to the small piston. Since pressure is equal throughout: F2 = F1(A2/A1) = 200(0.5/0.002) = 200(250) = 50,000 N — enough to lift a car weighing roughly 5,100 kg.
Why the small piston must move much farther Since the fluid is incompressible, the volume of fluid displaced by the small piston must equal the volume that appears under the large piston: A1d1 = A2d2. With A2/A1 = 250 in the example above, the small piston must move 250 times farther than the large piston moves — the mechanic pumps the handle many times to raise the car just a little, trading distance for force exactly as energy conservation requires.

1. Pascal's principle states that a pressure change applied to an enclosed fluid is:

Pascal's principle is specifically that the pressure change is transmitted undiminished throughout an enclosed, incompressible fluid.

2. A hydraulic lift has a small piston of area 0.01 m² and large piston of area 1 m². A 100 N force on the small piston produces what force on the large piston?

F₂ = F₁(A₂/A₁) = 100(1/0.01) = 100(100) = 10,000 N.

3. Why isn't a hydraulic lift a violation of energy conservation, despite multiplying force so dramatically?

Incompressibility (A₁d₁ = A₂d₂) forces the small piston to move much farther than the large piston, keeping work (force × distance) balanced on both sides.

4. Fill in the blank: the rule that a pressure change transmits undiminished throughout an enclosed fluid is called ___.

Pascal's principle is the basis for all hydraulic lift and brake systems.
