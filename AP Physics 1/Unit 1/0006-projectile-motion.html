Projectile Motion

Lesson 4's free fall in the y-direction, Lesson 5's constant velocity in the x-direction, running side by side and never interacting.

← Lesson 5 Unit 2, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

A projectile is any object launched into the air and then left to move under gravity alone (no thrust, negligible air resistance). Lesson 5's big idea — that x-motion and y-motion are independent — makes projectile motion nothing more than two lessons you already know, applied at the same time:

The two independent stories Horizontal (x): zero acceleration, so velocity is constant: x = v0xt. Vertical (y): constant acceleration −g, exactly Lesson 4's free fall: y = v0yt − ½gt². The only thing linking the two equations is that they share the same clock, t.

If the projectile launches at speed v0 and angle θ above the horizontal, Lesson 5's component formulas split the initial velocity: v0x = v0 cos θ, v0y = v0 sin θ.

Three quantities worth deriving in advance

For a projectile launched and landing at the same height, three results come up constantly enough to be worth having ready:

time of flight: T = 2v0sin θ / g
max height: H = (v0sin θ)² / 2g
range: R = v0²sin(2θ) / g
Where these come from T comes from Lesson 4's symmetry: time up equals time down, and time up is when vy = 0. H is Lesson 4's v² = v0² + 2aΔx applied to just the rise. R is simply v0x × T — constant horizontal velocity times the total flight time. None of these are new physics; they're Lessons 4 and 5 substituted into each other.

Notice as you drag: 45° maximizes range for a given launch speed (sin 2θ peaks at θ = 45°, where 2θ = 90°). Angles that add to 90° — like 30° and 60° — give the same range but different flight times and max heights, since sin(2×30°) = sin(60°) = sin(120°) = sin(2×60°).

Example A ball is launched at 20 m/s at 30° above horizontal. v0x = 20 cos 30° = 17.3 m/s, v0y = 20 sin 30° = 10 m/s. Time of flight: T = 2(10)/9.8 = 2.04 s. Range: R = v0x × T = 17.3 × 2.04 = 35.3 m. Max height: H = 10²/(2×9.8) = 5.1 m.

What changes when launch and landing heights differ

The three boxed formulas above assume launching and landing at the same height. If a ball is thrown horizontally off a cliff, or a projectile lands lower (or higher) than it launched, those shortcut formulas no longer apply directly — instead, fall back to the two raw equations (x = v0xt and y = v0yt − ½gt²) and solve for t using the actual vertical displacement, then substitute into the x-equation. The shortcuts are conveniences for a common special case, not the fundamental physics.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. During a projectile's flight (ignoring air resistance), the horizontal component of its acceleration is:

Gravity acts only vertically, so the horizontal component of acceleration is zero for the entire flight — horizontal velocity stays constant.

2. For a projectile launching and landing at the same height, which launch angle gives the maximum range?

Range is proportional to sin(2θ), which is maximized when 2θ = 90°, i.e. θ = 45°.

3. A projectile launches from a cliff edge and lands on lower ground than where it started. To find its total time of flight, you should:

The shortcut time-of-flight formula assumes equal launch and landing heights. When they differ, go back to the raw vertical displacement equation and solve for t directly.

4. Fill in the blank: projectile motion is solvable because the horizontal and vertical motions are ___ of one another.

This is Lesson 5's independence principle, applied: solve the x-story and y-story separately, joined only by their shared value of t.
