Vectors and Two-Dimensional Motion

Break any 2D motion into an x-story and a y-story, told completely independently of each other.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Everything so far has lived on one number line. Real motion — a thrown ball, a car turning a corner — happens in a plane. The tool for handling this cleanly is the vector: a quantity with both magnitude and direction, as opposed to a scalar, which has magnitude only.

Vectors vs. scalars Position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration are all vectors — direction is part of their meaning. Distance, speed, time, and mass are scalars — a number and a unit is the whole story. Writing an arrow over a symbol (or bolding it) is how physics marks "this one has a direction."

Components: turning one vector into two numbers

Any vector in a plane can be split into an x-component and a y-component — its "shadow" along each axis. For a vector of magnitude A at angle θ above the positive x-axis:

Ax = A cos θ  Ay = A sin θ

Going the other direction — from components back to magnitude and angle — uses the Pythagorean theorem and inverse tangent:

A = √(Ax² + Ay²)  θ = tan⁻¹(Ay / Ax)
Why bother splitting a vector up Once a vector is split into x- and y-components, each component can be treated with the same one-dimensional tools from Lessons 1–4, completely separately. This is the single biggest simplification in 2D kinematics: motion in a plane is just two independent 1D motions happening at the same time, one along each axis.

Adding vectors

Vectors add component-by-component: to add vector A and vector B, add their x-components to get the resultant's x-component, and add their y-components to get the resultant's y-component. This works regardless of the individual vectors' directions — it's the reason components are worth computing in the first place.

Example A hiker walks a displacement of 30 m at 0° (due east), then 40 m at 90° (due north). Components: first leg is (30, 0), second leg is (0, 40). Resultant displacement: (30 + 0, 0 + 40) = (30, 40). Magnitude: √(30² + 40²) = 50 m. Direction: tan⁻¹(40/30) = 53.1° north of east.

Independence of x- and y-motion

The deepest idea in this lesson, and the one Lesson 6 depends on entirely: for an object moving in two dimensions under a force like gravity that acts purely in one direction (here, y), the x-motion and y-motion are completely independent. Whatever is happening horizontally has zero effect on what's happening vertically, and vice versa. A dropped ball and a ball thrown horizontally from the same height hit the ground at the exact same time — their y-motions are identical free fall, untouched by the fact that one of them is also moving sideways.

Speed is a magnitude only, with no direction attached — the defining feature of a scalar. The other three all carry direction.

2. A ball is dropped from a windowsill at the same instant an identical ball is thrown horizontally from the same height. Ignoring air resistance, which hits the ground first?

Vertical and horizontal motion are independent — both balls have identical vertical free fall (initial vertical velocity = 0 for both), so both take the same time to reach the ground regardless of horizontal speed.
