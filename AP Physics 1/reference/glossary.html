Kinematics

Position, x

An object's location relative to a chosen origin, along a chosen axis. Displacement is the change in position, Δx.

Velocity, v = Δx/Δt

The rate of change of position. Average velocity uses total displacement over total time; instantaneous velocity is the value at one moment (the slope of the position-time graph at that instant).

Acceleration, a = Δv/Δt

The rate of change of velocity. A nonzero acceleration means velocity is changing — in magnitude, direction, or both.

Kinematic equations

The four constant-acceleration equations: v = v₀ + at; x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²; v² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀); x = x₀ + ½(v₀ + v)t.

Free fall

Motion under gravity alone, with constant downward acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m/s², independent of an object's mass.

Projectile motion

Two-dimensional motion where horizontal velocity stays constant and vertical motion is free fall — the two directions are independent and can be analyzed separately.

Force and Translational Dynamics

Newton's first law

An object's velocity stays constant unless a net external force acts on it. Also called the law of inertia.

Newton's second law, F_net = ma

The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Acceleration points in the same direction as the net force.

Newton's third law

Forces come in pairs: if object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts an equal and opposite force on A. These two forces act on different objects and never cancel each other.

Weight, F_g = mg

The gravitational force on an object near Earth's surface, distinct from mass — weight depends on local gravitational acceleration, mass does not.

Normal force, F_N

The contact force a surface exerts perpendicular to itself, preventing objects from passing through it. Adjusts to whatever value is needed to prevent penetration.

Friction

A force resisting relative sliding between surfaces in contact. Static friction (f_s ≤ μ_sF_N) prevents motion from starting; kinetic friction (f_k = μ_kF_N) opposes motion already in progress.

Tension

The pulling force transmitted through a string, rope, or cable, directed along its length.

Free-body diagram

A diagram showing every force acting on a single object as an arrow, with no forces the object exerts on other things.

Work, Energy, and Power

Work, W = Fd cos θ

The energy transferred by a force acting through a displacement, where θ is the angle between the force and displacement directions. A force perpendicular to motion does zero work.

Kinetic energy, KE = ½mv²

Energy of motion. The work-energy theorem states the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy.

Gravitational potential energy, PE_grav = mgh

Stored energy due to an object's height in a gravitational field, measured relative to a chosen reference height.

Elastic potential energy, PE_elastic = ½kx²

Stored energy in a stretched or compressed spring, where k is the spring constant and x is displacement from natural length.

Conservation of mechanical energy

ME = KE + PE stays constant when only conservative forces (gravity, springs) do work — no friction or other non-conservative forces.

Power, P = W/t

The rate at which work is done or energy is transferred, measured in watts (1 W = 1 J/s).

Linear Momentum

Momentum, p = mv

A vector quantity combining mass and velocity, representing an object's "quantity of motion."

Impulse, J = FΔt = Δp

The change in momentum produced by a force acting over a time interval. The impulse-momentum theorem equates the two.

Conservation of momentum

Total momentum of an isolated system (zero net external force) stays constant, even as momentum is exchanged between objects within it.

Elastic collision

A collision in which kinetic energy is conserved along with momentum. Objects bounce apart without permanent deformation or heat generation.

Inelastic collision

A collision in which momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not. Perfectly inelastic collisions end with the objects moving together as one.

Center of mass

The mass-weighted average position of a system. It moves as if the system's total mass and total external force were concentrated there, unaffected by internal forces.

Torque and Rotational Dynamics

Torque, τ = rF sin θ

The rotational analog of force — a measure of how effectively a force produces rotation about a pivot, depending on the force's magnitude, its distance from the pivot, and the angle between them.

Moment of inertia, I

The rotational analog of mass — a measure of resistance to changes in rotational motion, depending on both mass and how that mass is distributed relative to the rotation axis.

Rotational form of Newton's second law, τ_net = Iα

Net torque equals moment of inertia times angular acceleration, directly paralleling F_net = ma.

Rotational equilibrium

A state where net torque is zero, so angular acceleration is zero — the object's rotation rate (including zero) stays constant.

Rolling without slipping

Rolling motion where the contact point is momentarily at rest, linking translational and rotational motion via v = rω.

Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems

Rotational kinetic energy, KE_rot = ½Iω²

Kinetic energy due to rotation, the rotational analog of ½mv². A rolling object's total kinetic energy is the sum of its translational and rotational kinetic energies.

Angular momentum, L = Iω

The rotational analog of linear momentum, representing a rotating system's "quantity of rotational motion."

Conservation of angular momentum

Total angular momentum of a system stays constant when net external torque is zero, even as the system's moment of inertia and angular velocity individually change.

Angular impulse-momentum theorem, τΔt = ΔL

The rotational analog of the impulse-momentum theorem — net torque acting over a time interval produces a change in angular momentum.

Oscillations

Simple harmonic motion (SHM)

Oscillatory motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to displacement from equilibrium and points back toward it: F = −kx.

Amplitude, A

The maximum displacement from equilibrium during an oscillation.

Period and frequency, T = 1/f

Period is the time for one complete oscillation; frequency is the number of oscillations per unit time. They are reciprocals of one another.

Spring-mass period, T = 2π√(m/k)

The period of a mass oscillating on a spring, independent of amplitude — depending only on mass and spring constant.

Pendulum period, T = 2π√(L/g)

The period of a simple pendulum for small angles, independent of both amplitude and the pendulum bob's mass — depending only on length and gravitational acceleration.

Energy in SHM, E = ½kA²

Total mechanical energy of an oscillator, constant throughout the motion as it trades between kinetic and elastic potential energy.

Damping

The gradual loss of oscillation energy to friction or resistance, causing amplitude to decrease over time.

Resonance

The dramatic buildup of oscillation amplitude when a system is driven at or near its natural frequency.

Fluids

Density, ρ = m/V

Mass per unit volume, an intrinsic property of a material independent of how much of it is present.

Pressure, P = F/A

Force per unit area, acting equally in every direction at a given point within a fluid at rest.

Pressure at depth, P = P₀ + ρgh

Pressure increases linearly with depth in a fluid, since deeper fluid must support the weight of everything above it.

Pascal's principle

A pressure change applied anywhere to an enclosed, incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every other point in the fluid — the basis for hydraulic systems.

Buoyant force, F_b = ρ_fluid Vg (Archimedes' principle)

The upward force on an object submerged in a fluid, equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces.

Continuity equation, A₁v₁ = A₂v₂

For an incompressible fluid in a pipe, the volume flow rate (area times speed) stays constant — the fluid speeds up where the pipe narrows.

Bernoulli's equation, P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant

Energy conservation applied per unit volume of a flowing fluid along a streamline — pressure, kinetic energy, and gravitational potential energy trade off against each other.
