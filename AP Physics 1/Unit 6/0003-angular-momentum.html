Angular Momentum

Momentum's rotational twin — a quantity that torque changes, exactly as force changes linear momentum.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Angular Momentum Angular momentum L = Iω — the rotational equivalent of linear momentum p = mv (Unit 4, Lesson 1), with mass replaced by moment of inertia and linear velocity replaced by angular velocity. Its units are kg·m²/s.
Angular Impulse-Momentum Theorem Exactly as impulse changes linear momentum (Unit 4, Lesson 1), angular impulse changes angular momentum: τnetΔt = ΔL. This follows the same derivation pattern as before — starting from τnet = Iα and α = Δω/Δt, multiplying both sides by Δt gives τnetΔt = IΔω = ΔL.
Example: angular momentum of a spinning disk A solid disk (I = 0.18 kg·m², from earlier examples) spins at ω = 15 rad/s. L = Iω = (0.18)(15) = 2.7 kg·m²/s.
Example: angular momentum of a point mass (orbital case) For a point mass in circular motion at radius r with speed v, L = Iω = (mr²)(v/r) = mrv — angular momentum can also be computed directly this way for any object treated as a point mass a distance r from the axis, without separately finding I and ω.
Why L = mrv works for orbits without needing "spin" A planet in orbit isn't spinning about the axis it orbits — it's simply moving in a circle around a distant point. Treating it as a point mass at radius r, its moment of inertia about that axis is I = mr² (Unit 5, Lesson 3), and its angular velocity is ω = v/r (from v = rω, Unit 5, Lesson 1) — combining gives L = mr²(v/r) = mrv directly, which is why this formula applies just as well to orbital motion as to a physically spinning disk.

1. Angular momentum L = Iω is the direct rotational analog of which linear quantity?

Moment of inertia replaces mass and angular velocity replaces linear velocity, making L = Iω the rotational analog of p = mv.

2. A disk with I = 0.4 kg·m² spins at ω = 5 rad/s. What is its angular momentum?

L = Iω = (0.4)(5) = 2 kg·m²/s.

3. Why does L = mrv apply to a planet orbiting a star, even though the planet itself isn't physically spinning?

L = Iω with I = mr² (point mass) and ω = v/r combine algebraically to give L = mrv exactly, regardless of whether the object is "spinning" in the everyday sense.

4. Fill in the blank: the quantity L = Iω, changed by torque acting over time, is called ___.

Angular momentum is the rotational analog of linear momentum, related to torque via the angular impulse-momentum theorem.
