Rotational Kinetic Energy

A spinning object has kinetic energy even if its center of mass isn't going anywhere at all.

← Unit 5, Lesson 6 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Rotational Kinetic Energy KErot = ½Iω² — the rotational twin of KEtrans = ½mv² (Unit 3, Lesson 2), with mass replaced by moment of inertia and linear speed replaced by angular velocity. A spinning top has rotational kinetic energy even while sitting in one place, with zero translational KE.
Where this comes from Treat a rotating rigid object as a collection of point masses, each moving at its own tangential speed vi = riω (Unit 5, Lesson 1). Each contributes ½mivi² = ½miri²ω² to the total kinetic energy. Summing over every point mass: KErot = Σ½miri²ω² = ½(Σmiri²)ω² = ½Iω², since Σmiri² is exactly the definition of moment of inertia (Unit 5, Lesson 3).
Total Kinetic Energy of a Rolling Object An object that both translates and rotates (like a rolling ball) has KEtotal = ½Mvcm² + ½Icmω² — translational KE of the center of mass plus rotational KE about the center of mass, added together. Neither term alone captures the full kinetic energy of rolling motion.
Example: rotational KE of a spinning disk A solid disk (I = 0.18 kg·m², from Unit 5 Lesson 3's example) spins at ω = 10 rad/s. KErot = ½Iω² = ½(0.18)(100) = 9 J.
Example: total KE of a rolling ball A solid sphere (I = ⅖MR², M = 2 kg, R = 0.1 m) rolls without slipping at vcm = 4 m/s. Using the rolling constraint ω = vcm/R = 40 rad/s: I = ⅖(2)(0.01) = 0.008 kg·m². KEtrans = ½(2)(4²) = 16 J. KErot = ½(0.008)(40²) = 6.4 J. KEtotal = 16 + 6.4 = 22.4 J — noticeably more than the 16 J a sliding (non-rotating) object at the same speed would have.
Why a rolling object has more kinetic energy than a sliding one at the same speed A sliding block at speed v has only KEtrans = ½mv². A rolling object at the same center-of-mass speed has that same translational term plus rotational KE on top of it, since it's spinning as well as translating — this "extra" energy is exactly why it takes more work to bring a rolling object up to a given speed than a sliding one, a fact Lesson 2 puts to direct use.

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A top is spinning in place, with its center of mass stationary. Does it have kinetic energy?

Rotational kinetic energy ½Iω² doesn't require any translational motion at all — a spinning top in place has KE_rot but zero KE_trans.

2. An object with I = 0.5 kg·m² spins at ω = 6 rad/s. What is its rotational kinetic energy?

KE_rot = ½Iω² = ½(0.5)(36) = 9 J.

3. Why does a rolling ball have more total kinetic energy than a sliding (non-rotating) block moving at the same center-of-mass speed?

KE_total = ½Mv_cm² + ½Iω² for a rolling object — the rotational term is extra energy a non-rotating sliding block simply doesn't have.

4. Fill in the blank: the quantity ½Iω² is called ___, the rotational analog of ½mv².

Rotational kinetic energy KE_rot = ½Iω² is derived by summing ½m_ir_i²ω² over every point mass in a rotating rigid body.
