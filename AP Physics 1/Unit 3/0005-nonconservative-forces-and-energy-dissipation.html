Nonconservative Forces and Energy Dissipation

Mechanical energy isn't destroyed when friction acts — it's converted into a form this course doesn't track: heat.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Nonconservative Forces A nonconservative force is one where the work done depends on the path taken, not just the start and end points. Kinetic friction is the primary example in this course: sliding a block twice as far across the same rough floor does twice the negative work, even between the same two endpoints, because the path length itself matters — unlike gravity or the spring force (Lesson 3).
Why energy isn't destroyed, only converted When friction acts, mechanical energy (KE + PE) decreases — but total energy, including heat, is still conserved (this is the broader law of conservation of energy, which mechanical energy conservation is a special case of). The "missing" mechanical energy is converted into thermal energy: the block and the floor both get very slightly warmer. AP Physics 1 doesn't quantify that heat directly, but it always accounts for it as the destination of the "lost" mechanical energy.
MEi = MEf + |Wfriction|

With friction present, the energy-conservation equation from Lesson 4 gets one extra term: the initial mechanical energy equals the final mechanical energy plus the energy dissipated by friction (equal to the magnitude of the negative work friction does, Wfriction = −fkd, from Unit 2 Lesson 5's fk = μkN).

Example: a block sliding to a stop A 4 kg block slides onto a rough patch at 6 m/s and slides 3 m before stopping, brought to rest entirely by friction. Initial KE: ½(4)(6²) = 72 J. Final KE: 0 J (it stops). All 72 J of mechanical energy was dissipated by friction, so the friction force must have been fk = 72 J / 3 m = 24 N — found from energy alone, without needing μk or the normal force directly.
Example: a ramp with friction A 2 kg block starts from rest at the top of a 4 m high ramp and reaches the bottom at 6 m/s (instead of the frictionless √(2gh) ≈ 8.85 m/s from Lesson 4). Energy dissipated: MEi − MEf = mgh − ½mv² = (2)(9.8)(4) − ½(2)(6²) = 78.4 − 36 = 42.4 J lost to friction along the ramp.
Why this doesn't contradict Lesson 4 Lesson 4's MEi = MEf assumed conservative forces were the only ones doing work. The moment friction enters, it's simply no longer the only force doing work — the same underlying work-energy theorem (Lesson 2) still holds exactly; it just now includes friction's negative work term alongside gravity's or the spring's.

1. What fundamentally distinguishes a nonconservative force like friction from a conservative force like gravity?

Path-dependence is the defining feature: sliding twice as far across rough ground does twice the negative work between the same two points, unlike a conservative force's path-independent work.

2. When friction dissipates mechanical energy, where does that energy actually go?

Total energy is always conserved; friction simply converts mechanical energy into thermal energy, which this course tracks as "energy dissipated" without computing the resulting temperature change directly.

3. A 1 kg block slides across a rough floor at an initial speed of 4 m/s and comes to rest after sliding 2 m. How much energy did friction dissipate, and what was the friction force?

Initial KE = ½(1)(4²) = 8 J, final KE = 0, so 8 J was dissipated. Friction force: 8 J / 2 m = 4 N.

4. Fill in the blank: a force whose work depends on the path taken, like friction, is called a ___ force.

Nonconservative forces dissipate mechanical energy in a path-dependent way, in contrast to conservative forces.
