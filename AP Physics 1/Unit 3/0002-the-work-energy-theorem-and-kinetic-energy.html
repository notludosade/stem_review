The Work-Energy Theorem and Kinetic Energy

A second route to every problem Newton's second law solves — sometimes far shorter, especially when acceleration isn't constant.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because of its motion:

KE = ½mv²

Kinetic energy is always non-negative (mass and v² are both non-negative), and, like work, is measured in joules. It depends on speed squared — doubling an object's speed quadruples its kinetic energy, not merely doubles it.

The Work-Energy Theorem The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: Wnet = ΔKE = KEf − KEi. This holds regardless of whether the net force is constant, and regardless of the path taken — it only depends on the object's initial and final speeds.
Where this theorem comes from It follows directly from Lesson 3's kinematic equation v² = v0² + 2aΔx combined with Newton's second law. Multiply both sides of v² = v0² + 2aΔx by ½m: ½mv² = ½mv0² + m(aΔx). Since Fnet = ma (Unit 2, Lesson 2), m(aΔx) = FnetΔx = Wnet (Lesson 1, for force and displacement along the same line). Rearranging gives Wnet = ½mv² − ½mv0² = ΔKE — the work-energy theorem is Newton's second law and Unit 1's kinematics, combined and relabeled.
Example: a straightforward push A 3 kg cart starts at rest and a net force of 6 N acts on it over a 4 m displacement. Net work: Wnet = 6(4) = 24 J. By the theorem, ΔKE = 24 J, so KEf = 0 + 24 = 24 J. Solving ½(3)v² = 24 gives v = 4 m/s — found without ever computing acceleration or time.
Example: why this theorem shines with variable force A spring exerts a force that changes as it compresses (Lesson 3), making acceleration non-constant and Unit 1's kinematic equations inapplicable directly. But the work-energy theorem doesn't care whether force was constant along the way — only the net work actually done, and the resulting KE change. This is precisely why work and energy methods become indispensable once forces stop being constant.

When to reach for energy methods instead of Newton's laws directly

Newton's second law (Unit 2) and the work-energy theorem are not competing theories — they're the same physics, viewed two ways. Newton's laws are the better tool when you need details during the motion (acceleration at a specific instant, time elapsed). The work-energy theorem is the better tool when you only care about a before-and-after comparison of speeds, especially when force or acceleration changes in a complicated way along the path.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. If an object's speed doubles, its kinetic energy:

KE = ½mv² depends on v squared, so doubling v multiplies KE by 2² = 4.

2. A 2 kg object starts at 3 m/s. A net force does 16 J of work on it. What is its final speed?

KE_i = ½(2)(3²) = 9 J. KE_f = 9 + 16 = 25 J. Solving ½(2)v² = 25 gives v² = 25, so v = 5 m/s.

3. The work-energy theorem is especially useful compared to Newton's second law directly when:

The theorem doesn't require constant force or acceleration — it directly relates net work to the overall change in kinetic energy, which is exactly what's needed when force varies but only initial/final speeds are of interest.

4. Fill in the blank: the work-energy theorem states that net work equals the change in ___ energy.

W_net = ΔKE — net work causes a change in kinetic energy, the energy of motion.
