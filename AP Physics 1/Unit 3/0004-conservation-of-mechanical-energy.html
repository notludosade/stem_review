Conservation of Mechanical Energy

When only conservative forces act, the total of kinetic and potential energy never changes — one of the most powerful shortcuts in all of physics.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Mechanical Energy The mechanical energy of a system is the sum of its kinetic and potential energy: ME = KE + PE. When the only forces doing work are conservative (gravity, springs — Lesson 3), mechanical energy is conserved: MEi = MEf, or equivalently, KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf.
Why this follows from the work-energy theorem Lesson 2 established Wnet = ΔKE. For a conservative force, the work it does can always be written as the negative of a potential energy change: Wconservative = −ΔPE (this is essentially how PE is defined in the first place). If conservative forces are the only ones acting, Wnet = −ΔPE, so −ΔPE = ΔKE, which rearranges to ΔKE + ΔPE = 0 — the total ME never changes. Conservation of energy isn't a separate law bolted onto Newton's mechanics; it's the work-energy theorem specialized to conservative forces.

This gives a second problem-solving route, often far faster than tracking forces and accelerations step by step: identify the energy at a starting point, identify the energy at an ending point, and set them equal — skipping over whatever happens in between.

Example: a ball sliding down a frictionless ramp A 2 kg ball starts from rest at height 5 m on a frictionless ramp. At the bottom (h = 0): MEi = MEf ⟹ mgh + 0 = 0 + ½mv². The mass cancels: gh = ½v², so v = √(2gh) = √(2·9.8·5) ≈ 9.9 m/s — found instantly, without ever needing the ramp's shape or angle.
Why the ramp's shape never mattered Because gravity is conservative, the work it does — and hence the KE gained — depends only on the change in height, never on the path taken to get there (a straight ramp, a curved slide, a loop) as long as friction is absent. This is precisely the path-independence that defines a conservative force in Lesson 3.
Example: a mass on a spring A 0.5 kg mass is pushed against a spring (k = 200 N/m), compressing it 0.3 m, then released on a frictionless surface. All the elastic PE converts to KE: ½kx² = ½mv² ⟹ ½(200)(0.3)² = ½(0.5)v² ⟹ 9 = 0.25v² ⟹ v² = 36 ⟹ v = 6 m/s.

1. A ball slides down a frictionless curved ramp from height h. Its speed at the bottom depends on:

Since gravity is conservative and no friction acts, mechanical energy conservation gives v = √(2gh) — mass cancels and the path taken is irrelevant, only the height drop matters.

2. Conservation of mechanical energy (ME_i = ME_f) is valid whenever:

Mechanical energy is conserved specifically when only conservative forces do work — the moment a nonconservative force like friction acts, some mechanical energy is dissipated (Lesson 5).

3. A 1 kg mass is dropped from rest at height 3.2 m onto a frictionless setup. Using g = 10 m/s² for clean numbers, its speed just before impact is:

mgh = ½mv² ⟹ v = √(2gh) = √(2·10·3.2) = √64 = 8 m/s.

4. Fill in the blank: the sum of kinetic and potential energy is called ___ energy, and is conserved when only conservative forces act.

Mechanical energy ME = KE + PE is the quantity conserved in the absence of nonconservative forces like friction.
