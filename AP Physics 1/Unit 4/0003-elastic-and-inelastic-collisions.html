Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

Momentum is conserved in every collision. Kinetic energy is only conserved in the special ones.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Elastic vs. Inelastic Collisions A collision is perfectly elastic if total kinetic energy is conserved (KEi = KEf) in addition to momentum. A collision is inelastic if kinetic energy is not conserved — some is converted to heat, sound, or deformation. A perfectly inelastic collision is the extreme case where the colliding objects stick together and move with a common final velocity.
Why momentum is always conserved but kinetic energy isn't Momentum conservation (Lesson 2) follows from Newton's third law alone, which holds in every collision without exception. Kinetic energy conservation is a much stronger, additional condition — it requires that no mechanical energy be dissipated during the collision (Unit 3, Lesson 5), which is only true in idealized cases like billiard balls or certain molecular collisions. Real macroscopic collisions (cars, clay, most everyday impacts) are inelastic to some degree, since some KE always converts to heat, sound, and deformation.
Perfectly inelastic: m1v1i + m2v2i = (m1 + m2)vf

Because the objects stick together and move as one after a perfectly inelastic collision, there's only one unknown final velocity, making this the simplest collision type to solve, using momentum conservation alone.

Example: perfectly inelastic collision A 1000 kg car moving at 15 m/s rear-ends a stationary 1500 kg car, and the two lock together. Momentum conservation: (1000)(15) + (1500)(0) = (1000+1500)vf ⟹ 15000 = 2500vf ⟹ vf = 6 m/s. Checking kinetic energy: KEi = ½(1000)(15²) = 112,500 J; KEf = ½(2500)(6²) = 45,000 J — 67,500 J was dissipated as heat, sound, and deformation, confirming the collision is inelastic.
Example: verifying a collision is elastic A 2 kg ball moving at 6 m/s collides with a stationary 2 kg ball; afterward, the first ball is at rest and the second moves at 6 m/s. Momentum check: (2)(6)+0 = 0+(2)(6) ✓. KE check: KEi = ½(2)(6²) = 36 J; KEf = ½(2)(6²) = 36 J — kinetic energy is exactly conserved, so this collision (equal masses, a full "velocity swap") is perfectly elastic.
Why you always check both quantities separately Momentum conservation is a hard requirement for every collision and gives you one equation. Whether kinetic energy is also conserved is a separate question you must check (or be told) — never assume it, and never use conservation of kinetic energy as a second equation unless the problem explicitly establishes the collision is elastic.

1. Which statement correctly distinguishes elastic from inelastic collisions?

Momentum conservation follows from Newton's third law and holds in every collision; kinetic energy conservation is the stronger, additional condition that defines an elastic collision.

2. A 2 kg cart at 8 m/s collides and sticks to a stationary 6 kg cart. What is their common final velocity?

(2)(8) + (6)(0) = (2+6)v_f ⟹ 16 = 8v_f ⟹ v_f = 2 m/s.

3. In the collision from question 2, how much kinetic energy was dissipated?

KE_i = ½(2)(8²) = 64 J. KE_f = ½(8)(2²) = 16 J. Dissipated: 64 − 16 = 48 J.

4. Fill in the blank: a collision where the objects stick together and move with one common final velocity is called a ___ collision.

A perfectly inelastic collision is the extreme case of maximum kinetic energy loss consistent with momentum conservation, where the objects move together afterward.
