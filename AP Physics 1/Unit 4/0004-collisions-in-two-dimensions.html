Collisions in Two Dimensions

Momentum conservation still holds — it just has to hold separately in each direction.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Momentum Conservation by Component Momentum is a vector, so conservation of momentum applies independently to each component: px,i = px,f and py,i = py,f separately. This is exactly the same independence-of-components idea from Unit 1, Lesson 5's projectile motion — x and y are handled as two separate one-dimensional problems, linked only by time (or here, by the single collision event).
Why splitting into components works Newton's third law guarantees the collision forces between two objects are equal and opposite along whatever line they act — and that line's x- and y-components are each individually equal and opposite too. Summing impulses component-by-component gives Δpx = 0 and Δpy = 0 independently, so total momentum is conserved along each axis on its own, not just as a combined vector.
Example: an off-center collision A 3 kg puck moving at 4 m/s in the +x direction strikes a stationary 3 kg puck. After the collision, the first puck moves off at 2 m/s at 30° above the x-axis. Find the second puck's velocity, assuming it moves entirely below the x-axis (symmetric glancing collision).

x: (3)(4) + 0 = (3)(2 cos 30°) + (3)v2x ⟹ 12 = 5.196 + 3v2x ⟹ v2x ≈ 2.27 m/s
y: 0 = (3)(2 sin 30°) + (3)v2y ⟹ 0 = 3 + 3v2y ⟹ v2y = −1 m/s
So the second puck moves with components (2.27, −1) m/s — found entirely from two independent one-dimensional conservation equations.
Why kinetic energy is still just one scalar equation Unlike momentum, kinetic energy has no x- or y-component — it's a scalar (Unit 3, Lesson 2), computed from speed² regardless of direction. If a two-dimensional collision is elastic, KE conservation gives exactly one additional equation (½m1v1i² + ½m2v2i² = ½m1v1f² + ½m2v2f²), on top of the two momentum-component equations — never a fourth equation broken into components of its own.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In a two-dimensional collision, momentum conservation is applied by:

Because momentum is a vector, its x- and y-components are each conserved independently, giving two separate one-dimensional equations.

2. In a two-dimensional elastic collision, how many independent equations does kinetic energy conservation contribute?

Kinetic energy has no components — it's a single scalar quantity, so it contributes exactly one equation regardless of how many spatial dimensions the collision spans.

3. A 2 kg ball moving at 5 m/s in the +x direction hits a stationary ball and deflects to move entirely in the +y direction at 3 m/s. If the y-momentum must be conserved, what does this imply about the second ball?

Since initial y-momentum was zero (motion was purely along x), the two final y-momentum components must sum to zero, requiring the second ball to have an equal-and-opposite y-component.

4. Fill in the blank: in a two-dimensional collision, momentum conservation is applied separately to each ___ of momentum.

Momentum's x- and y-components are each conserved independently, just as with vector addition in Unit 1.
