Linear Momentum and Impulse

A second Newton's-second-law-flavored quantity — but one built for problems where force changes suddenly, like a collision.

← Unit 3, Lesson 6 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Linear Momentum Momentum is defined as p = mv — the product of an object's mass and velocity. Like velocity, momentum is a vector, pointing in the same direction as the object's motion. Its SI units are kg·m/s.
Impulse Impulse is the product of a net force and the time interval over which it acts: J = FnetΔt. The impulse-momentum theorem states that impulse equals the change in momentum: J = Δp = mΔv.
Where the impulse-momentum theorem comes from Newton's second law, Fnet = ma, can be rewritten using a = Δv/Δt as Fnet = mΔv/Δt. Multiplying both sides by Δt gives FnetΔt = mΔv — impulse equals change in momentum. This is Newton's second law rearranged, exactly the same relationship as the work-energy theorem was for Unit 3: one law of motion, viewed through a different lens suited to a different kind of problem.
Why impulse-momentum instead of the work-energy theorem here Collisions involve very large forces acting over very short, often unmeasured time intervals (a bat hitting a ball, a car crumpling in a crash). The impulse-momentum theorem lets you relate the overall change in momentum to a force and a time interval without needing to know the exact, rapidly-changing force profile during the collision itself — precisely the situation where Unit 2's F = ma becomes impractical to apply directly.
Example: a bat hitting a ball A 0.15 kg baseball arrives at −20 m/s (toward the batter) and leaves at +30 m/s (away from the batter) after 0.001 s of contact. Δp = m(vf − vi) = 0.15(30 − (−20)) = 0.15(50) = 7.5 kg·m/s. Average force: F = Δp/Δt = 7.5/0.001 = 7500 N — a huge force, made plausible only by the extremely short contact time.
Why airbags and padding work For a fixed change in momentum Δp (say, a person's momentum going from a crash speed to zero), the impulse-momentum theorem shows F and Δt trade off: F = Δp/Δt. Stretching out the collision time (airbags, crumple zones, bent knees on landing) reduces the average force needed to produce the exact same Δp — the same physical insight explains catching a ball by "giving" with your hands.

1. Why do airbags reduce injury in a crash, given that the change in momentum of the passenger is the same with or without one?

Since F = Δp/Δt, and Δp is fixed by the crash's speed change, stretching Δt (a longer, softer collision) directly reduces the average force experienced.

2. A 0.2 kg ball moving at 15 m/s is brought to rest by a wall over 0.05 s. What average force did the wall exert?

Δp = m(v_f − v_i) = 0.2(0 − 15) = −3 kg·m/s. F = Δp/Δt = −3/0.05 = −60 N, magnitude 60 N.

3. The impulse-momentum theorem (J = Δp) is most useful compared to Newton's second law directly when:

Collisions involve rapidly-varying forces over tiny time intervals; the impulse-momentum theorem relates the overall momentum change to force and time without needing the detailed force-vs-time profile.

4. Fill in the blank: the product of net force and the time interval over which it acts is called ___, and it equals the change in momentum.

J = F_net·Δt = Δp is the impulse-momentum theorem.
