Damping and Resonance

Real oscillators lose energy to friction — and can gain it back dramatically if pushed at just the right rhythm.

← Lesson 4 Unit 8, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Damping Damping is the loss of mechanical energy in an oscillating system to nonconservative forces like friction or air resistance (Unit 3, Lesson 5), causing amplitude to decrease over time. Underdamped systems still oscillate while amplitude shrinks; overdamped systems return to equilibrium slowly without oscillating at all; critically damped systems return to equilibrium in the shortest time possible without oscillating.
Why every real oscillator eventually stops without a driving force Lesson 4 showed that undamped SHM conserves total mechanical energy forever, oscillating at fixed amplitude indefinitely — but that assumed no friction or air resistance, an idealization. Real systems always have some nonconservative force draining energy each cycle (Unit 3, Lesson 5), which is precisely why a swing eventually stops and a plucked guitar string eventually goes silent unless something keeps adding energy back in.
Driven Oscillations and Resonance A driven oscillator has an external periodic force pushing it, rather than swinging freely. Resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the system's own natural frequency (the frequency it would oscillate at on its own, set by T = 2π√(m/k) or T = 2π√(L/g)) — at resonance, amplitude builds dramatically, since each push arrives perfectly timed to add energy rather than fight the motion.
Why timing (not just force) determines resonance Pushing a swing works best with small pushes precisely timed to match its own natural back-and-forth rhythm — not with a single hard shove. Each well-timed push adds a little more energy exactly when the swing is already moving in that direction, so amplitude accumulates push after push. A driving frequency far from the natural frequency instead alternates between helping and opposing the motion within a single cycle, adding much less net energy overall — timing, not just force magnitude, is what makes resonance dramatic.
Example: pushing a playground swing A swing has a natural period of 2 s (set by its length, via the pendulum formula from Lesson 3). Pushing it once every 2 s, timed with its own rhythm, builds up amplitude efficiently over many pushes — this is resonance in everyday form.
Example: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge Wind gusts near the bridge's own natural oscillation frequency drove increasingly large swaying oscillations in the (famously under-damped) structure — a real-world case where resonance amplified a driving force most bridges resist without incident, ultimately leading to the bridge's collapse in 1940. It remains one of the most cited engineering cautions about designing structures with damping and natural frequencies in mind.

1. An oscillator that returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible without oscillating back and forth at all is:

Critical damping is defined as the fastest return to equilibrium without any oscillation, distinct from the slower overdamped case and the still-oscillating underdamped case.

2. Resonance produces dramatically large amplitude when:

Resonance is fundamentally about matching timing (driving frequency to natural frequency), not just applying more force.

3. Why do small, well-timed pushes on a swing build up much larger amplitude than a single hard shove of similar total force?

Resonance accumulates energy over many cycles precisely because each push is timed to match the natural oscillation, unlike one large but untimed shove.

4. Fill in the blank: dramatic amplitude buildup when a driving frequency matches a system's natural frequency is called ___.

Resonance occurs specifically when driving frequency matches natural frequency, as in the Tacoma Narrows Bridge example.
