The Pendulum

A swinging pendulum is SHM in disguise — but only if you don't swing it too far.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

The Simple Pendulum A simple pendulum — a point mass on a massless string of length L — has period T = 2π√(L/g), valid only for small angles (roughly θ < 15°). Notably, the period depends on length and gravitational acceleration alone — not on the mass of the bob, and not on amplitude (for small angles).
Why the small-angle restriction matters The restoring force along the pendulum's arc is −mg sin θ, not −mg θ — true SHM requires a force proportional to displacement, and displacement along the arc is proportional to θ, not sin θ. Only for small angles does sin θ ≈ θ (in radians), making the restoring force approximately proportional to displacement and the motion approximately SHM. Swing a pendulum through a large angle (say 60°) and its period noticeably lengthens compared to the small-angle formula's prediction — the approximation breaks down.
Why the bob's mass doesn't appear in T = 2π√(L/g) Both the restoring force (−mg sin θ) and the pendulum's inertia scale with mass m in exactly the same way, so m cancels out of the equation of motion entirely — the same reason all objects fall at the same rate under gravity alone (Unit 1). A heavier bob feels a proportionally stronger restoring force but also has proportionally more inertia resisting acceleration, and the two effects cancel.
Example: finding a pendulum's period A pendulum of length 2 m swings through small angles near Earth's surface (g = 9.8 m/s²). T = 2π√(2/9.8) = 2π√0.204 ≈ 2π(0.452) ≈ 2.84 s — the same period whether the bob is 50 g or 5 kg.
Example: designing a pendulum clock A clock needs a pendulum with a 1-second half-period (2 s full period) to tick once per second. Solving T = 2π√(L/g) for L: L = g(T/2π)² = 9.8(2/2π)² = 9.8(0.318)² ≈ 0.994 m — almost exactly 1 meter, a design fact that motivated the original definition of the meter.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A simple pendulum's period T = 2π√(L/g) depends on:

Mass cancels out of the equation of motion, leaving only length and g in the small-angle period formula.

2. Why is the small-angle approximation (θ < 15°) necessary for the pendulum period formula to be accurate?

SHM requires force proportional to displacement; sin θ ≈ θ only holds for small angles, so the SHM approximation (and its period formula) breaks down at large angles.

3. A 2 m pendulum and a 2 m pendulum with twice the bob mass, both swinging at small angles on Earth, compare in period how?

Mass cancels out of T = 2π√(L/g) entirely, so bob mass has no effect on the period.

4. Fill in the blank: the pendulum period formula T = 2π√(L/g) is only accurate under the ___ approximation.

The small-angle approximation, sin θ ≈ θ, is what makes the pendulum's motion approximately SHM.
