The Spring-Mass System

A mass on a spring is the textbook example of SHM — and its period reveals a surprising independence from amplitude.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Period of a Spring-Mass System A mass m attached to a spring of stiffness k oscillates with period T = 2π√(m/k). Larger mass means more inertia to accelerate, lengthening the period; a stiffer spring (larger k) means a stronger restoring force pulling the mass back faster, shortening the period.
Where T = 2π√(m/k) comes from Starting from Newton's second law with the spring force: ma = −kx, so a = −(k/m)x. Comparing this to the defining property of SHM (acceleration proportional to negative displacement) shows ω² = k/m, so ω = √(k/m). Since T = 2π/ω (Lesson 1), substituting gives T = 2π/√(k/m) = 2π√(m/k).
Why the period doesn't depend on amplitude Notice that A never appears in T = 2π√(m/k) at all — a mass pulled back 5 cm and released takes exactly the same time per cycle as the same mass pulled back 20 cm. This seems surprising until you see why: a larger amplitude means the mass has farther to travel, but F = −kx also means a proportionally larger restoring force (and therefore proportionally larger speed) at every point along the way — the two effects exactly cancel, leaving the period unchanged. This independence is a defining signature of true SHM.
Example: finding the period A 2 kg mass is attached to a spring with k = 50 N/m. T = 2π√(2/50) = 2π√0.04 = 2π(0.2) ≈ 1.26 s — regardless of whether the mass is displaced 2 cm or 10 cm before release.
Example: finding k from a measured period A 0.5 kg mass on a spring completes 10 oscillations in 8 seconds, so T = 0.8 s. Solving T = 2π√(m/k) for k: k = m(2π/T)² = 0.5(2π/0.8)² = 0.5(7.85)² ≈ 30.8 N/m.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Doubling the mass on a spring (keeping k fixed) does what to the period?

T = 2π√(m/k) depends on the square root of mass, so doubling m multiplies T by √2, not by 2.

2. A 1 kg mass on a spring with k = 4 N/m has what period?

T = 2π√(1/4) = 2π(0.5) = π s.

3. Why does the period of a spring-mass system not depend on the amplitude of oscillation?

The proportionality in F = −kx means distance and speed scale together with amplitude, leaving the time per cycle unchanged.

4. Fill in the blank: T = 2π√(m/k) gives the ___ of a mass oscillating on a spring, independent of amplitude.

The period of a spring-mass system depends only on mass and spring stiffness, never on amplitude.
