Simple Harmonic Motion

A whole new kind of motion — not constant velocity, not constant acceleration, but a restoring force that always pulls back toward equilibrium.

← Unit 6, Lesson 5 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Restoring Force A restoring force always points back toward an equilibrium position, opposing displacement from it. Simple harmonic motion (SHM) is the specific case where the restoring force is directly proportional to displacement: F = −kx, with x measured from equilibrium and k a positive constant — this is exactly Hooke's law, first introduced for springs (Unit 3, Lesson 3), now recognized as the defining condition for an entire class of motion.
Why F = −kx always pushes back toward equilibrium The minus sign is the entire point: whenever x is positive (displaced right), F is negative (pointing left, back toward zero); whenever x is negative (displaced left), F is positive (pointing right, back toward zero). The force never points away from equilibrium — it always opposes displacement, which is exactly the condition that keeps an object oscillating back and forth rather than flying off in one direction.
Position, Period, and Frequency An object in SHM has position x(t) = A cos(ωt) (if released from maximum displacement), where A is the amplitude (maximum displacement from equilibrium) and ω is the angular frequency. The period T is the time for one full cycle, and frequency f = 1/T is cycles per second, related by ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
Why the same ω from rotational motion shows up here This ω is the same angular frequency used for circular motion (Unit 5, Lesson 1) — not a coincidence. SHM can be visualized as the shadow (projection) of a point moving in a circle at constant angular velocity ω, cast onto a single axis. This connection is why sinusoidal functions describe SHM position exactly, and why the same symbol and units (rad/s) carry over from rotational motion.
Example: reading amplitude and period from motion A mass oscillates between x = +0.2 m and x = −0.2 m, completing one full cycle every 0.5 s. Its amplitude is A = 0.2 m (the maximum displacement, not the total swing distance of 0.4 m). Its period is T = 0.5 s, so its frequency is f = 1/0.5 = 2 Hz, and its angular frequency is ω = 2π(2) = 4π ≈ 12.6 rad/s.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What condition defines simple harmonic motion?

SHM is specifically defined by a restoring force proportional to displacement, F = −kx, which produces sinusoidal position over time.

2. A mass oscillates between x = +0.3 m and x = −0.3 m. What is its amplitude?

Amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium in one direction, 0.3 m, not the total swing distance.

3. Why does the restoring force in SHM always point back toward equilibrium regardless of which side the object is displaced to?

The minus sign ensures F is always opposite in sign to x, so the force opposes displacement on either side of equilibrium.

4. Fill in the blank: the maximum displacement from equilibrium during simple harmonic motion is called the ___.

Amplitude A sets the maximum extent of the oscillation, appearing directly in x(t) = A cos(ωt).
