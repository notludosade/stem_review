Rotational Equilibrium

A sign hanging perfectly still needs more than balanced forces — it needs balanced torques too.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Static Equilibrium (Full Condition) An object is in complete static equilibrium only if both conditions hold: ΣF = 0 (no net force — Unit 2, Lesson 1's translational equilibrium) and Στ = 0 (no net torque about any chosen pivot). An object can satisfy one condition without the other — a spinning ice skater has ΣF = 0 (moving at constant velocity, or here, staying in place) but Στ ≠ 0 as they spin up; a see-saw can have zero net torque about its center while still accelerating downward if unsupported.
Why you can choose any pivot point If Στ = 0 about one point, it's automatically zero about every other point as well, for an object also satisfying ΣF = 0 — this is a consequence of the equilibrium condition, not an extra assumption. In practice, this means you're free to choose whichever pivot point makes the algebra simplest, typically one that eliminates an unknown force entirely by placing the pivot at that force's point of application (since any force acting exactly at the pivot has r = 0 and contributes zero torque).
Example: a seesaw A 4 m long seesaw pivots at its center. A 30 kg child sits 1.5 m from the pivot on the left. Where must a 45 kg child sit on the right to balance it? Setting net torque to zero about the pivot: (30)(9.8)(1.5) = (45)(9.8)(d) ⟹ 441 = 441d ⟹ d = 1 m from the pivot.
Example: a sign hanging from a beam A 3 m uniform beam of weight 50 N is hinged to a wall at one end and held horizontal by a cable attached at the far end, pulling straight up. A 20 N sign hangs from the far end too. Taking torques about the hinge (eliminating the unknown hinge force from the equation): the beam's own weight acts at its center (1.5 m from the hinge, since it's uniform — Unit 4's center of mass), and the cable and sign both act at 3 m. Στ = 0: T(3) − 50(1.5) − 20(3) = 0 ⟹ 3T = 75 + 60 = 135 ⟹ T = 45 N.
Why a uniform beam's weight acts at its center A uniform beam's weight is really the sum of the weight of every small piece along its length, each contributing its own tiny torque. By symmetry, this is exactly equivalent to a single force equal to the total weight acting at the beam's center of mass (Unit 4, Lesson 5) — the geometric center for a uniform object — which is why every torque problem involving a beam's own weight places that force at the midpoint.

1. Full static equilibrium of a rigid object requires:

An object can satisfy one condition without the other (e.g. a spinning skater has ΣF=0 but Στ≠0 while spinning up), so both ΣF=0 and Στ=0 are required for full static equilibrium.

2. When solving a torque equilibrium problem with an unknown force acting at a hinge, why is it convenient to choose the pivot at the hinge itself?

Since torque depends on distance from the pivot, placing the pivot exactly where an unknown force acts eliminates that force's torque contribution (r = 0), simplifying the algebra without changing the physics.

3. A 3 m seesaw pivots at its center. A 40 kg child sits 1 m from the pivot. Where must a 20 kg child sit on the other side to balance it?

(40)(1) = (20)(d) ⟹ d = 40/20 = 2 m.

4. Fill in the blank: a uniform beam's weight can be treated as a single force acting at its ___ for the purposes of a torque calculation.

By symmetry, the combined torque of every small piece of a uniform beam's weight equals the torque of the total weight acting at the beam's center of mass.
