Newton's Second Law for Rotation

τ = Iα is F = ma's rotational twin, and it's used exactly the same way.

Newton's Second Law for Rotation τnet = Iα — the net torque on an object equals its moment of inertia times its angular acceleration. This plays exactly the role Fnet = ma played in Unit 2: given the torques acting on a rotating object, it predicts the resulting angular acceleration, and vice versa.
Why the translation from F = ma is so direct Every quantity in Fnet = ma has already been given a rotational partner: force → torque, mass → moment of inertia, linear acceleration → angular acceleration. The physical content is identical — a net rotational "push" (torque) causes a change in rotational motion (angular acceleration), resisted in proportion to how much rotational inertia the object has, exactly as force causes linear acceleration resisted by mass.
Example: spinning up a disk A solid disk (I = ½MR², M = 4 kg, R = 0.3 m, so I = 0.18 kg·m² as in Lesson 3) has a net torque of 0.9 N·m applied to it. α = τnet/I = 0.9/0.18 = 5 rad/s².
Example: a falling mass turning a pulley A 2 kg mass hangs from a string wrapped around a pulley (a solid disk, M = 3 kg, R = 0.1 m, I = ½MR² = 0.015 kg·m²), free to fall and spin the pulley. The string tension T provides the torque: τ = TR = Iα, and the falling mass obeys mg − T = ma with a = Rα (the string doesn't slip). Combining: mg − T = mRα and TR = Iα ⟹ T = Iα/R. Substituting: mg − Iα/R = mRα ⟹ α = mgR / (mR² + I) = (2)(9.8)(0.1) / [(2)(0.01) + 0.015] = 1.96/0.035 ≈ 56 rad/s². This kind of coupled linear-rotational system is common on the AP exam — the key move is always matching alinear = Rα at the point of contact.
Why the pulley's own mass matters here If the pulley were massless (I = 0), the equation would reduce to mg = ma — the familiar Unit 2 free-fall-with-tension result, since a massless pulley has no rotational inertia to resist spinning up. A pulley with real mass "steals" some of the available force into spinning itself up rather than accelerating the hanging mass, which is exactly what the extra I/R² term in the denominator represents.

1. τ_net = Iα is the direct rotational analog of which linear equation?

Torque plays the role of force, moment of inertia plays the role of mass, and angular acceleration plays the role of linear acceleration — τ_net = Iα is F_net = ma translated to rotation.

2. A net torque of 4 N·m is applied to an object with moment of inertia 2 kg·m². What angular acceleration results?

α = τ_net/I = 4/2 = 2 rad/s².

3. In a falling-mass-and-pulley system, why does a heavier (more massive) pulley reduce the resulting linear acceleration of the falling mass, compared to a massless pulley?

The pulley's moment of inertia I appears in the denominator of a = mgR²/(mR² + I), so a larger I (heavier pulley) reduces the resulting acceleration.

4. Fill in the blank: in τ_net = Iα, the α represents the object's ___.

Angular acceleration α is the rate of change of angular velocity, playing the same role linear acceleration plays in F_net = ma.
