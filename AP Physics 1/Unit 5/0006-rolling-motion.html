Rolling Motion

Rolling without slipping links an object's translation and its rotation with a single constraint equation.

← Lesson 5 Unit 6, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Rolling Without Slipping An object rolls without slipping when its contact point with the ground is momentarily at rest — no skidding. This gives the rolling constraint: vcm = Rω (and correspondingly acm = Rα), linking the center of mass's linear speed directly to the object's angular velocity.
Why the contact point must be at rest Picture a wheel's contact point: if it were moving forward relative to the ground, the wheel would be dragging or skidding forward (like tires spinning on ice); if moving backward, it would be skidding the other way. Rolling without slipping is exactly the borderline case where that point's velocity is zero at each instant — even though the wheel's center is moving at vcm and its top edge is moving at 2vcm (found by adding the translational vcm to the rotational vt = Rω at each point, which cancel exactly at the bottom and add fully at the top).
Example: relating speed and angular velocity A bicycle wheel of radius 0.35 m rolls without slipping at vcm = 7 m/s. Its angular velocity: ω = vcm/R = 7/0.35 = 20 rad/s.

A rolling object has both kinds of kinetic energy

A rolling object's total kinetic energy is the sum of translational KE (its center of mass moving) and rotational KE (spinning about that center of mass) — a preview of Unit 6, which formalizes rotational kinetic energy as KErot = ½Iω². This split is exactly why, released from rest at the same height on a ramp, different-shaped objects reach the bottom with different speeds: some of the available gravitational PE goes into spinning them up rather than speeding up their center of mass, and how much depends on each shape's moment of inertia (Lesson 3).

Why a hoop loses a race against a solid disk Using the rolling constraint to write ω = vcm/R, a hoop's larger moment of inertia (I = MR², Lesson 3) means more of its available energy converts into rotational KE and correspondingly less into translational KE, for the same total energy budget — so at the bottom of an identical ramp, the hoop's center of mass is moving slower than the solid disk's, even though both started from the same height with the same total mechanical energy. Unit 6 works this comparison out with full equations.
Example: static friction enables rolling without slipping A ball accelerating while rolling without slipping needs a torque to spin it up — this comes from static friction at the contact point, acting even though the object isn't sliding. Static friction here does no work (the contact point has zero velocity at each instant — recall Lesson 1's Unit 3 tie-in that zero displacement means zero work), which is precisely why rolling without slipping can conserve mechanical energy while sliding friction cannot.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. For an object rolling without slipping, what is true of its contact point with the ground at each instant?

Rolling without slipping is defined by the contact point having zero velocity — any nonzero contact-point velocity means the object is skidding.

2. A wheel of radius 0.5 m rolls without slipping with angular velocity 10 rad/s. What is its center-of-mass speed?

v_cm = Rω = (0.5)(10) = 5 m/s.

3. Released from rest at the same height on identical ramps, why does a solid disk reach the bottom faster than a hoop of the same mass and radius?

With the same total mechanical energy available, a larger moment of inertia (the hoop's) means proportionally more energy goes into spinning and less into center-of-mass translation, resulting in a slower v_cm at the bottom.

4. Fill in the blank: the condition v_cm = Rω, where the contact point is instantaneously at rest, describes ___.

Rolling without slipping links translational and rotational motion through v_cm = Rω, distinguishing it from skidding.
