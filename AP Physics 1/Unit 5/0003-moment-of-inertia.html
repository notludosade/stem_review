Moment of Inertia

Rotational inertia isn't just about how much mass an object has — it's about how far that mass sits from the axis.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Moment of Inertia Moment of inertia (I) is the rotational equivalent of mass — a measure of an object's resistance to changes in its angular velocity. For a point mass, I = mr², where r is the distance from the rotation axis. For a system of point masses, I = Σmiri².
Why r is squared Doubling a point mass's distance from the axis doesn't just double its contribution to rotational inertia — it quadruples it. This follows from combining vt = rω (Lesson 1) with the mass's ordinary kinetic energy ½mvt² = ½m(rω)² = ½(mr²)ω² — the r² inside this expression is exactly what defines I = mr², so mass farther from the axis is "worth more" rotationally in a way that scales with the square of its distance.
Moments of Inertia for Common Shapes Real rigid objects are treated as continuous distributions of mass rather than a single point, giving standard formulas (provided on the AP Physics 1 exam, not required to derive): solid disk/cylinder about its center, I = ½MR²; thin hoop about its center, I = MR²; solid sphere about its center, I = ⅖MR²; thin rod about its center, I = (1/12)ML²; thin rod about one end, I = (1/3)ML².
Why a hoop and a disk of the same mass and radius have different I A hoop concentrates all its mass at radius R, while a disk spreads its mass from the center out to R, with much of it much closer to the axis than R. Since I depends on r², the disk's inner mass contributes far less per unit mass than the hoop's edge-concentrated mass — this is exactly why a solid disk (½MR²) has a smaller moment of inertia than a hoop of the same M and R (MR²), and why, released from the same height on a ramp, a disk outraces a hoop (a fact made precise in Unit 6).
Example: two masses on a rod Two 1 kg point masses sit at r = 0.2 m and r = 0.5 m from an axis. I = Σmr² = (1)(0.2²) + (1)(0.5²) = 0.04 + 0.25 = 0.29 kg·m².
Example: a solid disk A solid disk of mass 4 kg and radius 0.3 m rotating about its center: I = ½MR² = ½(4)(0.3²) = ½(4)(0.09) = 0.18 kg·m².

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. If a point mass's distance from the rotation axis is doubled, its contribution to moment of inertia:

I = mr² depends on r squared, so doubling r multiplies the contribution to I by 2² = 4.

2. Why does a hoop have a larger moment of inertia than a solid disk of the same mass and radius?

Since I depends on r², concentrating mass at the largest possible radius (as in a hoop) maximizes I compared to spreading mass throughout the interior (as in a disk).

3. A point mass of 2 kg sits at r = 0.3 m from an axis. What is its moment of inertia?

I = mr² = (2)(0.3²) = (2)(0.09) = 0.18 kg·m².

4. Fill in the blank: the rotational equivalent of mass, measuring resistance to changes in angular velocity, is called ___.

Moment of inertia (rotational inertia) I = Σmr² depends on both mass and its distribution relative to the axis.
