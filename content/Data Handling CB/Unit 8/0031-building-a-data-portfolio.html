Building a Data Portfolio

A data portfolio is where this course's units stop being separate skills and become one demonstrated piece of work — the artifact that actually gets a Data Analyst role's door open.

← Lesson 30 Lesson 32 → Contents Glossary →

A data portfolio is a small collection of complete, end-to-end projects showing the entire pipeline this course covers — not just a finished chart, but the messy dataset behind it and the reasoning that got from one to the other.

What a strong portfolio project actually shows

The full pipeline, visibly A strong project makes each stage visible, not just the final output: the raw dataset (with its real problems — Unit 5's missing values, outliers, duplicates), the cleaning decisions made and why (the cleaning log from Lesson 22), the SQL queries used to explore it (Unit 3), the statistical reasoning behind any claim (Unit 4, including explicitly checking for confounders per Lesson 16), and the final chart or dashboard with its audience-appropriate design (Unit 6). A reviewer evaluating a candidate isn't just checking whether the final chart looks nice — they're checking whether the reasoning in between holds up.
Why a polished-looking chart with no visible process is actually a weaker portfolio piece Lesson 24 showed that a chart can be technically accurate and still misleading depending on decisions made upstream — and a portfolio piece that shows only the final chart gives a reviewer no way to check whether those upstream decisions were made carefully. Showing the raw, messy data and the specific judgment calls made to clean it (with reasons, per Lesson 22's cleaning-log argument) is what lets a reviewer trust the conclusion, rather than just admire the picture. This is also, practically, more memorable: dozens of portfolios show a clean bar chart; far fewer show the reasoning that earned it.
Choosing a project topic A project built around a real question you personally care about (not just any public dataset picked at random) tends to produce a more coherent, better-argued story, because you already have Unit 6's "Context" step for free — you already know why the question matters. Public datasets from sources like data.gov, Kaggle, or a local government's open-data portal work well as raw material, as long as the project goes through this course's full pipeline rather than stopping at a first chart.
