The Data Case-Study Interview

Lesson 31's portfolio gets you the interview. This lesson is about the interview itself — the specific format data roles use to test whether you can actually think, not just recite.

← Lesson 31 Lesson 33 → Contents Glossary →

A case-study interview presents a realistic business scenario and a dataset (or a description of one) and asks the candidate to reason through it live — often "How would you investigate why signups dropped 20% last month?" rather than a pure technical quiz.

What's actually being evaluated

The interviewer is watching your process, not just your answer A case-study question almost never has one "correct" final answer — the interviewer is watching whether you: ask clarifying questions before diving in (what does "signups" mean exactly, over what exact time window, compared to what baseline?), propose a structured approach rather than guessing, and — critically — surface the same considerations this course has built: is the drop real or could it be a sampling/measurement artifact (Lesson 2), is there a plausible confounding explanation before jumping to a causal story (Lesson 16), and what would you actually check first, and why.
Why "I'd check for a confounding variable" is a stronger interview answer than a specific number Interviewers running case studies already know most candidates don't have the real data in front of them and can't produce an exact right number live — that isn't the point. What's actually being tested is whether the candidate's default instinct, when given a striking finding, is to reach for a plausible driver immediately (the Lesson 16 mistake) or to first ask what else could explain it. A candidate who says "before concluding X caused the drop, I'd rule out Y and Z as more likely explanations" is demonstrating exactly the discipline this course has built across Units 4 through 7 — which is a stronger, more hireable signal than confidently naming a specific (possibly wrong) root cause.
Worked example: talking through a case study out loud Prompt: "Weekly active users dropped 15% last week — what would you look into?" A strong response: "First, I'd confirm the drop is real and not a data-collection issue — did tracking break, or did the definition of 'active' change (Lesson 3's consistency)? Next, I'd segment the drop — is it concentrated in one user group, one platform, one region — since an aggregate number can hide where the actual change happened (Lesson 12's GROUP BY). I'd also check for an obvious confounder before assuming a product issue — a holiday, a competitor's launch, a known outage. Only after ruling those out would I look for a product-side explanation." Notice: no specific number is given, and that's fine — the reasoning is the answer.
