Proof by Contrapositive

Some conditionals are painful to prove head-on and easy to prove sideways. Contrapositive proof is the same direct-proof machinery from Unit 2, just aimed at a logically equivalent statement that happens to be easier to work with.

← Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

"If n² is even, then n is even" is true, but a direct proof starting from "n² is even" struggles immediately — n² = 2m tells you almost nothing useful about n itself. This lesson introduces a way around exactly that kind of stuck point, using a statement logically identical to the original but built out of negations that are often much easier to work with directly.

Definition — Contrapositive The contrapositive of P → Q is ¬Q → ¬P. A conditional and its contrapositive are logically equivalent — they have identical truth values in every case — so proving one proves the other.
Why P → Q and ¬Q → ¬P always agree P → Q is false in exactly one case: P true, Q false. ¬Q → ¬P is false in exactly one case: ¬Q true (Q false) and ¬P false (P true) — the identical case, just described in reversed order. Since the two statements are false under exactly the same circumstances and true under exactly the same circumstances, they're logically equivalent — not "usually" or "often," but in every one of the four possible truth-value combinations of P and Q, with no exceptions.
Definition — Contrapositive Proof A proof by contrapositive of P → Q proceeds by giving a direct proof of ¬Q → ¬P: assume ¬Q, reason forward through justified steps, and conclude ¬P. Because ¬Q → ¬P is logically equivalent to P → Q, this proves the original claim.
Contrapositive is not proof by contradiction — a distinction worth getting exactly right These two techniques are easy to blur together, and Lesson 8 depends on keeping them separate. A contrapositive proof is an ordinary direct proof of ¬Q → ¬P: assume ¬Q, reason forward, land on ¬P — no contradiction is ever derived, and P is never assumed at all. A proof by contradiction (next lesson) instead assumes P and ¬Q together — the negation of the whole implication — and hunts for two statements that directly conflict. Contrapositive proof has exactly one assumption and one forward chain; contradiction proof has two simultaneous assumptions and ends by breaking something, not by concluding ¬P.
Worked example Claim: If n² is even, then n is even.
Direct attempt: assume n² = 2m. Solving for n gives n = √(2m), which isn't obviously an integer, let alone visibly even — a dead end.
Contrapositive: the contrapositive is "if n is odd, then n² is odd" — exactly Lesson 5's theorem, already proved by ordinary direct proof: n = 2k+1 ⟹ n² = 2(2k²+2k)+1, odd. Since this contrapositive is proved, the original claim — if n² is even, then n is even — is proved too. ∎

When contrapositive is worth reaching for

Why "n is not even" is easier to work with than "n² is even" The hypothesis of the contrapositive, ¬Q, is often a positive, algebraically concrete statement ("n is odd," meaning n = 2k+1) even when the original conclusion Q was itself a negative or awkward one to assume backwards from. Whenever a direct attempt gets stuck specifically because the hypothesis is hard to unpack into something you can compute with — while its negation unpacks easily — contrapositive is the technique built for exactly that situation.

1. What is the contrapositive of "if x > 5, then x² > 25"?

The contrapositive negates and swaps both parts: ¬(x²>25) → ¬(x>5), i.e., x² ≤ 25 → x ≤ 5.

2. Which of the following is the contrapositive of P → Q?

¬Q → ¬P is the contrapositive — negate both parts and swap their order. Q → P is the converse; ¬P → ¬Q is the inverse; neither is guaranteed equivalent to P → Q.

3. A contrapositive proof of "if n² is even, then n is even" actually gives a direct proof of which statement?

The contrapositive of "n² even → n even" negates and swaps: "n odd → n² odd" — exactly Lesson 5's already-proved theorem.

4. Fill in the blank: the statement ¬Q → ¬P is called the ___ of P → Q, and is always logically equivalent to it.

Contrapositive — the one rearrangement of P → Q (out of converse, inverse, and contrapositive) that's guaranteed equivalent to the original.

5. How does proof by contrapositive differ from proof by contradiction (Lesson 8)?

Contrapositive proof has exactly one assumption (¬Q) and ends by concluding ¬P through ordinary forward reasoning. Contradiction proof assumes both P and ¬Q at once and ends by deriving something outright false — a genuinely different structure, covered next lesson.
