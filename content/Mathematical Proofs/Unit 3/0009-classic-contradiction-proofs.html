Classic Contradiction Proofs

Two of the oldest proofs in mathematics, both over two thousand years old, both still the cleanest possible demonstration of what contradiction is for.

← Lesson 8 Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 8 built the machinery. This lesson runs it on the two claims that made contradiction famous in the first place — both are existence-flavored claims that direct proof has no real angle on, and both fall apart cleanly once you assume they're false.

Theorem — √2 Is Irrational There do not exist integers p and q, with q ≠ 0, such that √2 = p/q.
Proof Suppose, toward contradiction, that √2 is rational. Then √2 = p/q for some integers p, q with q ≠ 0, and we may assume p/q is in lowest terms (any common factor cancelled) — so p and q share no common factor greater than 1. Squaring both sides: 2 = p²/q², so p² = 2q². This means p² is even, and by Lesson 7's theorem (n² even ⟹ n even), p itself is even: p = 2k for some integer k. Substituting back: (2k)² = 2q², so 4k² = 2q², so q² = 2k². This means q² is even, so by the same theorem q is even too. But then p and q are both even — contradicting the assumption that p/q was in lowest terms, since 2 would be a common factor. This contradiction shows √2 is irrational. ∎
Why "lowest terms" is the load-bearing assumption Without fixing p/q in lowest terms at the start, the argument only shows "p and q are both even," which isn't false on its own — 4/2 has an even numerator and denominator, and 4/2 = 2 just fine. The contradiction only appears because lowest terms was assumed and then violated. This is a second, quieter appearance of Lesson 3's lesson: read the setup of a proof as carefully as its algebra, since an unstated assumption early on is where the entire argument's leverage often comes from.
Theorem — There Are Infinitely Many Primes (Euclid) The set of prime numbers is infinite.
Proof Suppose, toward contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes: p₁, p₂, …, pₙ, a complete list. Consider N = p₁ · p₂ · ⋯ · pₙ + 1. Since N > 1, N has at least one prime factor (every integer greater than 1 does — Unit 4's induction argument makes this precise). Call that prime factor p. Since p₁, …, pₙ was assumed to be the complete list of primes, p must equal some pᵢ on the list. But then p divides p₁ · p₂ · ⋯ · pₙ (it's one of the factors), and p also divides N by construction — so p divides their difference, N − p₁p₂⋯pₙ = 1. But no prime divides 1, since every prime is greater than 1. This contradiction shows the list of primes could never have been complete — there are infinitely many. ∎
Why N = p₁p₂⋯pₙ + 1, and not just the product itself The "+1" is the entire trick. The product p₁p₂⋯pₙ alone is divisible by every prime on the list, which proves nothing. Adding 1 guarantees that N leaves remainder 1 when divided by any pᵢ on the list — so none of the "complete" list's primes can divide N. Yet N still needs a prime factor of its own. That factor can't be on the list and can't be off it either, under the assumption the list was complete — the contradiction Euclid needed.
Try It — Euclid's Construction on a Small "Complete" List

Suppose someone claims the primes are exactly {2, 3, 5} — a "complete" list. Click through what N = 2·3·5 + 1 reveals.

  1. Build N. N = 2 × 3 × 5 + 1 = 31.
  2. Check divisibility by each listed prime. 31 ÷ 2 leaves remainder 1. 31 ÷ 3 leaves remainder 1. 31 ÷ 5 leaves remainder 1. None of the "complete" list's primes divide 31.
  3. But 31 needs a prime factor. In fact 31 is itself prime — so its prime factor is 31.
  4. Contradiction. 31 is a prime not on the "complete" list {2, 3, 5} — so that list was never complete. In general N's prime factor might not be N itself (N can be composite), but it's always a new prime absent from the assumed list.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the √2 proof, what does "p and q are both even" ultimately contradict?

Both p and q even means they share the common factor 2 — directly contradicting the lowest-terms assumption fixed at the start of the proof.

2. Which earlier result does the √2 proof reuse twice?

It's applied once to conclude p is even from p² = 2q² being even, and again to conclude q is even from q² = 2k² being even.

3. In Euclid's proof, why is N built as p₁p₂⋯pₙ + 1 instead of just the plain product?

Adding 1 is what forces every listed prime to fail to divide N, forcing N's own prime factor to be new — the entire engine of the contradiction.

4. Fill in the blank: the √2 proof begins by assuming p/q is written in ___, meaning p and q share no common factor greater than 1.

Lowest terms is the assumption that later gets violated, producing the contradiction.

5. Fill in the blank: in Euclid's proof, the final contradiction is that some prime p would have to divide ___, which no prime can do.

p divides both N and the product p₁⋯pₙ, so it divides their difference, which equals 1 — and no prime (all greater than 1) can divide 1.
