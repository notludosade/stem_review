Quantifiers: For All and There Exists

"Every even number greater than 2 is a sum of two primes" and "some even number greater than 2 is a sum of two primes" are wildly different claims. Quantifiers are what tell them apart.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

A sentence like "x is even" isn't a statement by Lesson 1's definition — its truth depends on a free variable x that hasn't been pinned down. Quantifiers pin it down, in one of exactly two ways: by claiming it holds for every possible x, or by claiming it holds for at least one x.

Definition — Universal and Existential Quantifiers Let P(x) be a predicate (a sentence with a free variable x) and let x range over some fixed domain of discourse D. The universal quantifier ∀x ∈ D, P(x) ("for all x in D, P(x)") is true exactly when P(x) is true for every single x in D. The existential quantifier ∃x ∈ D, P(x) ("there exists x in D such that P(x)") is true exactly when P(x) is true for at least one x in D — one is enough, and it doesn't matter which.
Why the domain has to be fixed first "∀x, x² ≥ 0" is true if x ranges over the reals, but the domain always has to be nailed down before the claim even makes sense — the sentence has one truth value over ℝ and a possibly different one over a different domain (e.g. the complex numbers, where it fails). Every quantified statement in this course names its domain explicitly, or leaves it clear from context (usually ℤ, ℚ, ℝ, or ℕ) — a "for all" with no stated universe isn't a finished statement yet.
Example — one counterexample is enough Claim: ∀n ∈ {2, 3, 5, 7, 9}, n is prime. Checking each: 2 ✓, 3 ✓, 5 ✓, 7 ✓, 9 ✗ (9 = 3×3). Since the claim demands every element work, a single failure — 9 — makes the whole universal statement false. Compare: ∃n ∈ {2, 3, 5, 7, 9}, n is prime is true, and just as easily verified — 2 alone is enough, and the fact that 9 fails is completely irrelevant to it.

Negating a quantified statement

Disproving a claim means proving its negation. Quantified statements negate by a fixed, mechanical rule — the quantifier flips, and the predicate gets negated.

Rule — Negating Quantifiers ¬(∀x ∈ D, P(x)) is logically equivalent to ∃x ∈ D, ¬P(x). ¬(∃x ∈ D, P(x)) is logically equivalent to ∀x ∈ D, ¬P(x). In words: "not every x works" means exactly "some x fails," and "no x works" means exactly "every x fails."
Example Claim: "Every prime number greater than 2 is odd." Symbolically, ∀p ∈ ℙ with p > 2, p is odd. Its negation is not "every prime greater than 2 is even" — that's a much stronger, different claim. The correct negation is ∃p ∈ ℙ with p > 2, p is not odd (i.e., some prime greater than 2 is even). In this case the negation happens to be false — no such prime exists — but forming it correctly is a separate skill from evaluating it, and it's the one this lesson is testing.

Order matters: ∀x∃y is not the same claim as ∃y∀x

Why swapping quantifier order can change the truth value entirely Compare, over the integers: (1) ∀x ∃y, y > x — "every integer has some integer bigger than it" — true, just take y = x + 1. (2) ∃y ∀x, y > x — "some single integer is bigger than every integer" — false, since no integer can exceed itself or anything larger. The only difference between (1) and (2) is which quantifier comes first, yet one is true and the other is false. In (1), y is allowed to depend on whichever x you were just handed; in (2), a single y has to work simultaneously against every x at once. This exact pattern — a chosen quantity allowed to depend on what came before it, versus one required to work universally — is precisely the structure behind the ε-N and ε-δ definitions used throughout real analysis.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. To prove ∀x ∈ D, P(x) is false, what must you produce?

Since ¬(∀x, P(x)) ≡ ∃x, ¬P(x), disproving a universal claim only ever requires exhibiting one failing case — a counterexample.

2. What is the correct negation of "∀x ∈ ℝ, x² > 0"?

Negating ∀ flips it to ∃ and negates the predicate: ¬(x² > 0) is x² ≤ 0. (This negation is in fact true, witnessed by x = 0.)

3. Over the integers, which claim is true: "∀x ∃y, x + y = 0" or "∃y ∀x, x + y = 0"?

"∀x ∃y" lets y depend on x, so y = −x always works. "∃y ∀x" demands one fixed y that cancels every x simultaneously — impossible, since x ranges over infinitely many values.

4. Fill in the blank: a single element of the domain for which a universally quantified claim fails is called a ___.

One counterexample is exactly enough to disprove a ∀ claim, no matter how large the domain is.

5. Fill in the blank: to disprove ∃x ∈ D, P(x), you must show that P(x) is false for ___ x in D.

Since ¬(∃x, P(x)) ≡ ∀x, ¬P(x), disproving an existence claim is the harder direction — it requires ruling out P(x) across the entire domain, not just one case.
