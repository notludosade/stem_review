Limits

Limit, limx→c f(x) = L

As x gets arbitrarily close to c (from either side, without ever equaling c), f(x) gets arbitrarily close to L. A limit describes behavior near c, regardless of whether f(c) itself is defined.

Left-hand limit, limx→c⁻ f(x)

What f approaches as x nears c only from values less than c.

Right-hand limit, limx→c⁺ f(x)

What f approaches as x nears c only from values greater than c.

Does not exist (DNE)

A two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist and agree. If they disagree, the limit does not exist — even if f(c) itself is a perfectly ordinary number.

Instantaneous rate of change

The number a secant slope homes in on as the gap between its two points shrinks to zero — a limit, applied to slope. This is the derivative, in embryo.

Limit Laws & Techniques

Limit Laws

If lim f(x) = A and lim g(x) = B both exist, then lim[f(x) + g(x)] = A + B, lim[f(x)·g(x)] = A·B, and lim[f(x)/g(x)] = A/B (provided B ≠ 0). For polynomials and rational functions, this means direct substitution works wherever the function is defined.

Factor and cancel

When substitution gives 0/0, factor the expression and cancel the shared factor causing it, then substitute into the simplified form.

Rationalizing (the conjugate)

When a square root causes 0/0, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate to eliminate the root, cancel the shared factor, then substitute.

Squeeze Theorem

If f(x) ≤ g(x) ≤ h(x) for all x near c, and limx→c f(x) = limx→c h(x) = L, then limx→c g(x) = L too — g is squeezed to the same limit as its bounds, whatever g itself is doing in between.

Continuity

Continuity at a point

f is continuous at c when all three hold: f(c) is defined, limx→c f(x) exists, and limx→c f(x) = f(c). Failing any one of the three makes c a discontinuity.

Removable discontinuity

The limit exists, but it doesn't equal f(c) — either because f(c) is the wrong value or undefined. Fixable by redefining f(c) to match the limit; visually, a hole in the graph.

Jump discontinuity

The left-hand and right-hand limits both exist individually but disagree, so the two-sided limit fails to exist. Not fixable by redefining a single point.

Infinite discontinuity

f grows without bound (toward +∞ or −∞) on at least one side as x approaches c — a vertical asymptote sits at c. Not fixable by redefining a single point.

Infinite Limits & Asymptotes

Vertical asymptote

Occurs at x = c when a rational function's denominator is zero at c and its numerator is not also zero there. Near c, the function grows without bound rather than converging.

Limit at infinity, limx→∞ f(x)

What f(x) approaches as x grows without bound in the positive (or, for x → −∞, negative) direction.

Horizontal asymptote

The line y = L when limx→∞ f(x) or limx→−∞ f(x) equals the finite value L. Unlike a vertical asymptote, the function is often free to actually reach or cross it.

Degree-comparison shortcut

For a rational function: denominator's degree wins → horizontal asymptote y = 0. Degrees equal → y = ratio of leading coefficients. Numerator's degree wins → no horizontal asymptote.

Intermediate Value Theorem

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

If f is continuous on [a, b], and k is any value between f(a) and f(b), then there exists at least one c in [a, b] such that f(c) = k. Guarantees existence, not uniqueness — and never says how to find c.

Derivatives

Secant line

The line through two points on a curve. Its slope is the average rate of change between those two points.

Tangent line

The line a secant line converges to as its second point slides toward the first — the line that matches a curve's instantaneous direction at a single point.

Difference quotient

[f(a + h) − f(a)] / h — the secant slope between a and a + h, expressed as a function of h.

Derivative, f′(a)

limh→0 [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h, provided the limit exists. Equals the slope of the tangent line to f at a — the instantaneous rate of change of f there.

Leibniz notation, dy/dx

Writes the derivative as a ratio of tiny changes — "how much y changes per tiny change in x." At a point: dy/dx│x=a.

Symmetric difference quotient

[f(a + h) − f(a − h)] / (2h) — estimates f′(a) using values on both sides of a at once, usually more accurately than a one-sided quotient.

Corner

A point where a continuous graph's left and right one-sided slopes disagree but are both finite — like the vertex of |x|. No single tangent line fits, so the derivative fails to exist.

Cusp

A point where a continuous graph's one-sided slopes both go to infinity but in opposite directions (+∞ on one side, −∞ on the other). Pointy, like x^(2/3) at 0.

Vertical tangent

A point where a continuous graph's slope goes to infinity on both sides in agreement — the tangent line is vertical (undefined slope), but the curve stays smooth in direction, like ∛x at 0.

Power Rule

d/dx[xⁿ] = n·xn−1, for any real number n. Drop the exponent down as a coefficient, then subtract one from it.

Constant, Sum/Difference, Constant Multiple Rules

d/dx[c] = 0. d/dx[c·f(x)] = c·f′(x). d/dx[f(x) ± g(x)] = f′(x) ± g′(x). Together with the Power Rule, these differentiate any polynomial term by term.

d/dx[sin x] = cos x, d/dx[cos x] = −sin x

The two basic trig derivatives, proven from the limit definition. Note the sign flip on cosine — the only minus sign in the pair.

d/dx[eˣ] = eˣ

The exponential function is its own derivative — its slope at any point equals its height there. Unique among elementary functions.

d/dx[ln x] = 1/x

The derivative of the natural log, for x > 0. Notable because ln x is not itself a power function, yet its derivative is.

Product Rule

(f·g)′ = f′·g + f·g′. The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives — it's two cross-terms added together.

Quotient Rule

(f/g)′ = (f′·g − f·g′) / g². Shares the Product Rule's two cross-terms, but subtracted instead of added, and divided by g².

d/dx[tan x] = sec²x, d/dx[cot x] = −csc²x

Both follow from the Quotient Rule applied to sin x/cos x and cos x/sin x. As with sine and cosine, the "co" function's derivative carries the minus sign.

d/dx[sec x] = sec x tan x, d/dx[csc x] = −csc x cot x

Each derivative is a product of the original reciprocal function and its matching tangent-family function — again, the "co" function (cosecant) is the one with the minus sign.

Composite, Implicit & Inverse Functions

Chain Rule

If F(x) = f(g(x)), then F′(x) = f′(g(x))·g′(x). Differentiate the outside function at the untouched inside function, then multiply by the inside function's own derivative.

General power rule

d/dx[g(x)]ⁿ = n[g(x)]ⁿ⁻¹·g′(x) — the ordinary Power Rule, with an extra factor of g′(x) from the Chain Rule since the base is itself a function of x.

Implicit differentiation

Differentiating both sides of an equation in x and y with respect to x, treating y as a function of x — every y-term picks up a factor of y′ by the Chain Rule. Used when the equation can't (or shouldn't) be solved for y first.

Inverse function derivative rule

If g = f⁻¹, then g′(x) = 1/f′(g(x)), provided f′(g(x)) ≠ 0. Derived by differentiating x = f(g(x)) implicitly with the Chain Rule. Geometrically, tangent slopes on f and f⁻¹ are reciprocals of each other at mirrored points.

d/dx[sin⁻¹x] = 1/√(1−x²), d/dx[cos⁻¹x] = −1/√(1−x²)

Derived from the inverse function rule applied to sin and cos, simplified with a right triangle. Both blow up (undefined derivative) at x = ±1, where sin⁻¹x and cos⁻¹x have vertical tangents.

d/dx[tan⁻¹x] = 1/(1+x²)

Derived the same way from tan. Defined for every real x — tan⁻¹x has no vertical tangents anywhere.

Outermost-operation checklist

To pick which rule to apply first on a combined function, identify the last operation a number would go through: terms added/subtracted → Sum/Difference Rule; two factors multiplied → Product Rule; one expression divided by another → Quotient Rule; a function applied to something other than plain x → Chain Rule. Repeat on whatever pieces remain inside.

Higher-order derivative, f″(x), f‴(x), f⁽ⁿ⁾(x)

The result of differentiating a function more than once: f″(x) = d/dx[f′(x)], f‴(x) = d/dx[f″(x)], and so on. The parenthesized exponent means "differentiated n times" — f²(x) with no parentheses instead means [f(x)]², an unrelated quantity.

Polynomial-degree derivative fact

For a degree-n polynomial, every derivative of order n + 1 or higher equals 0 — each differentiation drops the degree by exactly one, until nothing but a constant, then zero, remains.

Contextual Applications of Differentiation

Derivative as a rate of change

f′(a) is the instantaneous rate of change of f with respect to x at x = a, in units of (units of f) per (units of x) — the same "per" any rate carries, whatever f and x actually measure.

Sign of a rate of change

A positive derivative means the quantity is increasing at that instant; negative means decreasing. The magnitude gives the speed of that change, independent of how large the quantity itself is.

Position, velocity, acceleration

For position s(t), velocity is v(t) = s′(t) and acceleration is a(t) = v′(t) = s″(t) — each one the derivative of the last in a three-link chain.

Speed

|v(t)| — velocity with its sign removed. Velocity's sign carries direction; speed only carries how fast.

Speeding up vs. slowing down

An object speeds up when velocity and acceleration share the same sign, and slows down when their signs are opposite — matching signs, not the sign of acceleration alone, is what decides it.

Related rates

Two quantities tied together by an equation that holds at every instant. Differentiating that equation implicitly with respect to t relates their rates of change — numbers get substituted only after differentiating, never before.

Linearization

L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) — the tangent line's equation at x = a, used as a stand-in for f(x) when x is close to a. The approximation worsens as x moves away from a.

Concavity and approximation error

A concave-up function lies above its tangent lines, so linearization underestimates it; a concave-down function lies below its tangent lines, so linearization overestimates it.

Indeterminate form

A limit of f(x)/g(x) where direct substitution gives 0/0 or ∞/∞ — undefined on its own, since the result depends on how fast numerator and denominator each approach their limit.

L'Hôpital's Rule

If lim f(x)/g(x) is 0/0 or ∞/∞, then lim f(x)/g(x) = lim f′(x)/g′(x), provided the second limit exists — differentiate numerator and denominator separately, then re-check the form before reapplying.

Analytical Applications of Differentiation

Mean Value Theorem (MVT)

If f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), there exists at least one c in (a, b) with f′(c) = (f(b) − f(a)) / (b − a) — some tangent line is parallel to the secant line through the endpoints.

Rolle's Theorem

The special case of the MVT where f(a) = f(b): guarantees a c in (a, b) with f′(c) = 0, a horizontal tangent between two equal function values.

Extreme Value Theorem

If f is continuous on a closed interval [a, b], f attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere in [a, b]. Requires both continuity and a closed, bounded interval.

Critical point

A point c in the domain of f where f′(c) = 0 or f′(c) is undefined. Every local extremum occurs at a critical point, but not every critical point is a local extremum.

First Derivative Test

At a critical point c: if f′ changes from positive to negative, f has a local maximum at c; if negative to positive, a local minimum; if f′ doesn't change sign, c is neither.

Candidates Test

To find the absolute extrema of a continuous f on [a, b]: evaluate f at every critical point in (a, b) and at both endpoints. The largest value is the absolute maximum, the smallest is the absolute minimum.

Concave up / concave down

f is concave up where f′ is increasing (f″(x) > 0), concave down where f′ is decreasing (f″(x) < 0) — describes which way the curve bends, independent of whether f itself is increasing or decreasing.

Inflection point

A point where concavity actually changes — f″ changes sign there, typically (but not sufficiently) where f″(x) = 0 or is undefined.

Second Derivative Test

At a critical point c where f′(c) = 0: if f″(c) > 0, local minimum; if f″(c) < 0, local maximum; if f″(c) = 0, inconclusive — fall back to the First Derivative Test.

Combined sign classification

Four sign combinations of f′ and f″ describe every piece of a smooth curve: increasing/concave up, increasing/concave down, decreasing/concave up, decreasing/concave down.

Optimization strategy

Identify the quantity to optimize, write a formula for it, use a constraint equation to reduce it to one variable, determine the domain from physical limits, then apply the Candidates Test.

Integration and Accumulation of Change

Riemann sum

An approximation of the area under y = f(x) on [a, b]: split into n subintervals of width Δx = (b − a)/n, build a rectangle on each using a sample height, and add the areas.

Left, right, midpoint sums

Three common sample-point choices for a Riemann sum's rectangle heights: the left endpoint, the right endpoint, or the midpoint of each subinterval. All converge to the same value as n → ∞.

Definite integral, ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx

The limit of a Riemann sum as n → ∞. Measures net signed area: regions above the x-axis add positively, regions below subtract.

Properties of definite integrals

∫ₐᵃ f dx = 0. ∫ᵦᵃ f dx = −∫ₐᵇ f dx. ∫ₐᵇ c·f dx = c∫ₐᵇ f dx. ∫ₐᵇ(f ± g) dx = ∫ₐᵇf dx ± ∫ₐᵇg dx. ∫ₐᵇ f dx + ∫ᵦᶜ f dx = ∫ₐᶜ f dx (additivity over adjacent intervals).

Accumulation function, g(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt

A function of x built from a definite integral with a variable upper bound — the running total of signed area swept out as that bound slides.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1

If g(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt and f is continuous, g′(x) = f(x) — differentiating an accumulation function just hands back the original integrand.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2

If F is any antiderivative of f, ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a). Evaluates a definite integral exactly, with no limit of a sum required.

Net Change Theorem

∫ₐᵇ F′(x) dx = F(b) − F(a): the integral of a rate of change equals the net change in the original quantity — e.g., velocity integrates to net displacement, not total distance.

Antiderivative & indefinite integral, ∫f(x)dx = F(x) + C

F is an antiderivative of f if F′ = f. Since constants differentiate to 0, every antiderivative belongs to a family F(x) + C, denoted all at once by the indefinite integral.

u-Substitution

The Chain Rule reversed: for ∫f(g(x))g′(x)dx, let u = g(x), du = g′(x)dx, integrate ∫f(u)du, then substitute back. For a definite integral, converting the bounds to u avoids substituting back at all.

Long division (improper rational integrand)

When a numerator's degree is at least the denominator's, divide first — the fraction becomes a polynomial (integrates by the Power Rule) plus a proper remainder fraction.

Completing the square (for integration)

Reshapes an irreducible quadratic denominator into (x + h)² + k², matching the standard form ∫du/(u² + a²) = (1/a)arctan(u/a) + C.

Partial fractions (repeated factor)

A repeated linear factor (x − r)ᵏ needs one decomposition term per power, up to k: A/(x−r) + B/(x−r)² + … + K/(x−r)ᵏ, each integrating to a log or power-rule term.

Integration by parts, ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du

The Product Rule reversed. Choose u to simplify under differentiation and dv to be easy to antidifferentiate.

Improper integral

An integral with an infinite interval or an unbounded integrand. Replace the problem bound with a variable and take a limit; if the limit is finite, the integral converges, otherwise it diverges. ∫₁^∞ 1/xᵖ dx converges exactly when p > 1.

Differential Equations

Differential equation

An equation relating a function to one or more of its derivatives, such as dy/dx = 2y. A solution is an entire function that makes the equation true, not a single number.

Verifying a solution

Differentiate the proposed function and substitute it (and its derivative) into the differential equation; if both sides match for every x, it's a valid solution.

Slope field (direction field)

A grid of short line segments, one per sample point (x, y), each with slope f(x, y) from dy/dx = f(x, y). Any solution curve must run tangent to the field at every point it passes through.

Euler's Method

A numerical technique approximating a solution: xₙ₊₁ = xₙ + h, yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + h·f(xₙ, yₙ). Smaller step size h reduces error but requires more steps.

Separable equation

A differential equation of the form dy/dx = f(x)g(y), which can be rearranged so all y-terms sit with dy and all x-terms sit with dx, then integrated independently on each side.

General & particular solution

Integrating a separated equation leaves an arbitrary constant, producing a whole family of curves — the general solution. An initial condition pins down that constant, giving the one particular solution passing through it.

Exponential model, y = y₀e^(kt)

The solution to dy/dt = ky, where any quantity's rate of change is proportional to its current value. k > 0 gives growth; k < 0 gives decay.

Logistic differential equation

dP/dt = rP(1 − P/K), where K is the carrying capacity. Growth looks exponential while P is small and slows to zero as P approaches K, producing an S-shaped curve.

Carrying capacity & inflection point

K is the population level a logistic model levels off at as t → ∞. The curve grows fastest — its inflection point — at P = K/2, the midpoint of the S-shape.

Applications of Integration

Average value of a function

favg = (1/(b−a)) ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx — the total accumulated area divided by the interval's width, generalizing the average of finitely many numbers to a continuum.

Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

If f is continuous on [a, b], some point c in [a, b] has f(c) = favg — the function actually attains its own average value somewhere in the interval.

Motion via the Net Change Theorem

s(b) = s(a) + ∫ₐᵇv(t)dt and v(b) = v(a) + ∫ₐᵇa(t)dt. Displacement is the signed integral of velocity; total distance traveled is ∫|v(t)|dt, splitting at every sign change of v.

Area between two curves (wrt x)

A = ∫ₐᵇ [f(x) − g(x)] dx, where f(x) ≥ g(x) on [a, b] — top function minus bottom function, integrated across the shared interval.

Area between two curves (wrt y)

A = ∫cd [f(y) − g(y)] dy, where f(y) ≥ g(y) (right of g) on [c, d] — the same idea with the axes' roles swapped, useful when boundaries are naturally left/right functions of y.

Splitting at every crossing

When two curves intersect more than twice on an interval, "top minus bottom" can flip at each interior intersection. The total area is the sum of |top − bottom| integrated separately over each piece.

Volume from known cross sections

V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, where A(x) is the area of a cross section built from the base region's width s(x) = f(x) − g(x) at each x — square: s², triangle: (√3/4)s², semicircle: (π/8)s².

Disc method

Revolving the region under y = f(x) around an axis sweeps out circular cross sections. V = π∫ₐᵇ[R(x)]²dx, where R(x) is the distance from the curve to the axis of revolution.

Washer method

V = π∫ₐᵇ[R(x)² − r(x)²]dx — the disc method with an inner radius r(x) subtracted, used when the revolved region doesn't touch the axis and each slice sweeps out a ring instead of a solid disc.

Arc length of y = f(x)

L = ∫ₐᵇ √(1 + [f′(x)]²) dx, from the Pythagorean theorem applied to an infinitesimal piece of curve with horizontal leg dx and vertical leg f′(x)dx.

Distance traveled, parametric (BC)

For a path (x(t), y(t)), distance traveled = ∫ₐᵇ √([x′(t)]² + [y′(t)]²) dt — the arc length idea applied to a path traced over time, generalizing 1D distance traveled to two dimensions.

Parametric, Polar & Vector-Valued Functions

Parametric curve

x = x(t), y = y(t) for t in some interval — a curve traced by a point whose coordinates are each their own function of a shared parameter t. Every y = f(x) graph is the special case x(t) = t, y(t) = f(t).

Parametric derivative

dy/dx = (dy/dt) / (dx/dt), from the chain rule dy/dt = (dy/dx)(dx/dt), wherever dx/dt ≠ 0. If both dx/dt and dy/dt vanish at once, the point needs closer inspection.

Parametric second derivative

d²y/dx² = [d/dt(dy/dx)] / (dx/dt) — differentiate dy/dx with respect to t, then divide by dx/dt again. Its sign gives concavity, exactly as for y = f(x).

Arc length of a parametric curve

L = ∫ₐᵇ √[(dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²] dt — the same formula as parametric distance traveled, now measuring the length of the path itself rather than motion over time.

Vector-valued function

r(t) = ⟨x(t), y(t)⟩ — packages a parametric pair into a single arrow-valued function. Differentiation and integration act componentwise: r′(t) = ⟨x′(t), y′(t)⟩.

Velocity, acceleration & speed

For position r(t): velocity v(t) = r′(t), acceleration a(t) = v′(t). Speed is the scalar magnitude |v(t)| = √{[x′(t)]² + [y′(t)]²}, distinct from the velocity vector itself.

Polar coordinates

A point (r, θ) is r units from the pole at angle θ from the positive x-axis. Converts to rectangular via x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ — a parametric pair with θ as the parameter.

Area of one polar region

A = (1/2)∫ₐᵇ [f(θ)]² dθ — summing thin circular wedges of area (1/2)r²dθ, the fraction dθ/2π of a full circle's area πr².

Area between two polar curves

A = (1/2)∫ₐᵇ ([f(θ)]² − [g(θ)]²) dθ, where a and b are the θ-values at which the outer curve f and inner curve g intersect — outer wedge minus inner wedge.

Infinite Sequences and Series

Partial sum, convergent & divergent series

For Σaₙ, the nth partial sum Sₙ = a₁ + a₂ + ... + aₙ is a plain finite sum. The series converges to L if limₙ→∞ Sₙ = L (finite); otherwise it diverges.

Geometric series

Σ (from n=0) a·rⁿ converges to a/(1−r) when |r| < 1, and diverges when |r| ≥ 1 — one of the few series types with a known exact sum.

nth Term (Divergence) Test

If limₙ→∞ aₙ ≠ 0 or doesn't exist, Σaₙ diverges. If limₙ→∞ aₙ = 0, the test is inconclusive — it never proves convergence.

Integral Test

If f is positive, continuous, and decreasing for x ≥ 1 with aₙ = f(n), then Σaₙ and ∫₁^∞ f(x) dx share the same convergence behavior.

p-series

Σ (from n=1) 1/nᵖ converges when p > 1 and diverges when p ≤ 1. The harmonic series Σ1/n is the borderline case p = 1, which diverges.

Direct Comparison Test

If 0 ≤ aₙ ≤ bₙ, then Σbₙ converges ⟹ Σaₙ converges, and Σaₙ diverges ⟹ Σbₙ diverges — a series is squeezed under, or dragged past, a known reference series.

Limit Comparison Test

If aₙ, bₙ > 0 and limₙ→∞ (aₙ/bₙ) is a finite positive number, Σaₙ and Σbₙ either both converge or both diverge — avoids the inequality bookkeeping of direct comparison.

Alternating series & the Alternating Series Test

Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹bₙ with bₙ > 0 strictly alternates sign. It converges if bₙ is eventually decreasing and limₙ→∞ bₙ = 0 — sign-flipping, not size alone, drives convergence.

Absolute vs. conditional convergence

Σaₙ converges absolutely if Σ|aₙ| converges (a stronger statement). It converges conditionally if Σaₙ converges but Σ|aₙ| diverges — the alternating harmonic series is the standard example.

Ratio Test

Let L = limₙ→∞ |aₙ₊₁/aₙ|. L < 1 ⟹ absolute convergence; L > 1 (or ∞) ⟹ divergence; L = 1 is inconclusive. The standard tool whenever factorials or exponentials appear.

Alternating Series Error Bound

If Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹bₙ converges to L by the alternating series test, |L − Sₙ| ≤ bₙ₊₁ — the first omitted term caps the error, no infinite tail required.

Taylor polynomial, Pₙ(x)

Pₙ(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x−a) + f″(a)(x−a)²/2! + ... + f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)(x−a)ⁿ/n! — the degree-n polynomial matching f and its first n derivatives at x = a.

Lagrange Error Bound

|Rₙ(x)| ≤ M/(n+1)! · |x−a|ⁿ⁺¹, where M bounds |f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(c)| between a and x — caps a Taylor polynomial's error without computing f(x) exactly.

Power series & radius of convergence

Σ (from n=0) cₙ(x−a)ⁿ converges absolutely for |x−a| < R and diverges for |x−a| > R, where R is found by applying the ratio test.

Interval of convergence

The radius R settles only the open interval (a−R, a+R) — each endpoint x = a±R must be checked separately with a numeric convergence test, since the ratio test is always inconclusive exactly there.

Taylor series, Maclaurin series

The infinite series Σ (from n=0) f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n! · (x−a)ⁿ — Pₙ(x) with n → ∞. Centered at a = 0, it's called a Maclaurin series. Converges to f(x) exactly where the Lagrange remainder → 0.

Representing functions as power series

New series are often built from a known one by substitution (e.g. −x² for x in 1/(1−x) = Σxⁿ) or by differentiating/integrating a known series term by term — both preserve the radius of convergence.
