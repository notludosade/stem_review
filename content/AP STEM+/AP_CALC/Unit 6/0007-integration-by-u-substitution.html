Integration by u-Substitution

Lesson 6's table only matches an integrand exactly. u-substitution is the Chain Rule run backward — it turns a composed, messier integral into one the table already knows.

← Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

1. The method

Method — u-Substitution Given ∫ f(g(x)) g′(x) dx, let u = g(x), so du = g′(x) dx. Rewriting the whole integral in terms of u turns it into ∫ f(u) du — no more x anywhere. Integrate normally in u, then substitute u = g(x) back in at the end.
Worked Example ∫ 2x(x² + 1)⁴ dx: let u = x² + 1, so du = 2x dx — exactly the factor sitting outside the parentheses. The integral becomes ∫ u⁴ du = u⁵/5 + C, and substituting back gives (x² + 1)⁵/5 + C.
Fact — Changing the Bounds of a Definite Integral For a definite integral, converting the bounds along with the variable avoids ever substituting back: ∫01 2x(x² + 1)⁴ dx with u = x² + 1 has x = 0 → u = 1 and x = 1 → u = 2, so the integral becomes ∫12 u⁴ du = [u⁵/5]12 = (32 − 1)/5 = 31/5. Forgetting to change the bounds — and instead plugging the old x-bounds into a u-expression — is one of the most common u-substitution errors on the AP exam.
