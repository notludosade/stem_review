The Derivative as a Limit

Shrinking the secant line's two points into one — and turning "average" into "exact, right now."

← Unit 1, Lesson 6 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

The average rate of change between two points is a secant line's slope — it tells you how much a function changed on average over an interval. The derivative asks a sharper question: how fast is the function changing at one single instant? Unit 1's whole toolkit — limits — is exactly what's needed to make that question precise.

1. Shrinking the secant into a tangent

Recall The average rate of change of f from a to a + h is the secant slope: [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h. As h shrinks toward 0, the second point slides along the curve toward the first — and the secant line rotates toward whatever line the graph is instantaneously heading in at a.

Drag point B along f(x) = x² and watch the secant line through A (fixed at x = 1) swing as h = xB − 1 shrinks.

Try It — Secant Sliding Into a Tangent

—

A stays fixed at (1, 1). Drag B — the gray secant line is the average rate of change from A to B; watch its slope as h shrinks.

Why it works A secant line only needs two points, so it can always be drawn — but it describes the interval between them, not any single instant. Sliding B toward A removes the interval entirely: in the limit, the "secant" touches the curve at just one point and matches the direction the curve is actually moving there. That limiting line is the tangent line, and its slope is the derivative.

2. The formal definition

Definition The derivative of f at x = a is f′(a) = limh→0 [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h, provided this limit exists. It equals the slope of the tangent line to f at a, and it is often read as "the instantaneous rate of change of f at a."

This is exactly a limit like Unit 1's — just with a slope formula standing in for f(x). Watch it numerically: for f(x) = x² at a = 1, the difference quotient is a function of h alone. Step h down toward 0 from both sides and see where the slope value lands.

1
