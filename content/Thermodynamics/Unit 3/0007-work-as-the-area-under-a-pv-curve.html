Work as the Area Under a PV Curve

The PV diagram from Lesson 6 was quietly doing double duty all along — the same picture that shows a gas's state also, it turns out, encodes the exact work a process performs.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Work as an Integral, W = ∫P dV For a gas whose boundary moves by a small volume dV against pressure P, the work done by the gas is dW = P dV. Summed over an entire process from V₁ to V₂: W = ∫V₁V₂ P dV — geometrically, the area between the process's path on a PV diagram and the V-axis. For an isobaric process (P constant), this collapses to W = PΔV. For an isochoric process (V constant, dV = 0 everywhere), W = 0 — no matter how much the pressure changes.
Method — Work for an Isothermal Process On an isothermal path, P isn't constant — it varies as P = nRT/V. The integral becomes W = ∫nRT/V dV = nRT ∫(1/V) dV = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) (T factors out of the integral because it's fixed along an isothermal path). This is the first place in the course an actual antiderivative — not just multiplication — is required to get the work right.
Why W Depends on the Path, Not Just the Endpoints Two different paths connecting the same starting and ending PV points enclose different areas underneath them — so W genuinely depends on which path was taken, confirming Lesson 2's claim that work is a process quantity, not a state quantity. This is the geometric heart of why ΔU (a state function) can stay fixed between two states while Q and W individually vary between different processes connecting them.
Worked Example — Isobaric Expansion A gas expands at a constant pressure P = 2×10⁵ Pa from V₁ = 0.01 m³ to V₂ = 0.04 m³. Find the work done by the gas.
W = PΔV = (2×10⁵)(0.04 − 0.01) = (2×10⁵)(0.03) = 6000 J.
