Unit 1: Temperature, Heat, and the Zeroth Law

Thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law

Two systems in contact are in thermal equilibrium when no net heat flows between them — equivalently, they share the same temperature. The zeroth law: if A is in thermal equilibrium with C, and B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other, even without direct contact — the reason a thermometer works.

Temperature scales, K = °C + 273.15, °F = (9/5)°C + 32

Kelvin is the SI absolute scale (0 K at absolute zero) used in every physics equation in this course; Celsius and Fahrenheit are offset, non-absolute scales. Ratios of temperature (as in PV = nRT) are only physically meaningful in Kelvin.

Internal energy U, heat Q, and work W

U is the sum of a system's microscopic kinetic and potential energy — a state function the system "has." Q is energy transferred due to a temperature difference; W is energy transferred by any other means (typically a moving boundary). Sign convention: Q > 0 into the system, W > 0 done by the system on its surroundings. Q and W are process quantities (path-dependent); U is a state quantity.

Conduction and Fourier's law, Q̇ = kAΔT/L

Heat transfer through direct molecular contact, with no bulk motion of the material. k is thermal conductivity, A is cross-sectional area, ΔT the temperature difference, L the thickness in the direction of flow. Q̇ is a rate (W), not an energy.

Convection and radiation

Convection transfers heat via bulk fluid motion (natural, from density differences, or forced, from a fan/pump) — requires a fluid medium. Radiation transfers heat via electromagnetic waves, needs no medium, and is the only mechanism that crosses a vacuum; radiated power scales with T⁴ (qualitatively, in this course).

Unit 2: The First Law of Thermodynamics

First law, ΔU = Q − W

Conservation of energy for a system exchanging heat and work with its surroundings. The minus sign on W reflects that work done by the system leaves as energy pushed outward, while heat flowing in adds directly to U.

Specific heat, Q = mcΔT

The heat needed to change a mass m by ΔT within a single phase, where c (J/(kg·K)) is the material's specific heat capacity.

Latent heat, Q = mL

The heat needed to change a mass m's phase at constant temperature, where L (J/kg) is L_f (fusion) or L_v (vaporization). All added heat goes into rearranging intermolecular bonds, none into raising temperature — why temperature plateaus during a phase change.

Calorimetry

In an isolated mixture, the heat lost by a hotter substance equals the heat gained by a colder one (Q_lost = Q_gained) — the first law applied to a closed system, used to solve for an equilibrium temperature.

Ideal gas law, PV = nRT

Relates pressure, volume, moles, and absolute temperature (R ≈ 8.314 J/(mol·K)), assuming negligible particle volume and no intermolecular forces.

Isobaric, isochoric, and isothermal processes

Holding P, V, or T fixed respectively while a gas changes state, tracing a horizontal line, vertical line, or P ∝ 1/V hyperbola on a PV diagram.

Unit 3: Work and PV Diagrams

Work as an integral, W = ∫P dV

The area between a process's path on a PV diagram and the volume axis. Isobaric: W = PΔV. Isochoric: W = 0. Isothermal: W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁). W is path-dependent, confirming it as a process quantity.

Adiabatic process, PVᵞ = constant

A process with Q = 0 throughout. γ = C_P/C_V (about 5/3 monatomic, 7/5 diatomic). Because γ > 1, an adiabatic curve is steeper than an isothermal curve through the same point — adiabatic compression heats a gas more than isothermal compression, since no energy can escape as heat.

Cyclic process and net work

A sequence of processes returning to the exact starting state, forcing ΔU = 0 and therefore Q_net = W_net. Net work equals the area enclosed by the loop on a PV diagram — positive (done by the gas) if traversed clockwise, negative (done on the gas) if counterclockwise.

Unit 4: Entropy and the Second Law

Second law: Kelvin and Clausius statements

Kelvin: no cyclic engine converts a single reservoir's heat entirely into work. Clausius: heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot with no other effect. The two are logically equivalent — violating either lets you build a device violating the other.

Reversible vs. irreversible processes

A reversible process is quasi-static and dissipation-free, exactly reversible with no lasting trace — an idealized limit. Irreversible processes (friction, free expansion, finite-ΔT heat flow) cannot be undone without a permanent change elsewhere. No real process is perfectly reversible.

Entropy, ΔS = ∫dQ_rev/T

A state function computed by integrating along any convenient reversible path between two states, even for a process that actually happened irreversibly. Isothermal: ΔS = Q/T. Heating at constant c: ΔS = mc ln(T₂/T₁). For an isolated system, ΔS_total ≥ 0 — a restatement of the second law.

Microstates, macrostates, and Boltzmann entropy, S = k_B ln Ω

A macrostate is what's measurable at large scale; Ω counts the microstates (exact configurations) consistent with it. Higher-Ω macrostates are more probable, which is why entropy tends to increase — a statistical near-certainty, not an absolute mechanical law.

Arrow of time

Microscopic physical laws are time-symmetric, but the overwhelming statistical dominance of high-Ω macrostates gives macroscopic entropy a strongly preferred direction — the only physical quantity that picks out a direction for time.

Unit 5: Heat Engines and Refrigerators

Heat engine efficiency, e = W/Q_h = 1 − Q_c/Q_h

The fraction of absorbed heat converted to useful work. Always strictly less than 1, since the Kelvin statement forbids Q_c = 0.

Carnot cycle and Carnot efficiency, e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h

A four-leg reversible cycle setting the theoretical maximum efficiency between two reservoirs (T in Kelvin), independent of the working substance or engine design — Carnot's theorem. Real engines always fall at or below this ceiling.

Coefficient of performance, COP_R = Q_c/W, COP_HP = Q_h/W

A refrigerator's COP_R measures cold-space heat removed per unit of work; a heat pump's COP_HP measures heat delivered per unit of work. Related by COP_HP = COP_R + 1. Unlike efficiency, COP routinely exceeds 1 — it measures heat moved per work spent, not work out per heat in.

Unit 6: Real Gases and Applications

Van der Waals equation, (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT

Corrects the ideal gas law for real-gas behavior: b subtracts the volume molecules physically occupy; an²/V² adds back pressure lost to intermolecular attraction softening wall impacts. Deviations from ideal behavior are strongest at high pressure and low temperature. Setting a = b = 0 recovers PV = nRT exactly.

Phase diagram, triple point, and critical point

A P-T plot divided into solid, liquid, and gas regions by the fusion, vaporization, and sublimation curves. At the triple point, all three phases coexist. Past the critical point, the liquid-gas distinction disappears entirely (supercritical fluid).

Heating curve

A plot of temperature against heat added at constant pressure. Ramps (within one phase) follow Q = mcΔT; flat plateaus (during a phase transition) follow Q = mL with zero temperature change — the same latent-heat fact from Unit 2, now placed on one continuous curve.

Why no real engine is 100% efficient

Three independent layers: e = 1 is directly forbidden by the Kelvin statement; even the ideal (Carnot) case is capped below 1 by e_Carnot = 1 − T_c/T_h; and real irreversibility (ΔS_total > 0) pushes actual engines below even that ceiling. None of the three is an engineering shortfall correctable with better materials.
