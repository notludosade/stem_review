Entropy as an Integral

Lesson 10's second law was qualitative — this direction allowed, that one forbidden. Entropy turns that qualitative rule into a state function you can actually compute.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Entropy, ΔS = ∫dQ/T ΔS = ∫dQ_rev/T, integrated along a reversible path between the initial and final states. Entropy S is a state function — like U, its change depends only on the endpoints, not the path — so even for an irreversible process, ΔS is computed by inventing any convenient reversible path connecting the same two states and integrating along that instead. For an isothermal process (T constant), this collapses to ΔS = Q/T. For heating a mass m with constant specific heat c from T₁ to T₂, it becomes ΔS = mc ln(T₂/T₁).
Method — Why "Reversible" Doesn't Mean "Unrealistic Answer" A gas freely expanding into a vacuum (Q = 0, irreversible, no reversible path literally followed) still has a well-defined ΔS > 0 — computed by instead imagining a slow, reversible isothermal expansion between the same start and end volumes, where heat actually would need to flow in to keep T constant. The real process and the imagined one share nothing physically except their endpoints — but because S is a state function, that's all that's needed for ΔS to come out identical either way.
Why ΔS ≥ 0 for an Isolated System Is the Second Law, Restated For any process in an isolated system, ΔS_total ≥ 0 — equal to zero only for the idealized reversible limit, strictly positive for every real, irreversible process. This single inequality turns out to be exactly equivalent to both the Kelvin and Clausius statements from Lesson 10, just written as a number instead of a sentence — which is why entropy, not heat or work, is the quantity Unit 5's engine-efficiency limits are ultimately built from.
Worked Example — Entropy Change of an Isothermal Process A gas absorbs Q = 500 J of heat during a reversible isothermal expansion at T = 300 K. Find ΔS.
ΔS = Q/T = 500/300 ≈ 1.67 J/K.

1. Why does ΔS = ∫dQ/T get integrated along a reversible path, even when computing ΔS for a process that actually happened irreversibly?

Because S depends only on the state, not the history, any convenient reversible path between the same two states gives the correct ΔS — even one that never physically happened.

2. Fill in the blank: a gas absorbs Q = 720 J of heat during a reversible isothermal process at T = 240 K. ΔS = ___ J/K.

ΔS = Q/T = 720/240 = 3 J/K.

3. If heat flows out of a system (Q < 0) during a reversible isothermal process, that system's entropy change ΔS is:

ΔS = Q/T carries the same sign as Q — heat leaving a system lowers that system's own entropy (even though the total entropy of system + surroundings still can't decrease, per Lesson 10's second law).

4. Fill in the blank: 2 kg of water (c ≈ 4186 J/(kg·K)) is heated from 293.15 K to 353.15 K. Using ΔS = mc ln(T₂/T₁), ΔS ≈ ___ J/K (nearest whole number, or one decimal).

ΔS = mc ln(T₂/T₁) = (2)(4186) ln(353.15/293.15) = 8372 × ln(1.2047) ≈ 8372 × 0.1863 ≈ 1559 J/K.
