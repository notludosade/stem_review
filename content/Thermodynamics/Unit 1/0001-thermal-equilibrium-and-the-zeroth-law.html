Thermal Equilibrium and the Zeroth Law

Before any engine, any entropy, any calculus — thermodynamics needs a working definition of temperature itself. That definition turns out to rest on a law so basic it was named after the other three were already numbered.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Thermal Equilibrium and the Zeroth Law Two systems placed in thermal contact are in thermal equilibrium when no net heat flows between them — equivalently, they share the same temperature. The zeroth law of thermodynamics states: if system A is in thermal equilibrium with system C, and system B is separately in thermal equilibrium with that same system C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other, even if A and B are never placed in contact.
Method — Converting Between Temperature Scales This course works in three scales: Kelvin (K), the SI absolute scale used in essentially every equation from here on, with 0 K at absolute zero; Celsius (°C), offset from Kelvin by a fixed constant; and Fahrenheit (°F), used only when a source states a temperature that way.
K = °C + 273.15  ·  °C = K − 273.15
°F = (9/5)°C + 32  ·  °C = (5/9)(°F − 32)
Why the Zeroth Law Comes First The zeroth law sounds obvious, but it's doing real work: it's the reason a thermometer (system C) can stand in for direct contact. Without it, "A and B have the same temperature" would only be verifiable by physically touching A to B — a thermometer lets you compare both to it instead, and trust that a matching reading means they'd agree with each other too. It was proven logically independent of the first, second, and third laws only after those were already named and numbered — hence "zeroth."
Worked Example — Converting a Furnace Reading A kiln thermometer reads 450°F. Convert to Celsius and Kelvin.
°C = (5/9)(450 − 32) = (5/9)(418) ≈ 232.22°C
K = 232.22 + 273.15 ≈ 505.37 K

1. Which statement correctly expresses the zeroth law of thermodynamics?

The zeroth law is specifically the transitive statement about thermal equilibrium — it's what lets a thermometer (playing the role of C) stand in for direct contact between A and B.

2. Why is the Kelvin scale used for essentially every physics equation involving temperature, rather than Celsius?

Laws like PV = nRT depend on T being measured from true zero energy, not from an arbitrary reference point like water's freezing point — only Kelvin does that, so "doubling T" is only meaningful in Kelvin.

3. Fill in the blank: 15°C expressed in Kelvin is ___ K.

K = °C + 273.15 = 15 + 273.15 = 288.15 K.

4. Two blocks are placed in contact and, after some time, no net heat flows between them anymore. What can you conclude?

Zero net heat flow between two systems in contact is the operational definition of thermal equilibrium — and thermal equilibrium is exactly what "same temperature" means.
