Cauchy Sequences

A way to test for convergence using only the sequence's own terms — no candidate limit required, ever.

← Lesson 12 Unit 3, Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Every convergence proof so far — even the Monotone Convergence Theorem — eventually needed to name a specific candidate limit (even if, as in Lesson 10, that candidate was supplied automatically as a supremum). Cauchy sequences give a test for convergence that never mentions a limit at all — phrased entirely in terms of how the sequence's own terms behave relative to each other.

Definition — Cauchy Sequence A sequence (aₙ) is Cauchy if: for every ε > 0, there exists N such that |aₘ − aₙ| < ε for all m, n > N.

Compare this to Lesson 7's convergence definition directly: instead of every term eventually landing near a fixed L, every pair of terms (past some point) eventually lands near each other. No L appears anywhere in the definition.

Convergent implies Cauchy

Theorem Every convergent sequence is Cauchy.
Proof Suppose lim aₙ = L. Let ε > 0. There exists N such that |aₙ − L| < ε/2 for all n > N. Then for any m, n > N: |aₘ − aₙ| = |(aₘ − L) + (L − aₙ)| ≤ |aₘ − L| + |aₙ − L| < ε/2 + ε/2 = ε (using the triangle inequality, exactly as in Lesson 9's sum rule). So (aₙ) is Cauchy. ∎

The converse — and why it's the genuinely hard direction

Theorem — Cauchy Criterion Every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges (to some real number).
Proof Let (aₙ) be Cauchy. Step 1 — Cauchy implies bounded: taking ε = 1 in the Cauchy definition gives some N with |aₘ − aₙ| < 1 for all m, n > N; in particular |aₙ − a_{N+1}| < 1 for all n > N, so all terms past N are trapped in an interval of width 2 around a_{N+1} — and there are only finitely many terms before that, which are automatically bounded too. So (aₙ) is bounded. Step 2 — apply Bolzano-Weierstrass: since (aₙ) is bounded, Lesson 12 guarantees a convergent subsequence (anₖ) → x for some real x. Step 3 — the whole sequence follows the subsequence: let ε > 0. Since (aₙ) is Cauchy, there is N with |aₘ − aₙ| < ε/2 for all m, n > N. Since anₖ → x, there is K such that |anₖ − x| < ε/2 for all k > K, and nₖ > N for k large enough (since nₖ → ∞). Fix such a k. Then for any n > N: |aₙ − x| ≤ |aₙ − anₖ| + |anₖ − x| < ε/2 + ε/2 = ε. So lim aₙ = x. ∎
Why this direction needed both completeness and Bolzano-Weierstrass "Cauchy implies convergent" is exactly where the whole unit's machinery pays off at once: boundedness (Step 1) feeds Bolzano-Weierstrass (Step 2, itself built on completeness via nested intervals), which produces a convergent subsequence that the full Cauchy sequence is then shown to chase down (Step 3). This theorem is false over ℚ alone — a Cauchy sequence of rationals (like Lesson 1's √2 approximations, which are Cauchy) can fail to converge to a rational number. So the Cauchy Criterion, like the Monotone Convergence Theorem, is really completeness in disguise.
Why this test is useful in practice The Cauchy Criterion lets you prove a sequence converges — very usefully, when it arises as a series' partial sums or a recursively-defined sequence — by checking only that its own terms bunch together, without ever having to guess or construct the limit itself first. This is often far easier than finding an explicit L to test against.

1. What is distinctive about the Cauchy sequence definition, compared to the ordinary convergence definition?

The defining feature of the Cauchy condition is comparing terms to each other, never to an external candidate limit.

2. In the proof that Cauchy implies convergent, what was step 1 used to establish?

Boundedness is exactly the missing ingredient Bolzano-Weierstrass needs — step 1 supplies it directly from the Cauchy condition.

3. Why does the fact that a Cauchy sequence of rationals can fail to converge in ℚ matter for this lesson?

This is the same gap from Unit 1 resurfacing — the Cauchy Criterion is another theorem that is, at its core, a restatement of completeness.

4. Fill in the blank: a sequence where |aₘ − aₙ| can be made arbitrarily small for all sufficiently large m and n is called a ___ sequence.

Cauchy is the term for this self-referential closeness condition, defined without any mention of a limit.

5. Fill in the blank: the proof that every Cauchy sequence converges relies on the ___ Theorem to extract a convergent subsequence once boundedness is established.

Bolzano-Weierstrass is the theorem from Lesson 12 that turns "bounded" into "has a convergent subsequence," the pivotal middle step.
