Inner Products, Norms, and Cauchy-Schwarz

Length and angle were always computed with the dot product in Linear Algebra A, without ever asking what makes the dot product special. This lesson generalizes it — and proves the inequality every later "angle makes sense" argument in this course depends on.

← Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Linear Algebra A used u·v = u₁v₁+⋯+uₙvₙ freely for length and angle, but never asked what properties of that formula actually make "length" and "angle" behave sensibly. This lesson names those properties as axioms, generalizes past the dot product, and proves the inequality that keeps "angle" well-defined at all.

1. Inner products, generalized

Definition — Inner Product (Real Vector Space) An inner product on a real vector space V is a function ⟨·,·⟩: V × V → ℝ satisfying, for all u, v, w ∈ V and c ∈ ℝ: symmetry ⟨u,v⟩ = ⟨v,u⟩; bilinearity ⟨u+w,v⟩ = ⟨u,v⟩+⟨w,v⟩ and ⟨cu,v⟩ = c⟨u,v⟩; and positive-definiteness ⟨v,v⟩ ≥ 0, with equality only when v = 0. The norm of v is ‖v‖ = √⟨v,v⟩.
Inner Products Beyond the Dot Product (a) The standard dot product on ℝⁿ, ⟨u,v⟩ = u₁v₁+⋯+uₙvₙ, is one inner product among many — not the only possible one. (b) On C[0,1], ⟨f,g⟩ = ∫₀¹f(x)g(x)dx satisfies all four properties, using properties of the integral instead of finite sums. (c) On P₂(ℝ), ⟨p,q⟩ = p(−1)q(−1) + p(0)q(0) + p(1)q(1) (evaluating and multiplying at three fixed points) is also a valid inner product.
Remark — Complex Inner Products Over ℂ, "symmetry" is relaxed to conjugate symmetry, ⟨u,v⟩ = ⟨v,u⟩, so that ⟨v,v⟩ stays real and positive-definiteness still makes sense. This adjustment is what Unit 6 needs for Hermitian operators and complex spectral theory — everything proved below for ℝ carries over with this one change.

2. The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Theorem — Cauchy-Schwarz For any u, v in an inner product space: |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖, with equality if and only if u, v are linearly dependent.
Proof If v = 0, both sides are 0 and the inequality holds trivially (with u, v dependent, since {0} is always dependent — Lesson 10 of Linear Algebra A). Assume v ≠ 0. For any real t, positive-definiteness gives 0 ≤ ⟨u−tv, u−tv⟩. Expanding using bilinearity and symmetry: 0 ≤ ‖u‖² − 2t⟨u,v⟩ + t²‖v‖². This is a quadratic in t that is never negative, so its discriminant cannot be positive: (2⟨u,v⟩)² − 4‖v‖²‖u‖² ≤ 0, i.e. ⟨u,v⟩² ≤ ‖u‖²‖v‖². Taking square roots: |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖.

Equality holds exactly when the discriminant is 0, i.e. the quadratic has a real root t₀ — meaning ⟨u−t₀v, u−t₀v⟩ = 0, which by positive-definiteness forces u−t₀v = 0, i.e. u = t₀v. That's exactly u, v being linearly dependent. ∎
Why this is what makes "angle" well-defined Cosine of the angle between u and v is defined as ⟨u,v⟩/(‖u‖‖v‖) — and that formula only ever produces a value in [−1,1], which cos requires, precisely because Cauchy-Schwarz bounds |⟨u,v⟩| by ‖u‖‖v‖. Without this theorem, "angle" in a general inner product space wouldn't even be a coherent definition.
Corollary — The Triangle Inequality ‖u+v‖ ≤ ‖u‖+‖v‖.
Proof ‖u+v‖² = ⟨u+v,u+v⟩ = ‖u‖² + 2⟨u,v⟩ + ‖v‖² ≤ ‖u‖² + 2‖u‖‖v‖ + ‖v‖² = (‖u‖+‖v‖)², using Cauchy-Schwarz (⟨u,v⟩ ≤ |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖) in the middle step. Taking square roots of both sides (both nonnegative) gives ‖u+v‖ ≤ ‖u‖+‖v‖. ∎
Worked Example — Checking Cauchy-Schwarz Numerically In ℝ³ with the dot product, let u = (1,2,2) and v = (2,2,1). ⟨u,v⟩ = 1(2)+2(2)+2(1) = 2+4+2 = 8. ‖u‖ = √(1+4+4) = √9 = 3, and ‖v‖ = √(4+4+1) = √9 = 3. Cauchy-Schwarz predicts |8| ≤ 3·3 = 9 — true, and not an equality, confirming u and v are not parallel (indeed, (1,2,2) is not a scalar multiple of (2,2,1)).

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which of the following is one of the four inner product axioms?

Positive-definiteness is one of the four defining axioms; the other options aren't part of the definition at all — norms need not be 1, and ⟨u,v⟩ can be negative or non-integer.

2. In the Cauchy-Schwarz proof, why must the quadratic 0 ≤ ‖u‖² − 2t⟨u,v⟩ + t²‖v‖² have a nonpositive discriminant?

A quadratic with positive leading coefficient and a positive discriminant necessarily dips negative between its two roots — which would contradict the quadratic being ⟨u−tv,u−tv⟩, always ≥ 0 by positive-definiteness.

3. Fill in the blank: equality in Cauchy-Schwarz, |⟨u,v⟩| = ‖u‖‖v‖, holds exactly when u and v are linearly ___.

A zero discriminant means u = t₀v for some scalar t₀ — exactly linear dependence.

4. In the Triangle Inequality's proof, where specifically is Cauchy-Schwarz used?

Replacing the cross term 2⟨u,v⟩ with its Cauchy-Schwarz upper bound 2‖u‖‖v‖ is exactly the step that turns ‖u+v‖² into (‖u‖+‖v‖)² as an upper bound.

5. For u=(1,2,2), v=(2,2,1) in ℝ³, ‖u‖=‖v‖=3. Cauchy-Schwarz predicts |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ___.

‖u‖‖v‖ = 3×3 = 9, and indeed ⟨u,v⟩=8 ≤ 9.
