Jordan Canonical Form

Lesson 19's chains inside a nilpotent operator turn out to be exactly the right basis: ordered correctly, each chain becomes one block of a matrix that's the closest thing to diagonal every operator is guaranteed to have.

← Lesson 19 Lesson 21 → Contents Glossary →

1. The Jordan block

Definition — Jordan Block The Jordan block Jk(λ) is the k×k matrix with λ on every diagonal entry, 1 on every entry directly above the diagonal, and 0 everywhere else. (J₁(λ) = [λ], a single entry; J₂(λ) = [[λ,1],[0,λ]]; and so on.)

2. The theorem

Theorem — Jordan Canonical Form If T's characteristic polynomial splits into linear factors over F (always true over ℂ), then V has a basis relative to which [T] is block diagonal, with every block a Jordan block. The blocks for eigenvalue λ have sizes summing to λ's algebraic multiplicity, and this matrix — the Jordan canonical form of T — is unique up to reordering the blocks.

This uses two facts stated here without full proof (both genuine theorems, just beyond this course's scope to prove completely): first, that V decomposes as a direct sum V = Kλ₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ Kλm of all the generalized eigenspaces — the natural extension of Lesson 11's "eigenspace sums are direct" lemma to generalized eigenspaces; and second, that every nilpotent operator's space decomposes into a direct sum of chains like Lesson 19's. What is fully provable, and is the actual content of this lesson, is exactly what a single chain contributes once you have one.

3. From a chain to a Jordan block

Theorem — A Chain Produces a Jordan Block Let N = (T−λI)|Kλ, and let {v, Nv, …, Nk−1v} be a chain with Nk−1v≠0, Nkv=0 (Lesson 19). Order the basis w₁=Nk−1v, w₂=Nk−2v, …, wk=v (from the "top" of the chain down to v itself). Relative to (w₁,…,wk), T acts exactly as the Jordan block Jk(λ).
Proof For i ≥ 2: T(wi) = T(Nk−iv) = (N+λI)(Nk−iv) = Nk−i+1v + λNk−iv = wi−1 + λwi (using T=N+λI on Kλ, Lesson 19). For i=1: T(w₁) = T(Nk−1v) = Nkv + λNk−1v = 0 + λw₁ = λw₁ (using Nkv=0). So column i of [T] in this basis is: λ in row i, 1 in row i−1 (for i≥2), 0 elsewhere — exactly Jk(λ)'s columns. ∎
Why the chain has to be read top-down, not bottom-up w₁ = Nk−1v is the vector N finally kills — it's a genuine eigenvector (T(w₁)=λw₁ exactly, no leftover term), which is why it has to occupy the "first" position with nothing feeding into it. Every other wi gets sent to λwi plus the vector one step closer to being killed, wi−1 — which is exactly the single superdiagonal 1 in Jk(λ).
Worked Example — Recovering a Jordan Block from a Scrambled Basis Let C = [[1,1],[−1,3]]. Characteristic polynomial: trace=4, det=1(3)−1(−1)=4, so λ²−4λ+4=(λ−2)², a repeated eigenvalue λ=2. (C−2I) = [[−1,1],[−1,1]]; solving (C−2I)v=0 gives only v=(1,1) (up to scaling) — a defective eigenspace, dim(E₂)=1 < algebraic multiplicity 2.

N=(C−2I) is nilpotent: N² = [[−1,1],[−1,1]]² = [[0,0],[0,0]] (direct computation), so K₂=ℝ². Build a chain: try v=(1,0). Nv = (C−2I)(1,0) = (−1,−1) ≠ 0, and N²v=0 (confirmed above). So {(1,0), (−1,−1)} is a chain, with w₁=Nv=(−1,−1) and w₂=v=(1,0).

P = [w₁ w₂] = [[−1,1],[−1,0]]. Direct computation confirms CP = P·[[2,1],[0,2]] (both sides equal [[−2,1],[−2,−1]]), so P⁻¹CP = [[2,1],[0,2]] = J₂(2) — the Jordan canonical form of C, recovered entirely from one chain.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof that a chain produces a Jordan block, why does T(w₁)=λw₁ exactly, with no extra term?

N^k=0 is exactly what makes Nw₁ vanish, leaving T(w₁) with only its λw₁ term — the one genuine eigenvector inside the chain.

2. What forces the two facts cited without full proof (V=⊕K_λ and every nilpotent decomposes into chains) to be labeled that way in this lesson?

Both facts are genuine, provable theorems — they're just more involved than what this course's proof toolkit (built through Lesson 19) covers in full generality.

3. Fill in the blank: in Jk(λ), every entry directly above the diagonal is ___.

The superdiagonal entries are always exactly 1 in a Jordan block — that's what encodes each w_{i-1}=N(w_i) relationship.

4. For C=[[1,1],[−1,3]], what is dim(E₂), the ordinary eigenspace for λ=2?

(C−2I)=[[−1,1],[−1,1]] has a 1-dimensional solution set, confirming C is defective — exactly why a Jordan block (not a diagonal matrix) is needed.

5. In the worked example, w₁=Nv for v=(1,0), giving w₁=(−1,−1). Fill in the blank: since w₁ is a genuine eigenvector, Cw₁ should equal 2w₁ = (−2, ___).

2w₁ = 2(−1,−1) = (−2,−2), so the second coordinate is −2. (Check directly: C(−1,−1) = (1(−1)+1(−1), −1(−1)+3(−1)) = (−2,−2). ✓)
