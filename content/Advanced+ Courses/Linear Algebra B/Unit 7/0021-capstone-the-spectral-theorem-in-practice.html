Capstone — The Spectral Theorem in Practice

Every proof in this course was building toward one guarantee: a symmetric matrix always diagonalizes, orthogonally. This closing lesson uses that guarantee to classify quadratic forms outright, and reconnects it to PCA — a fact Linear Algebra A used without ever proving it worked.

← Lesson 20 Contents Glossary →

This closing lesson adds one new tool — the quadratic form — and no new machinery beyond it: everything below is Lesson 17's Spectral Theorem, applied.

1. Quadratic forms, diagonalized

Definition — Quadratic Form A quadratic form on ℝⁿ is a function Q(x) = xᵀAx for a symmetric matrix A. Expanded, Q(x) = Σᵢaᵢᵢxᵢ² + Σᵢ≠ⱼaᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ — every pure-quadratic expression in n variables can be written this way, uniquely, with A symmetric.
Theorem — The Principal Axis Theorem For Q(x)=xᵀAx with spectral decomposition A=QDQᵀ (eigenvalues λ₁,…,λn), the substitution y=Qᵀx turns Q into Q = λ₁y₁²+⋯+λnyn² — a pure sum of squares, with no cross terms at all.
Proof Q(x) = xᵀAx = xᵀ(QDQᵀ)x = (Qᵀx)ᵀD(Qᵀx), using (QDQᵀ)=A and regrouping (Qᵀx)ᵀ=xᵀQ. Setting y=Qᵀx: Q = yᵀDy = Σλᵢyᵢ², since D is diagonal. ∎
Why this is the Spectral Theorem doing real work Without a guaranteed orthogonal Q, "diagonalize the cross terms away" would be just a hope — some quadratic forms might resist it entirely, the same way Lesson 11's defective matrix resisted ordinary diagonalization. Because A is always symmetric here, Lesson 17 guarantees Q exists, is orthogonal, and this substitution always works, for every quadratic form, with no exceptions.
Worked Example — Classifying Q(x₁,x₂) = 5x₁² + 4x₁x₂ + 5x₂² This is xᵀAx for A=[[5,2],[2,5]] — Lesson 16's running example, with eigenvalues 7 and 3 (both positive) and orthonormal eigenvectors (1/√2,1/√2), (1/√2,−1/√2). In principal-axis coordinates, Q = 7y₁² + 3y₂². Since every coefficient is positive, Q(x) > 0 for every x≠0 — Q is positive definite. (In general: Q is positive definite exactly when every eigenvalue of A is positive, since a sum of positive multiples of squares can only be 0 when every yᵢ=0, i.e. y=0, i.e. x=0.)

2. Reconnecting to PCA

Linear Algebra A's Lesson 21 introduced Principal Component Analysis — "projection onto covariance eigenvectors ordered by captured variance" — as a recipe, without ever explaining why the covariance matrix's eigenvectors behave so well. This course now can: a covariance matrix Σ is always symmetric (Σᵢⱼ = Σⱼᵢ, since covariance itself is symmetric in its two variables), so the Spectral Theorem applies directly. Its eigenvalues are the variances captured along each principal axis — literally the λᵢ's in the Principal Axis Theorem above, applied to the quadratic form Q(x)=xᵀΣx measuring variance in direction x. The eigenvectors are orthonormal for free (Lesson 16), which is exactly why principal components never overlap or need re-orthogonalizing by hand. PCA was never a heuristic — it was the Spectral Theorem the entire time.

Looking Ahead — Quantum Computing The next course in this sequence, Quantum Computing, picks up exactly here. A qubit's state lives in a complex inner product space; quantum gates are unitary operators — the complex analogue of the orthogonal matrices built throughout Unit 6; and every measurement outcome comes directly from the spectral decomposition of a Hermitian operator: its real eigenvalues (Lesson 16) are the only possible measured values, and its orthonormal eigenvectors are the possible states the system collapses into. The Spectral Theorem, stated in Lesson 17 for real symmetric matrices but remarked there to hold identically for complex Hermitian ones, is not background material for that course — it is the operating principle every measurement in it runs on.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the Principal Axis Theorem's proof, what substitution turns xᵀAx into a pure sum of squares?

Substituting y=Qᵀx (equivalently, changing to eigenvector coordinates) is exactly what turns (Qᵀx)ᵀD(Qᵀx) into yᵀDy, a pure sum of squares.

2. For Q(x₁,x₂)=5x₁²+4x₁x₂+5x₂², what does the Principal Axis Theorem give in principal-axis coordinates?

The eigenvalues of A=[[5,2],[2,5]] are 7 and 3 (Lesson 16), and the Principal Axis Theorem replaces the cross-term expression with exactly those eigenvalues as coefficients of the squared new coordinates.

3. Fill in the blank: a quadratic form whose every eigenvalue is positive is called ___.

Positive definiteness of a quadratic form corresponds exactly to every eigenvalue of its symmetric matrix being positive.

4. Why is a covariance matrix always symmetric?

Covariance is symmetric in its two variables by definition, for any data distribution, which is exactly what forces Σᵢⱼ=Σⱼᵢ — no distributional assumption required.

5. Fill in the blank: Quantum Computing needs the Spectral Theorem's complex-numbers version, for ___ operators — the complex analogue of a symmetric real matrix.

Hermitian is exactly the complex generalization of symmetric (A=A*, conjugate transpose, instead of A=Aᵀ), and Lesson 16 remarked that every proof in Unit 6 carries over to it unchanged.
