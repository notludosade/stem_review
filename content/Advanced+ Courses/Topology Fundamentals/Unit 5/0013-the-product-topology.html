The Product Topology

Building a topology on X × Y out of the topologies on X and Y separately — and confirming it reproduces ℝ² exactly as expected.

← Unit 4, Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 11 used [−M, M]ⁿ, a product of intervals, without ever specifying which topology it carried. This lesson supplies that missing piece: given topological spaces X and Y, there is a canonical way to topologize X × Y — and, reassuringly, applying it to ℝ × ℝ reproduces the standard topology on ℝ² exactly.

The definition

Definition — Product Topology Let (X, τX) and (Y, τY) be topological spaces. The product topology on X × Y is the topology generated (Lesson 2) by the basis ℬ = {U × V : U ∈ τX, V ∈ τY}.
Theorem — ℬ Is a Valid Basis ℬ satisfies basis axioms (B1) and (B2), so Lesson 2's theorem guarantees it generates a genuine topology.
Proof (B1): for (x, y) ∈ X × Y, X ∈ τX and Y ∈ τY (axiom T1 for each), so X × Y ∈ ℬ contains (x,y). (B2): for (x,y) ∈ (U₁ × V₁) ∩ (U₂ × V₂) = (U₁ ∩ U₂) × (V₁ ∩ V₂), the set (U₁ ∩ U₂) × (V₁ ∩ V₂) is itself in ℬ (U₁ ∩ U₂ ∈ τX and V₁ ∩ V₂ ∈ τY by axiom T3 for each factor), and it contains (x,y) and sits inside the intersection — satisfying (B2) with B₃ equal to that same set exactly. ∎

This reproduces ℝ² exactly

Example — ℝ × ℝ = ℝ² with the Standard Topology Take X = Y = ℝ with τstd. The product topology's basis consists of sets (a, b) × (c, d) — open rectangles. Every open disk (ε-ball in ℝ², used implicitly throughout Real Analysis A and Lesson 8's topologist's sine curve) contains an open rectangle around any of its points, and every open rectangle contains an open disk around any of its points — so the topology generated by rectangles and the topology generated by disks agree (the same "different basis, same topology" argument as Lesson 2's rational-endpoint intervals). The product topology on ℝ × ℝ is exactly the familiar standard topology on ℝ².

Projections are continuous

Theorem The projection maps πX : X × Y → X, πX(x,y) = x, and πY : X × Y → Y, πY(x,y) = y, are both continuous.
Proof For U open in X, πX⁻¹(U) = U × Y, which is a basis element of the product topology (U ∈ τX, Y ∈ τY), hence open. So πX is continuous; πY is identical by symmetry. ∎
Why the product topology is defined this way, and not some other way The product topology is deliberately the smallest topology on X × Y making both projections continuous — no smaller collection of open sets could make πX⁻¹(U) = U × Y open for every open U, since that already forces every basis rectangle to be included. This "smallest topology making certain maps continuous" idea reappears, run in reverse, as the defining idea behind the quotient topology in Lesson 14.

Extending to finite products

The construction extends immediately to any finite product X₁ × X₂ × ⋯ × Xₙ, using boxes U₁ × U₂ × ⋯ × Uₙ as the basis. This is exactly the topology on ℝⁿ = ℝ × ℝ × ⋯ × ℝ (n factors) used implicitly throughout Lesson 11's Heine-Borel proof and its "finite product of compact spaces is compact" fact — a genuine theorem, whose proof (a short induction on n from the two-factor case, using this lesson's projection theorem) is left as a natural extension of the tools already assembled here. Infinite products need a different, more careful definition (the basis rectangles must be "eventually all of Xᵢ" in all but finitely many coordinates) and are not needed anywhere in this course.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What basis generates the product topology on X × Y?

Products (not unions) of open sets from each factor form the basis — "open rectangles," generalized to arbitrary spaces.

2. Why does the product topology on ℝ × ℝ agree exactly with the standard topology on ℝ²?

Two different bases can generate the same topology, and rectangles vs. disks is exactly such a case — the mutual-containment argument from Lesson 2 applies directly.

3. Why is πX⁻¹(U) = U × Y automatically a basis element of the product topology, for U open in X?

Y itself is always open in τ_Y by axiom (T1), so U × Y is a bona fide basis rectangle whenever U is open in X.

4. Fill in the blank: the product topology is the ___ topology on X × Y that makes both projection maps continuous.

Smallest — any topology making both projections continuous must already contain every basis rectangle, so the product topology is the minimal choice.

5. Fill in the blank: the basis for the product topology on ℝ × ℝ consists of open ___ (a,b) × (c,d), rather than open disks — though both generate the identical topology.

Rectangles are the natural basis for a product topology, though disks generate the same standard topology on ℝ².
