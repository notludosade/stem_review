The Quotient Topology

Gluing points of a space together, and topologizing the result — the machinery Lesson 15 uses to build a circle, a torus, and a Möbius strip out of nothing but a square.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Every construction so far in this course has taken an existing topological space and either restricted it (subspaces, Lesson 6) or combined two of them side by side (products, Lesson 13). The quotient topology does something new: it identifies points of a single space with each other — literally gluing them together — and asks what topology the resulting, smaller space should carry.

The definition

Definition — Quotient Map, Quotient Topology Let X be a topological space, Y a set, and q : X → Y a surjective function (typically Y = X/~, the set of equivalence classes of some equivalence relation ~ on X, with q sending each point to its class). The quotient topology on Y is: V ⊆ Y is open if and only if q⁻¹(V) is open in X. With this topology, q is called a quotient map.
Theorem — The Quotient Topology Is a Genuine Topology The collection τY = {V ⊆ Y : q⁻¹(V) open in X} satisfies (T1)–(T3).
Proof (T1): q⁻¹(∅) = ∅ and q⁻¹(Y) = X, both open in X, so ∅, Y ∈ τY. (T2): for {Vα} ⊆ τY, q⁻¹(⋃Vα) = ⋃q⁻¹(Vα), a union of open sets in X, hence open — so ⋃Vα ∈ τY. (T3): for V₁, V₂ ∈ τY, q⁻¹(V₁ ∩ V₂) = q⁻¹(V₁) ∩ q⁻¹(V₂), a finite intersection of open sets, hence open — so V₁ ∩ V₂ ∈ τY. Every step is the same preimage-commutes-with-set-operations fact used to prove continuity behaves well (Lesson 4) and the subspace topology is well-defined (Lesson 6). ∎
Why q is automatically continuous, by construction Unlike ordinary continuity, which has to be checked, the quotient topology is defined so that q⁻¹(V) is open exactly when V is declared open — so q : X → Y is automatically continuous, and in fact the quotient topology is the largest topology on Y for which q remains continuous. This is the mirror image of Lesson 13's observation that the product topology is the smallest topology making the projections continuous.

Worked example: identifying the endpoints of an interval

Example Let X = [0, 1] with the equivalence relation identifying 0 ~ 1 (every other point is only equivalent to itself). Let Y = X/~ and q : [0,1] → Y the quotient map. A set V ⊆ Y not containing the glued point [0]=[1] is open in Y exactly when its preimage (an ordinary subset of (0,1)) is open in [0,1] — an unsurprising case. A set V containing the glued point is open exactly when its preimage in [0,1] is an open set that contains both 0 and 1 (since q⁻¹ of the glued point is the two-point set {0, 1}) — meaning it must contain an interval [0, ε) around 0 and an interval (1−ε, 1] around 1 simultaneously. This is exactly the "wrap-around" openness a circle needs near the point where its two ends meet — a fact Lesson 15 makes precise.

A useful sufficient condition for a homeomorphism

Theorem If q : X → Y is a continuous surjection, X is compact, and Y is Hausdorff (Unit 6, Lesson 16 — informally, any two distinct points of Y can be separated by disjoint open sets), then q is a quotient map, and any continuous bijection built from a space homeomorphic to X onto Y is automatically a homeomorphism.
Why this is stated now, ahead of Unit 6 This fact — "continuous bijection from compact to Hausdorff is automatically a homeomorphism" — is exactly what will let Lesson 15 conclude that gluing the endpoints of [0,1] genuinely produces a space homeomorphic to the circle, without repeating Lesson 5's [0, 2π) → S¹ counterexample's failure. The difference is Lesson 5's map went from a non-compact space [0, 2π); [0,1] is compact, and that single change is enough to guarantee the inverse is automatically continuous too.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the quotient topology, when is V ⊆ Y declared open?

The quotient topology is defined entirely through preimages under q, exactly mirroring how continuity itself is defined.

2. Why is the quotient map q : X → Y automatically continuous, with no separate verification needed?

Continuity of q holds by construction, since the quotient topology's openness test is exactly the continuity condition applied to q itself.

3. In the [0,1]/(0~1) example, what must a set V containing the glued point [0]=[1] satisfy to be open in Y?

Since q⁻¹ of the glued point is the two-point set {0,1}, any open set around the glued point must pull back to a set open around both 0 and 1 at once — the "wrap-around" neighborhood a circle needs.

4. Fill in the blank: the quotient topology is the ___ topology on Y for which q remains continuous — the mirror image of the product topology being the smallest such topology for the projections.

Largest — any additional open sets beyond those in the quotient topology would make q⁻¹ of some "open" set fail to be open in X, breaking continuity.

5. Fill in the blank: the sufficient-condition theorem needs the domain X to be ___ and the codomain Y to be Hausdorff, to guarantee a continuous bijection is automatically a homeomorphism.

Compactness of the domain, together with the Hausdorff property of the codomain, is exactly what rules out the kind of inverse-discontinuity Lesson 5's [0,2π) → S¹ example exhibited.
