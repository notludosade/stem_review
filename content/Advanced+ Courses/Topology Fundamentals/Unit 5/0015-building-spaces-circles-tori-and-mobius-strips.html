Building Spaces — Circles, Tori, and Möbius Strips

Three familiar shapes, all constructed from nothing but a square (or an interval) and an instruction for which edges to glue.

← Lesson 14 Unit 6, Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

This closing lesson of Unit 5 puts Lesson 14's quotient topology to work: the circle, the torus (the surface of a donut), and the Möbius strip are all built by taking a simple shape — an interval or a square — and declaring certain edges identified. Which edges, and in which direction, is the entire difference between them.

The circle, formalized

Theorem — [0,1]/(0~1) ≅ S¹ Let X = [0, 1] with 0 ~ 1, Y = X/~ with the quotient topology, and S¹ ⊆ ℝ² the unit circle. Then Y ≅ S¹.
Proof sketch The map f(t) = (cos 2πt, sin 2πt) is continuous [0,1] → S¹ and satisfies f(0) = f(1), so it factors through the quotient as a continuous bijection f̄ : Y → S¹ (Lesson 14's quotient-map universal property: any continuous map constant on the glued points descends to a continuous map on the quotient). X = [0,1] is compact (Heine-Borel, Lesson 11), so Y = X/~ is compact too (continuous image of compact, Lesson 12, applied to the quotient map q). S¹ ⊆ ℝ² is Hausdorff (any two of its points are separated by disjoint open disks). By Lesson 14's compact-to-Hausdorff theorem, the continuous bijection f̄ is automatically a homeomorphism. ∎
Why this succeeds where Lesson 5's example failed Lesson 5's f : [0, 2π) → S¹ was a continuous bijection with a discontinuous inverse, precisely because [0, 2π) is not compact — the point 0 has no partner endpoint being glued to it within the domain itself. Here, the domain is the quotient Y = [0,1]/(0~1), which is compact by construction (built from the compact space [0,1]), and that one difference is exactly enough to invoke the compact-to-Hausdorff theorem and conclude a genuine homeomorphism.

Squares with identified edges

The same idea, applied to a square [0,1] × [0,1] instead of an interval, builds surfaces instead of curves — depending on which pairs of edges get identified, and whether the identification includes a flip.

Matching arrow colors mark identified edges; matching direction means no flip, opposite direction means the edge is glued with a reversal. Unmarked (black) edges are left free.

Construction — Cylinder Square [0,1]² with (0, y) ~ (1, y) for all y (left and right edges glued, matching direction, no flip). The top and bottom edges are left free, forming the cylinder's two circular boundary rims.
Construction — Möbius Strip Square [0,1]² with (0, y) ~ (1, 1−y) for all y (left and right edges glued with a flip — the top-left corner joins the bottom-right corner instead of the top-right). Top and bottom remain free.
Construction — Torus T² Square [0,1]² with (0, y) ~ (1, y) and (x, 0) ~ (x, 1) — both pairs of opposite edges glued, matching direction on both. Gluing left-right first produces a cylinder; gluing the cylinder's two circular rims together (top-bottom of the original square) then closes it into the surface of a donut.
Why the flip matters — a genuinely different surface, informally The cylinder and the Möbius strip use the exact same pair of edges — only the direction of the identification differs. Yet they are not homeomorphic: a cylinder has two separate boundary circles (top and bottom) and two distinguishable sides (an inside and an outside you could paint different colors); the Möbius strip has a single boundary curve (tracing the "free" edges continuously all the way around visits both what looked like the top and the bottom before returning to the start) and only one side (a line drawn along its center, followed all the way around, returns to its start on the opposite face from where it began — no full rigorous treatment of orientability is given in this course, but the one-sided, one-boundary behavior is a genuine, checkable topological difference from the cylinder). A single reversed arrow in the gluing instructions is the entire cause.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof that [0,1]/(0~1) ≅ S¹, why is compactness of the quotient space Y needed?

Compactness of the domain, combined with S¹ being Hausdorff, is exactly what the compact-to-Hausdorff theorem needs to upgrade a continuous bijection into a full homeomorphism.

2. What edge identification distinguishes the Möbius strip construction from the cylinder construction?

The only difference between the two constructions is the direction of the identification on the left-right edge pair — matching (cylinder) versus reversed (Möbius strip).

3. How does building the torus relate to building the cylinder?

The torus is built in two stages: first a cylinder (one pair of edges glued), then closing the cylinder's rims together (the second pair) — no flips involved at either stage.

4. Fill in the blank: the single difference between the cylinder and Möbius strip constructions is whether the left-right edge identification includes a ___.

A flip in the gluing direction is the entire cause of the Möbius strip's one-sided, one-boundary behavior, absent from the cylinder.

5. Fill in the blank: every construction in this lesson — the circle, the cylinder, the Möbius strip, the torus — is an example of the ___ topology from Lesson 14, applied to a square or interval.

The quotient topology, gluing points via an equivalence relation, is the single mechanism behind every construction in this lesson.
