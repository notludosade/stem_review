Bases for a Topology

A small generating set of "building-block" open sets — so a topology can be described by a handful of shapes instead of listing every open set by hand.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Writing out τstd on ℝ as "every set built from ε-balls" already hints at a shortcut: instead of naming every open set individually, name a small collection of "basic" open sets and declare the topology to be everything buildable from unions of those. This lesson makes that shortcut precise.

The definition

Definition — Basis for a Topology Let X be a set. A basis ℬ for a topology on X is a collection of subsets of X (called basis elements) such that:
(B1) For every x ∈ X, there is some B ∈ ℬ with x ∈ B.
(B2) If x ∈ B₁ ∩ B₂ for B₁, B₂ ∈ ℬ, there is B₃ ∈ ℬ with x ∈ B₃ ⊆ B₁ ∩ B₂.
The topology generated by ℬ is τℬ = {unions of collections of members of ℬ} (together with ∅, the empty union).
Theorem — τℬ Is Actually a Topology If ℬ satisfies (B1) and (B2), then τℬ is a topology on X.
Proof (T1): ∅ ∈ τℬ by convention (the empty union); X ∈ τℬ since (B1) guarantees every x ∈ X lies in some basis element, so X is the union of all basis elements. (T2): a union of sets, each itself a union of basis elements, is still a union of basis elements — so τℬ is closed under arbitrary unions automatically. (T3): let U, V ∈ τℬ and x ∈ U ∩ V. Since U is a union of basis elements, x ∈ B₁ for some B₁ ∈ ℬ with B₁ ⊆ U; likewise x ∈ B₂ ⊆ V for some B₂ ∈ ℬ. By (B2), there is B₃ ∈ ℬ with x ∈ B₃ ⊆ B₁ ∩ B₂ ⊆ U ∩ V. Since this holds for every x ∈ U ∩ V, the set U ∩ V is itself a union of such B₃'s, so U ∩ V ∈ τℬ. ∎
Why bother with a basis at all Checking (T1)–(T3) directly for a huge, unwieldy collection of sets is painful. Checking (B1)–(B2) for a small, concrete collection is usually easy — and once it's checked, the theorem above hands over an entire topology for free. Every topology used in this course from here on will be introduced via a basis rather than by listing its open sets directly.

Example 1 — the standard topology, generated

Example Let ℬ = {(a, b) : a < b, a, b ∈ ℝ}, the collection of all open intervals. (B1) holds since any x lies in, say, (x − 1, x + 1). (B2) holds since the intersection of two open intervals is itself an open interval (or empty). The topology τℬ generated by ℬ is exactly τstd from Lesson 1: every ε-ball (x − ε, x + ε) is itself an open interval (so lies in ℬ, making τstd ⊆ τℬ), and every open interval satisfies the ε-ball condition at each of its points (so τℬ ⊆ τstd). The two constructions of "the standard topology on ℝ" — via ε-balls in Lesson 1, and via a basis of open intervals here — agree exactly.

Example 2 — bases for the discrete and indiscrete topologies

Example For any set X, ℬ = {{x} : x ∈ X} (all singletons) is a basis satisfying (B1)–(B2) trivially, and it generates the discrete topology — any subset of X is a union of the singletons of its own elements. By contrast, ℬ = {X} (the single set X itself) is a basis generating the indiscrete topology {∅, X}; no smaller collection could satisfy (B1), since some basis element has to contain each point.

A basis is not unique

Why the same topology can come from different bases ℬ = {(a, b)} (open intervals) and ℬ' = {(a, b) : a, b ∈ ℚ} (open intervals with rational endpoints only) generate the exact same topology τstd on ℝ — because density of ℚ in ℝ (Real Analysis A, Unit 1, Lesson 6) guarantees a rational endpoint interval can always be found squeezed inside any ε-ball around a point, and vice versa. This second basis matters later: it's countable, while the first is not, and Unit 6's metrizability discussion will come back to exactly this distinction.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does the topology generated by a basis ℬ consist of?

Every open set in τℬ is built as a union of basis elements — that's the entire construction.

2. In the proof that τℬ satisfies axiom (T3) (finite intersections are open), what does axiom (B2) supply?

(B2) is precisely the condition that lets U ∩ V be rewritten as a union of basis elements, one per point, exactly what membership in τℬ requires.

3. Why does the basis of open intervals with rational endpoints generate the exact same topology as the basis of all open intervals?

Density of ℚ (Real Analysis A, Unit 1, Lesson 6) is exactly what lets the smaller rational-endpoint basis reach every point every irrational-endpoint interval could reach.

4. Fill in the blank: the discrete topology on a set X is generated by the basis of all ___ — one-element subsets of X.

Singletons generate the discrete topology, since every subset of X is a union of the singletons of its own points.

5. Fill in the blank: the standard topology on ℝ is generated by the basis of all ___, and this construction agrees exactly with the ε-ball definition from Lesson 1.

Open intervals (a, b) form the basis that generates τstd, matching the ε-ball construction exactly since every ε-ball is an open interval and vice versa.
