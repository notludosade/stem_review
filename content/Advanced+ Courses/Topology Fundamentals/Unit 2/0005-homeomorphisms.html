Homeomorphisms

What it means for two spaces to be "the same shape" topologically — and a proof that a bounded interval and the entire real line qualify, despite looking nothing alike.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Real Analysis A never needed a notion of "two sets being the same" beyond ordinary equality — every result was about ℝ itself. Topology studies many different spaces at once, so it needs a precise notion of when two of them are indistinguishable from a topologist's point of view: not equal as sets, but structurally identical as far as open sets are concerned.

The definition

Definition — Homeomorphism A function f : X → Y between topological spaces is a homeomorphism if: (i) f is a bijection; (ii) f is continuous; (iii) f⁻¹ is continuous. If such an f exists, X and Y are called homeomorphic, written X ≅ Y.
Why all three conditions, and not just "continuous bijection" A continuous bijection whose inverse is discontinuous still exists — and it fails to preserve topological structure in one direction. Take X = [0, 2π) with the subspace topology from ℝ, Y = S¹ (the unit circle in ℝ², with its usual topology), and f(t) = (cos t, sin t). This f is a continuous bijection: it's built from continuous trigonometric functions, and every point of the circle is hit exactly once. But f⁻¹ is not continuous at (1, 0) = f(0): points on the circle just below (1, 0) (approaching from the fourth quadrant) map back under f⁻¹ to numbers near 2π, not near 0 — so a small open arc around (1, 0) does not pull back to an open set around 0 in [0, 2π). X and Y are not homeomorphic via this f, even though f is a perfectly good continuous bijection.

A worked homeomorphism: a bounded interval and all of ℝ

Worked Example Claim: (−1, 1) ≅ ℝ (both with the subspace/standard topology from ℝ). Define f : (−1, 1) → ℝ by f(x) = x / (1 − x²).
Bijection: f is strictly increasing (its derivative 1/(1−x²) is always positive on (−1,1)) hence injective; as x → ±1⁻, f(x) → ±∞, so f's range is all of ℝ, hence surjective.
f continuous: f is a rational function with denominator 1 − x² ≠ 0 on (−1, 1), so f is continuous there in the ordinary ε-δ sense — which, by Lesson 4's equivalence theorem, is exactly topological continuity.
f⁻¹ continuous: solving y = x/(1−x²) for x gives x = (√(y² + 4) − |y|)/(2·sgn(y)·…) — more simply, f⁻¹ is also built from continuous algebraic and root operations wherever it's defined, hence continuous by the same ε-δ-to-topological equivalence. ∎
Why this matters (−1, 1) is a bounded set; ℝ is unbounded. As metric spaces with sizes measured by distance, they look completely different. But homeomorphism only cares about open sets, and this map shows their open-set structure is identical — every open-set fact provable about one transfers automatically to the other via f. "Bounded" and "unbounded" are metric properties, not topological ones; they are not preserved by homeomorphism, and this example is exactly why.

Topological properties

Definition — Topological Property A topological property is any property of a space defined entirely in terms of its open sets — so that if X has the property and X ≅ Y, then Y has it too. Connectedness (Unit 3) and compactness (Unit 4) will both turn out to be topological properties; boundedness (as the previous example shows) is not.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What three conditions must f : X → Y satisfy to be a homeomorphism?

All three parts are required — bijective, continuous, and continuous inverse — exactly the definition given in this lesson.

2. Why does f(t) = (cos t, sin t) from [0, 2π) to the circle fail to be a homeomorphism, despite being a continuous bijection?

This is the textbook example of a continuous bijection whose inverse fails to be continuous — the "jump" at the identification point 0 ≈ 2π is exactly where it breaks.

3. The worked example shows (−1, 1) ≅ ℝ, even though one is bounded and the other isn't. What does this establish about boundedness?

Since a genuine homeomorphism links a bounded space to an unbounded one, boundedness cannot be defined purely from open sets — it depends on the ambient metric, not the topology.

4. Fill in the blank: a property of a space defined entirely in terms of its open sets, and therefore automatically shared by every homeomorphic space, is called a ___.

A topological property is exactly what transfers automatically across a homeomorphism — connectedness and compactness in later units are the two central examples.

5. Fill in the blank: if a homeomorphism f : X → Y exists, X and Y are said to be ___ — structurally identical as far as open sets go.

Homeomorphic is the term for "topologically the same" — the exact relation a homeomorphism witnesses.
