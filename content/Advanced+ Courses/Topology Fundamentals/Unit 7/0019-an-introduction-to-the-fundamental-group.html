An Introduction to the Fundamental Group

A genuine preview, not a rigorous treatment: the first invariant strong enough to tell a disk and a circle apart, even though homeomorphism arguments alone can't.

← Unit 6, Lesson 18 Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

Everything through Unit 6 distinguished spaces using properties defined at a single point at a time, or via global covers — connectedness, compactness, separation. None of those tools, on their own, can tell a filled disk apart from an unfilled circle: both are compact, both are connected, both are path-connected. This closing preview lesson of the course introduces the idea that finally can — informally, and without the rigor the rest of this course insisted on elsewhere.

Loops and homotopy, informally

Informal Definition — Loop A loop in X based at x₀ is a path γ : [0,1] → X (Unit 3, Lesson 8's definition) with γ(0) = γ(1) = x₀ — a path that starts and ends at the same point.
Informal Definition — Homotopy of Loops Two loops γ₀, γ₁ based at x₀ are homotopic if one can be continuously deformed into the other, through a family of loops all still based at x₀ — formally, a continuous H : [0,1] × [0,1] → X with H(s,0) = γ₀(s), H(s,1) = γ₁(s), and H(0,t) = H(1,t) = x₀ for every t (for the curious; this course does not require working with H directly).
Why "homotopic to a constant loop" is the key question The constant loop at x₀ (γ(s) = x₀ for all s) never leaves x₀. A loop that is homotopic to this constant loop can be continuously shrunk down to a point without ever leaving X. A loop that is not homotopic to a constant loop is genuinely "caught" on some feature of X's shape — and that caughtness is exactly what the fundamental group measures.
Informal Definition — Fundamental Group π₁(X, x₀) The fundamental group π₁(X, x₀) is the set of homotopy classes of loops based at x₀, with a group operation given by concatenating loops (run one loop, then the other, in the same total time interval [0,1]) — stated here without proof that this operation is associative or that inverses exist. X is simply connected if X is path-connected and π₁(X, x₀) is the trivial group (every loop is homotopic to a constant).

On the disk, nested dashed loops show a genuine shrinking family reaching the center. On the circle, no such family exists while staying on the curve — the loop is stuck.

Why the disk is simply connected but the circle isn't On D² (the filled disk), any loop can be shrunk straight-line toward the center: H(s,t) = (1−t)γ(s) + t·x₀ stays inside D² for every t, since D² is convex — so π₁(D², x₀) is trivial. On S¹ (just the boundary curve, with nothing filled in), a loop that winds around once has nowhere to shrink to without leaving the curve entirely — the "hole" in the middle isn't part of the space, so there's no interior point to contract toward. This is stated here, not proved; the rigorous argument (usually via a "covering space" unwinding S¹ into ℝ) is genuinely beyond this course.
Stated Without Proof — π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ The fundamental group of the circle is isomorphic to (ℤ, +): a loop's homotopy class is completely determined by an integer, its winding number — how many times, and in which net direction, it winds around the circle. A loop winding around twice is not homotopic to one winding around once; a loop winding once clockwise is not homotopic to one winding once counterclockwise. This single fact is what finally, rigorously, distinguishes the circle from the disk: trivial group versus ℤ.

Why this matters for everything else in this course

A genuine topological invariant, stronger than anything in Units 1–6 π₁ is a topological property (Unit 2, Lesson 5): homeomorphic spaces have isomorphic fundamental groups. Since D² and S¹ have different fundamental groups (trivial versus ℤ), they cannot be homeomorphic — a fact none of connectedness, compactness, or the separation axioms could ever have detected, since D² and S¹ agree on all of those. The fundamental group is the first tool in this course sensitive enough to see the "hole" a circle has and a disk doesn't.

1. What does it mean for a loop to be homotopic to the constant loop at x₀?

Homotopy to a constant loop is exactly "can be shrunk to a point without leaving the space," the central question this lesson builds toward.

2. Why can every loop in the disk D² be contracted to its center point?

Convexity of D² is exactly what guarantees the straight-line shrinking homotopy never leaves the space.

3. What does π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ mean, informally?

Winding number is exactly the integer invariant that classifies every loop on S¹ up to homotopy.

4. Fill in the blank: a path-connected space whose fundamental group is trivial (every loop contracts to a point) is called ___.

Simply connected is the term for a space with trivial fundamental group, like the disk D².

5. Fill in the blank: since D² and S¹ have different fundamental groups, they cannot be ___ — a conclusion none of Units 1–6's tools alone could reach.

Homeomorphic is exactly the relation ruled out — the fundamental group being a topological property, isomorphic groups are required for spaces to be homeomorphic.
