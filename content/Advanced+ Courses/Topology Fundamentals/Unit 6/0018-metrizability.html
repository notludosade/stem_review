Metrizability

When does a bare topological space, with no distance in sight, turn out to secretly be a metric space wearing a disguise?

← Lesson 17 Unit 7, Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

This entire course opened (Lesson 1) by stripping distance out of Real Analysis A's definition of open. Having spent five units building everything — continuity, connectedness, compactness, products, quotients, separation — without ever needing a metric again, this closing lesson of Unit 6 asks the question in reverse: given a bare topological space with no metric mentioned anywhere, when is it guaranteed that a suitable metric secretly exists, generating exactly the same open sets?

The definition

Definition — Metric, Metrizable A metric on a set X is a function d : X × X → [0, ∞) with: d(x,y) = 0 iff x = y; d(x,y) = d(y,x); and d(x,z) ≤ d(x,y) + d(y,z) (triangle inequality) — exactly the properties |x−y| has on ℝ (Real Analysis A, Unit 1, Lesson 3). A topological space (X, τ) is metrizable if there is a metric d on X whose ε-balls generate exactly τ, via Lesson 2's basis construction.

Metrizable spaces are automatically well-behaved

Theorem Every metrizable space is normal (and hence, by Lesson 17's hierarchy, also regular, Hausdorff, and T1).
Why (proof sketch) Given a metric d generating τ, Lesson 17's ℝⁿ-normality proof generalizes without a single change: for disjoint closed F, G, the function h(x) = d(x,F) − d(x,G) is continuous with respect to d's own ε-balls (the triangle inequality argument never actually used anything specific to ℝⁿ beyond having a metric available), and its sign again separates F from G with disjoint open sets. Metrizability is therefore a genuinely demanding condition: it forces normality (and everything below it) as a necessary consequence, for free.

A concrete non-metrizable space

Example The indiscrete topology on a set X with |X| ≥ 2 is not metrizable. If it were, some metric d would generate it — but then for distinct x, y, the balls B(x, d(x,y)/2) and B(y, d(x,y)/2) would be disjoint open sets separating x and y (Lesson 16's Hausdorff proof, verbatim), making the space Hausdorff. The indiscrete topology is not Hausdorff (Lesson 16), so by contrapositive, no such metric d can exist.

The converse question: when is normal (or Hausdorff) enough?

Why normal alone isn't enough to guarantee metrizability The previous theorem only runs in one direction: metrizable ⟹ normal. A space can be normal, Hausdorff, T1 — every separation axiom in Lesson 17's hierarchy — and still fail to be metrizable, for reasons that have nothing to do with separation at all (a space with "too many points to be reached by countably many ε-balls" is the typical obstruction, related to Lesson 2's rational-vs-irrational-endpoint basis comparison). Determining exactly when the converse holds is a genuinely deep question, answered by one of general topology's landmark theorems.
Theorem (Urysohn Metrization Theorem — stated without proof) Every regular, Hausdorff (T3) topological space with a countable basis (Lesson 2) is metrizable.
Why this theorem is only stated here, not proved Urysohn's Metrization Theorem is a genuinely deep result — its standard proof constructs an explicit embedding of X into an infinite-dimensional cube using countably many continuous functions built from Urysohn's Lemma (a strengthening of the normal-space separation argument in Lesson 17), well beyond the scope of an introductory course. It is stated here, without proof, for exactly the reason Real Analysis A's Lesson 1 stated completeness before proving anything from it: to name precisely the theorem that tells you when a bare topological space is secretly a metric space in disguise — the question this entire lesson exists to ask.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does it mean for a topological space (X, τ) to be metrizable?

Metrizability is about whether some metric's ε-ball basis reproduces the given topology exactly, matching Lesson 2's basis construction.

2. Why does metrizability guarantee a space is normal?

Lesson 17's normality proof for ℝⁿ only used the triangle inequality and the existence of a metric, so it transfers unchanged to any metrizable space.

3. What does the Urysohn Metrization Theorem state?

Regularity, the Hausdorff property, and a countable basis together are exactly the sufficient conditions this landmark theorem identifies.

4. Fill in the blank: the ___ topology on a set with two or more points is not metrizable, since it fails to be Hausdorff, and every metrizable space must be Hausdorff.

Indiscrete is the standard example of a space failing Hausdorff, hence failing metrizability by contrapositive.

5. Fill in the blank: the Urysohn Metrization Theorem's third hypothesis, beyond regular and Hausdorff, requires the space to have a ___ basis.

Countable is the third hypothesis, connecting back to Lesson 2's observation that ℝ has a countable basis (rational-endpoint intervals) generating the same topology as the uncountable basis of all intervals.
