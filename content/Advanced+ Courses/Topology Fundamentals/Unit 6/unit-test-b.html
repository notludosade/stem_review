1. Which of the following is the standard example of a non-Hausdorff space?

The indiscrete topology has too few open sets (only ∅ and X) to separate any two distinct points, failing Hausdorff.

2. What generalization of Real Analysis A's ε-N definition does topological convergence use?

Replacing ε-balls with arbitrary open sets containing the candidate limit is exactly the topological generalization of ε-N convergence.

3. Fill in the blank: every ___ space is automatically T1, but the cofinite topology on an infinite set shows the converse fails.

Hausdorff spaces are automatically T1, though T1 alone is strictly weaker.

4. How is "regular" defined, building on T1?

Regular separates a point from a closed set not containing it — one level up from Hausdorff's point-from-point separation, one level below normal's closed-set-from-closed-set separation.

5. Fill in the blank: since every metrizable space is normal, and normal implies regular implies ___, every metrizable space has unique sequence limits (Lesson 16).

Hausdorff is the property in the chain that directly guarantees uniqueness of limits, per Lesson 16.

6. Consider two distinct points x, y ∈ ℝ³ with |x − y| = 6. Answer both parts:

(a) What radius r should the two separating balls B(x,r), B(y,r) use?

(b) Fill in the blank: using r = 3 guarantees the balls are disjoint by the ___, since a shared point would force |x−y| < |x−y|.

(a) Half the distance, 3, is exactly the largest safe radius. (b) The triangle inequality is what forces the contradiction if the balls overlapped.

7. Consider two nonempty open sets U, V in the cofinite topology on an infinite set X. Answer both parts:

(a) What must be true of X \ U and X \ V?

(b) Fill in the blank: since X \ (U ∩ V) = (X\U) ∪ (X\V) is a union of two finite sets, hence finite, U ∩ V = X minus a finite set must be ___, so U and V always intersect.

(a) Cofinite means exactly this — every nonempty open set has a finite complement. (b) An infinite set minus a finite set is always nonempty, forcing any two nonempty opens to intersect.

8. Let F = {(0,0)} and G = {(x,y) : x² + y² = 9} (a circle of radius 3) in ℝ², disjoint closed sets. Answer both parts:

(a) What is d((0,0), F)?

(b) Fill in the blank: since d((0,0),F) = 0 and d((0,0),G) = 3, h((0,0)) = 0 − 3 = −3, which is ___, correctly placing the origin in U.

(a) The origin is in F, so its distance to F is 0. (b) h is negative at the origin, placing it in U = h⁻¹((−∞,0)), the side containing F.

9. Suppose (X, τ) is known not to be Hausdorff. Answer both parts:

(a) What can be concluded about metrizability of (X, τ)?

(b) Fill in the blank: this follows because any ___ automatically generates a Hausdorff topology, via the same disjoint-ball construction proved for ℝⁿ.

(a) Non-Hausdorff directly rules out metrizability. (b) Any metric automatically produces a Hausdorff topology via its ε-balls, so a non-Hausdorff space cannot have one.

10. Consider why Lesson 2's comparison of the rational-endpoint basis to the full interval basis on ℝ is relevant to Urysohn's theorem. Answer both parts:

(a) What property of ℝ's rational-endpoint basis does this connect to?

(b) Fill in the blank: since ℝ is also Hausdorff and ___ (as a normal, hence regular, space by Lesson 17), Urysohn's theorem confirms ℝ is metrizable — as expected, via |x−y|.

(a) The countable rational-endpoint basis is exactly what satisfies Urysohn's third hypothesis. (b) ℝ being regular (via normal ⟹ regular) completes all three hypotheses, confirming metrizability, consistent with ℝ's already-known metric |x−y|.
