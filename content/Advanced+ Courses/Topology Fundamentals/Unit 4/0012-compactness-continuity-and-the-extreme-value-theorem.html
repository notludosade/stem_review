Compactness, Continuity, and the Extreme Value Theorem

Continuous functions preserve compactness — and once that's known, the Extreme Value Theorem drops out in three lines, no maximizing sequence required.

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Real Analysis A, Unit 4, Lesson 23 proved the Extreme Value Theorem in two steps: first show f(K) is bounded (else a sequence xₙ with |f(xₙ)| → ∞ exists, and compactness of K extracts a convergent subsequence, contradicting continuity), then show the supremum is attained (a similar subsequence-extraction argument). This closing lesson of Unit 4 replaces both steps with a single general theorem about compactness and continuity.

Continuous images of compact spaces are compact

Theorem If f : X → Y is continuous, X is compact, and f is surjective, then Y is compact.
Proof Let 𝒱 = {Vα} be an open cover of Y. Since f is continuous, {f⁻¹(Vα)} is a collection of open sets in X, and it covers X: for any x ∈ X, f(x) ∈ Y lies in some Vα (𝒱 covers Y), so x ∈ f⁻¹(Vα). Since X is compact, finitely many f⁻¹(Vα₁), …, f⁻¹(Vαk) already cover X. Then Vα₁, …, Vαk cover Y: for any y ∈ Y, surjectivity gives x ∈ X with f(x) = y; x lies in some f⁻¹(Vαᵢ), so y = f(x) ∈ Vαᵢ. So 𝒱 has a finite subcover, and Y is compact. ∎
Why this proof is a near-copy of Lesson 7's Compare this to Lesson 7's "continuous image of connected is connected": both proofs pull a hypothetical obstruction (a separation; an infinite cover with no finite subcover) back through f⁻¹ to the domain, where the domain's known property (connected; compact) rules it out. The pattern — preimages of a well-behaved structure on Y become the same well-behaved structure on X, because f is continuous — is the single most repeated proof technique in this entire course.

The Extreme Value Theorem, rebuilt

Theorem — Extreme Value Theorem If f : X → ℝ is continuous and X is compact, then f attains a maximum and a minimum value on X.
Proof By the theorem above, f(X) is compact (X compact, f continuous, and f is surjective onto its own image). By the Heine-Borel theorem (Lesson 11), f(X) ⊆ ℝ compact means f(X) is closed and bounded. Boundedness gives s = sup f(X) ∈ ℝ. Since f(X) is bounded above and s = sup f(X), every ε-ball around s meets f(X) (epsilon characterization of supremum, Real Analysis A Unit 1 Lesson 4) — so s is either in f(X) already, or is a limit point of f(X) not in f(X). The second case is impossible: f(X) is closed, hence contains all its own limit points (Lesson 3). So s ∈ f(X), meaning s = f(c) for some c ∈ X — the maximum is attained at c. The minimum follows identically, applied to −f. ∎
Why this proof needed no maximizing sequence at all Real Analysis A's proof had to manually construct a maximizing sequence xₙ with f(xₙ) → sup f(K), then extract a convergent subsequence via sequential compactness, then argue continuity carries the limit through. Every one of those steps is superseded here by one fact: a bounded above closed subset of ℝ automatically contains its own supremum. That fact is a direct corollary of "closed sets contain their limit points" (Lesson 3) plus the epsilon characterization of supremum — no sequence is constructed anywhere in this version of the proof.

Worked example

Worked Example Let K = {(x, y) ∈ ℝ² : x² + y² ≤ 4} (the closed disk of radius 2), which is closed and bounded, hence compact by Heine-Borel. Let f(x, y) = x + 2y, continuous (a linear function). By this lesson's theorem, f attains a maximum on K. (Direct check via Lagrange multipliers, outside this course's scope, gives the maximum at (2/√5, 4/√5), with value 2√5 — but the existence of some maximizer, guaranteed purely from compactness and continuity, requires none of that computation.)

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the proof that continuous images of compact spaces are compact, what does compactness of X supply?

Compactness of X is applied to the pulled-back cover {f⁻¹(V_α)}, and the resulting finite subcover of X translates directly into a finite subcover of Y.

2. In the EVT proof, why must s = sup f(X) actually belong to f(X)?

Since f(X) is closed, it can't have a limit point sitting outside itself — forcing s, which is either in f(X) or a limit point of it, to actually belong to f(X).

3. What does this lesson's EVT proof avoid constructing, compared to Real Analysis A's original proof?

This proof reaches attainment directly from "closed sets contain their limit points," with no maximizing sequence or subsequence extraction anywhere in the argument.

4. Fill in the blank: the theorem in this lesson shows continuous images of ___ spaces are themselves ___ (same word both times) — the direct analogue of Lesson 7's connectedness result.

Compact is the property preserved by continuous surjective images, mirroring exactly how connectedness was preserved in Lesson 7.

5. Fill in the blank: the EVT proof concludes f(X) is closed and bounded by applying the ___ theorem to the compact set f(X) ⊆ ℝ.

Heine-Borel (Lesson 11) is exactly what converts "compact subset of ℝ" into "closed and bounded," setting up the sup-is-attained argument.
