The Heine-Borel Theorem

Compact subsets of ℝⁿ are exactly the closed, bounded ones — reproved by bisection from the open-cover definition, and extended past ℝ to every dimension at once.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 17 proved that a subset of ℝ is (sequentially) compact if and only if it is closed and bounded. This lesson proves the identical characterization using Lesson 10's open-cover definition, first for a closed bounded interval [a, b], then for general closed bounded subsets of ℝⁿ.

Step 1 — [a, b] is compact

Theorem Every closed, bounded interval [a, b] ⊆ ℝ is compact.
Proof sketch (bisection) Suppose toward contradiction some open cover 𝒰 of [a, b] has no finite subcover. Bisect [a, b] into two closed halves of equal length; if both halves had a finite subcover from 𝒰, their union would give a finite subcover of [a, b], contradicting the assumption — so at least one half, call it I₁, also has no finite subcover. Repeat: bisect I₁, get I₂ with no finite subcover, and so on, producing a nested sequence [a,b] = I₀ ⊇ I₁ ⊇ I₂ ⊇ ⋯ of closed intervals, each with no finite subcover, and lengths shrinking to 0. The endpoints of the Iₙ's form sequences squeezing toward a single point c (a consequence of the completeness axiom, Real Analysis A Unit 1 Lesson 5 — the nested interval property). Since c ∈ [a, b], some U ∈ 𝒰 contains c, and U being open means some ε-ball around c lies in U. Once the intervals Iₙ shrink below width ε, Iₙ ⊆ U — meaning the single set U is already a finite (one-element) subcover of Iₙ, contradicting that Iₙ was chosen to have no finite subcover. ∎
Why completeness resurfaces here The nested intervals shrinking to a single point c is not automatic — it fails over ℚ for exactly the reason Real Analysis A opened the entire course with (Unit 1, Lesson 1's √2 example). Compactness of [a,b], the theorem underlying this entire unit, ultimately still traces back to the completeness axiom, just reached by a different route than Real Analysis A's original sequential proof.

Step 2 — general closed, bounded subsets of ℝⁿ

Theorem — Heine-Borel Theorem A subset K ⊆ ℝⁿ is compact (open-cover sense) if and only if K is closed and bounded.
Proof sketch (⇐, closed + bounded ⇒ compact) If K is bounded, K fits inside some closed box [−M, M]ⁿ = [−M,M] × ⋯ × [−M,M]. Each factor [−M, M] is compact (Step 1). A finite product of compact spaces is compact (a fact this course states here and proves properly once the product topology is built in Unit 5, Lesson 13) — so [−M, M]ⁿ is compact. K is a closed subset of this compact box (closed in ℝⁿ, hence closed in the box's subspace topology too), so by Lesson 10's closed-subset theorem, K is compact.
Proof sketch (⇒, compact ⇒ closed + bounded) Bounded: the collection of open balls {B(0, m) : m = 1, 2, 3, …} covers ℝⁿ, hence covers K; compactness of K extracts a finite subcover B(0, m₁), …, B(0, mk), and taking M = max(mᵢ) shows K ⊆ B(0, M) — bounded. Closed: fix x ∉ K. For each k ∈ K, since x ≠ k, the balls B(x, |x−k|/2) and B(k, |x−k|/2) are disjoint. The collection {B(k, |x−k|/2) : k ∈ K} covers K; extract a finite subcover around k₁, …, km. Then the intersection of the corresponding disjoint x-balls, ⋂B(x, |x−kᵢ|/2), is a single open neighborhood of x missing K entirely (it's disjoint from every one of the finitely many covering k-balls). So every point outside K has an open neighborhood avoiding K — meaning ℝⁿ \ K is open, so K is closed. ∎
Why the "closed" half of this proof previews Unit 6 The disjoint-balls trick used above — separating a point x from a set K using two disjoint open balls — is exactly the defining property of a Hausdorff space, formalized in Unit 6, Lesson 16. It works here because ℝⁿ has a metric to build the balls from; that same separation trick will need to be assumed as an axiom, rather than derived, once metrics are no longer available.

Connecting back explicitly

Example — Comparing to Real Analysis A Real Analysis A, Unit 3, Lesson 17 proved [a, b] is (sequentially) compact via the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem: every sequence in [a,b] has a convergent subsequence, landing back in [a,b] because [a,b] is closed. This lesson reaches the identical conclusion — compactness of [a, b] — via bisection and open covers instead, with no sequences anywhere in the argument. Both proofs ultimately draw on completeness; only the packaging differs, and this lesson's version is the one that extends past ℝ to ℝⁿ (and, as Lesson 10 noted, past ℝⁿ to any topological space) without modification.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the bisection proof that [a,b] is compact, what property of ℝ guarantees the nested intervals Iₙ shrink to a single point c?

The nested interval property, which guarantees the shrinking intervals squeeze down to a single point, is itself a consequence of completeness — the same axiom Real Analysis A opened the entire course with.

2. According to the Heine-Borel theorem, which subsets of ℝⁿ are compact?

Closed and bounded together are exactly equivalent to compactness in ℝⁿ — neither condition alone suffices, as (0,1) (bounded, not closed) shows.

3. In the proof that compact ⇒ closed, what does the disjoint-balls construction around x and each k ∈ K accomplish?

Intersecting finitely many disjoint x-balls (one per point of a finite subcover of K) yields a single open set around x missing K — exactly what's needed to show ℝⁿ \ K is open.

4. Fill in the blank: the proof that [a,b] is compact uses repeated ___ — halving the interval at each step to locate a point where the open cover argument breaks down.

Bisection is the technique that produces the nested shrinking intervals central to the proof.

5. Fill in the blank: the disjoint-balls separation trick used in the "closed" half of the Heine-Borel proof previews the ___ property, formalized in Unit 6.

Hausdorff is the axiom that abstracts exactly this "separate any two points/point-and-set with disjoint open sets" trick, once a metric is no longer available to build the balls from directly.
