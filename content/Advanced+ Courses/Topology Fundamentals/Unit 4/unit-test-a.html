1. X is compact when:

Compactness requires that no matter how the open cover is chosen (possibly infinite), a finite piece of it already suffices.

2. Why is a closed subset of a compact space automatically compact?

This is the exact strategy of the proof: patch the cover with the open complement, apply compactness of the ambient space, then discard the complement from the resulting finite subcover.

3. Fill in the blank: the Heine-Borel theorem states that compact subsets of ℝⁿ are exactly those that are ___.

Closed and bounded together are exactly equivalent to compactness in ℝⁿ.

4. In the bisection proof that [a,b] is compact, what happens once the shrinking intervals fall below width ε?

Once the interval is small enough to fit inside U, that single set is already a finite subcover of it — contradicting that every Iₙ was chosen to have no finite subcover.

5. Fill in the blank: if f : X → Y is a continuous surjection and X is compact, then Y is guaranteed to be ___ as well.

Compactness transfers forward under continuous surjections, exactly mirroring the connectedness result from Unit 3.

6. Consider the open cover {(−n, n) : n = 1, 2, 3, …} of ℝ. Answer both parts:

(a) Does this cover have a finite subcover of ℝ?

(b) Fill in the blank: this confirms ℝ is not compact, consistent with Heine-Borel since ℝ is ___.

(a) A largest finite index n always leaves points outside (−n,n) uncovered. (b) ℝ fails Heine-Borel's boundedness requirement, consistent with this example.

7. In the proof that compact ⇒ closed for K ⊆ ℝⁿ, consider a point x ∉ K. Answer both parts:

(a) What construction separates x from each point k ∈ K?

(b) Fill in the blank: extracting a ___ subcover of the k-balls (via compactness of K) lets the intersection of the corresponding x-balls form a single open neighborhood of x avoiding K.

(a) The disjoint-ball construction is the key separation tool. (b) Only a finite intersection of the x-balls is needed, guaranteed by compactness of K.

8. Let K = [1, 5] ⊆ ℝ (compact by Heine-Borel) and f(x) = −x² + 6x. Answer both parts:

(a) Does the topological EVT guarantee f attains a maximum on K?

(b) Fill in the blank: the proof shows s = sup f(K) belongs to f(K) because f(K) is ___, so it must contain all its own limit points.

(a) Continuity plus compactness is exactly the hypothesis EVT needs. (b) f(K) is closed (Heine-Borel), so it contains its supremum as a limit point or element.

9. Comparing this course's EVT proof to Real Analysis A's. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this course's proof avoid constructing that Real Analysis A's needed?

(b) Fill in the blank: instead, this course's proof uses the fact that closed sets contain all their own ___, applied directly to f(X).

(a) No maximizing sequence is ever built in this course's version. (b) "Closed sets contain their limit points" directly forces the supremum into f(X).

10. Consider the box [−3, 3] × [−3, 3] ⊆ ℝ². Answer both parts:

(a) Is this box compact?

(b) Fill in the blank: Lesson 11 relies on the fact that a ___ product of compact spaces is compact, to extend Heine-Borel from single intervals to boxes in ℝⁿ.

(a) Closed and bounded in ℝ² means compact, by Heine-Borel. (b) Finite products of compact spaces stay compact, the fact underlying the box-in-ℝⁿ argument.
