The Total Derivative

Lesson 17 showed partial derivatives can miss a function's behavior entirely. This lesson defines the notion that doesn't: differentiability as the existence of a single best linear approximation, sensitive to every direction at once.

← Lesson 17 Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — (Total) Differentiability Let f: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ and a ∈ ℝⁿ. f is differentiable at a if there exists a linear map L: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ such that

limh→0 |f(a+h) − f(a) − Lh| / |h| = 0.

Such an L, when it exists, is unique, and is called the total derivative of f at a, written Df(a).
Why this is the right definition Rewriting the limit condition as f(a+h) = f(a) + Df(a)h + o(|h|), the total derivative is exactly the linear map making f(a) + Df(a)h the best possible linear approximation to f near a — with an error that vanishes faster than |h| itself, in every direction h simultaneously, not just the n coordinate directions a partial derivative probes. This is precisely what Lesson 17's counterexample was missing.
Theorem — Differentiable Implies Continuous If f is differentiable at a, then f is continuous at a.
Proof Since Df(a) is linear on ℝⁿ (finite-dimensional), it is bounded: |Df(a)h| ≤ M|h| for some constant M and all h. By the triangle inequality, |f(a+h) − f(a)| ≤ |Df(a)h| + |f(a+h) − f(a) − Df(a)h| ≤ M|h| + |h|·ε(h), where ε(h) → 0 as h → 0 by the definition of differentiability. As h → 0, both terms on the right vanish, so f(a+h) → f(a). ∎
Why this restores what Lesson 17 lost Total differentiability succeeds exactly where mere existence of partial derivatives failed: it forces continuity, just as in the single-variable case. The xy/(x²+y²) example of Lesson 17 is therefore not totally differentiable at the origin, even though both its partial derivatives exist there — partial derivatives existing is strictly weaker than total differentiability.
Theorem — Differentiability Implies All Partials Exist, Given by the Jacobian If f: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ is differentiable at a, then every partial derivative ∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ(a) exists, and Df(a) is the m×n matrix [∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ(a)] — the Jacobian matrix — acting by matrix multiplication.
Proof Apply the defining limit with h = t·eⱼ for small real t: |f(a+t·eⱼ) − f(a) − t·Df(a)eⱼ| / |t| → 0 as t → 0. Looking at the i-th component, this forces [fᵢ(a+t·eⱼ) − fᵢ(a)]/t → [Df(a)eⱼ]ᵢ as t → 0 — exactly the statement that ∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ(a) exists and equals the i-th entry of Df(a)eⱼ, the j-th column of the matrix representing Df(a). Since this holds for every i, j, the matrix of Df(a) is precisely [∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ(a)]. ∎
Why the converse of this theorem is false — and what fixes it Lesson 17's example shows partials can exist with no total derivative present at all, so this theorem's implication only runs one way. The standard repair is a sufficient condition rather than a full converse:
Theorem (stated) — C¹ Implies Differentiable If all partial derivatives of f exist in a neighborhood of a and are continuous at a, then f is differentiable at a.
Proof sketch Write the increment f(a+h) − f(a) as a telescoping sum of n single-variable increments, changing one coordinate at a time: f(a₁+h₁,…,aₙ+hₙ) − f(a) is a sum of terms each differing in exactly one coordinate. Apply the single-variable Mean Value Theorem to each term, producing an intermediate point where the corresponding partial derivative is evaluated exactly. Because each partial derivative is continuous at a, replacing these intermediate evaluations with ∂f/∂xⱼ(a) itself introduces an error that shrinks faster than |h| — which is exactly the o(|h|) condition differentiability requires, with Df(a) equal to the Jacobian matrix of continuous partials. The full accounting of the error terms is in the source reading below.
Example f(x,y) = x²y has ∂f/∂x = 2xy and ∂f/∂y = x², both polynomials, hence continuous everywhere. By the theorem above, f is differentiable at every point of ℝ², with Df(a,b) given by the 1×2 Jacobian matrix [2ab, a²].
