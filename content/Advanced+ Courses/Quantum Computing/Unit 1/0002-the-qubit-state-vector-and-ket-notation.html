The Qubit State Vector and Ket Notation

Physicists write vectors with a strange-looking bracket, |·⟩, instead of the bold v or arrow-v from Linear Algebra A. It's still just a column vector — the notation is doing real work, and this lesson explains exactly what.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Linear Algebra A wrote a vector as v = (v₁, v₂). Quantum computing almost never uses that notation — instead it uses Dirac notation (also called bra-ket notation), introduced by physicist Paul Dirac. It looks unfamiliar at first, but every symbol translates directly to something you already know: a column vector, a row vector, and an inner product.

1. Kets: the basis states

Definition — Computational Basis Kets A single qubit's state lives in the 2-dimensional complex vector space ℂ². The two ket vectors |0⟩ and |1⟩ — read "ket-zero" and "ket-one" — are defined as the standard basis:
|0⟩ = (1, 0)ᵀ  |1⟩ = (0, 1)ᵀ

These are exactly the standard basis vectors e₁ and e₂ from Linear Algebra A, just written with a ket instead of a bold letter. |0⟩ corresponds to the classical bit value 0, and |1⟩ to the classical bit value 1 — but as you'll see in Lesson 3, they are only two of infinitely many valid qubit states, not the only two.

2. General kets and complex amplitudes

Definition — Qubit State (Ket Form) A general single-qubit state is written |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, where α, β ∈ ℂ are called probability amplitudes. As a column vector, |ψ⟩ = (α, β)ᵀ. The Greek letter ψ ("psi") is a conventional placeholder name for "some state," exactly the way linear algebra uses v for "some vector."

Notice that the amplitudes are allowed to be complex numbers, not just real ones — this is new territory that Linear Algebra B's treatment of complex eigenvalues was quietly preparing you for. Complex amplitudes are what make interference (Lesson 1's teaser) possible: two complex numbers can partially or fully cancel each other out in ways two nonnegative real probabilities never could.

3. Bras: the dual vectors

Definition — Bra The bra ⟨ψ| corresponding to a ket |ψ⟩ = (α, β)ᵀ is its conjugate transpose (Hermitian adjoint): ⟨ψ| = (α*, β*), a row vector, where α* and β* are the complex conjugates of α and β. In particular ⟨0| = (1, 0) and ⟨1| = (0, 1) — real numbers are their own conjugates, so for the basis kets, "bra" just means "transpose."
Why the conjugate, not just the transpose For a complex vector space, the transpose alone doesn't give a well-behaved notion of length: if you compute vᵀv for a complex vector v, the result isn't generally real, let alone nonnegative — so it can't be a "squared length." Conjugating before transposing fixes this. For v = (α, β)ᵀ, v†v = |α|² + |β|², always a nonnegative real number, exactly the generalization of the dot product you need. This is the same conjugate-transpose construction Linear Algebra B used for Hermitian and unitary matrices — a qubit's bra-ket machinery is that construction applied to individual vectors.

4. Bra-ket products: the inner product

Definition — Inner Product Writing a bra next to a ket, ⟨φ|ψ⟩, computes their inner product: for |φ⟩ = (γ, δ)ᵀ and |ψ⟩ = (α, β)ᵀ, ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = γ*α + δ*β. This is exactly the complex-vector generalization of the dot product from Linear Algebra A.

The computational basis is orthonormal under this inner product, just as e₁ and e₂ were in ℝ²:

⟨0|0⟩ = 1 ⟨1|1⟩ = 1 ⟨0|1⟩ = 0 ⟨1|0⟩ = 0

This orthonormality is what lets you read amplitudes straight off a state: for |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, computing ⟨0|ψ⟩ = α⟨0|0⟩ + β⟨0|1⟩ = α picks out exactly the coefficient of |0⟩, the same "dot with a basis vector to extract a coordinate" trick from Linear Algebra A, now written in bra-ket form.

Example Let |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4i/5)|1⟩. As a column vector, |ψ⟩ = (3/5, 4i/5)ᵀ. Its bra is ⟨ψ| = (3/5, −4i/5), conjugating 4i/5 to −4i/5. Then ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = (3/5)(3/5) + (−4i/5)(4i/5) = 9/25 − 16i²/25 = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1 — this state is properly normalized, the requirement Lesson 3 states precisely.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. As a column vector, what is |1⟩?

|1⟩ is defined as the second standard basis vector of ℂ², (0, 1)ᵀ — |0⟩ is the first, (1, 0)ᵀ.

2. For |ψ⟩ = (α, β)ᵀ, what operation produces the bra ⟨ψ|?

The bra is the Hermitian adjoint of the ket — transpose it into a row vector, and conjugate every entry — which is exactly what makes ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ come out real and nonnegative.

3. For |φ⟩ = |0⟩ + i|1⟩ and |ψ⟩ = i|0⟩ − |1⟩, what is ⟨φ|ψ⟩?

|φ⟩ = (1, i)ᵀ, so ⟨φ| = (1, −i) (conjugating i to −i). |ψ⟩ = (i, −1)ᵀ. Then ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = (1)(i) + (−i)(−1) = i + i = 2i.

4. Fill in the blank: the bra ⟨ψ| is defined as the ___ of the ket |ψ⟩.

Conjugate transpose (equivalently, Hermitian adjoint) turns a column ket into a row bra while conjugating every entry — the construction that makes ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ always real and nonnegative.

5. Fill in the blank: for the computational basis, ⟨0|1⟩ = ___, expressing that |0⟩ and |1⟩ are orthogonal.

⟨0|1⟩ = (1)(0) + (0)(1) = 0 — the computational basis kets are orthonormal, exactly like the standard basis in ℝⁿ.
