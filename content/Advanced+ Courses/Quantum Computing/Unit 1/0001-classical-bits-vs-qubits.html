Classical Bits vs. Qubits

A classical bit is a switch that is definitely on or definitely off. A qubit is a vector — and that single change is the whole reason quantum computing needs Linear Algebra A and B as prerequisites.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Every course you've taken up to this point has been about describing the world with real numbers, functions, and — most recently, in Linear Algebra A and B — vectors and linear maps. Quantum computing asks a strange-sounding question: what if the state of a bit of information were itself a vector, in a complex vector space, instead of a single number 0 or 1? That question turns out to have a precise, checkable answer, and this course builds it from the ground up. This first lesson just gets the vocabulary and the contrast in place.

1. The classical bit

Definition — Classical Bit A classical bit is a system with exactly two possible states, labeled 0 and 1. At every instant it is in exactly one of them. A classical computer's entire state — its memory, its registers, everything — is, underneath, just a very long string of definite 0s and 1s.

A probabilistic bit — one you don't know the value of, but which is "really" a 0 or a 1 with some probability of each — is still a classical bit in the sense that matters here: it has one definite value at any given time, you simply haven't looked yet. Uncertainty is a fact about the observer, not a fact about the bit.

2. The qubit

Definition — Qubit (Informal) A qubit (quantum bit) is a two-level quantum system. Its state is not simply 0 or 1 — it is a vector in a two-dimensional complex vector space, and that vector can be a genuine combination of "the 0 state" and "the 1 state" simultaneously, called a superposition. Lesson 2 makes this precise with ket notation; Lesson 3 pins down exactly what "combination" is allowed to mean.

Physically, a qubit can be built from many different two-level systems: the spin of an electron (up or down), the polarization of a photon (horizontal or vertical), two energy levels of a trapped ion, or the two lowest states of a superconducting circuit. This course will not depend on any one physical realization — everything through Unit 7 is pure linear algebra, and Unit 8 comes back to survey the real hardware.

Why "a qubit is a weighted coin" is the wrong picture It's tempting to think of a qubit in superposition as a coin spinning in the air — "really" heads or tails, just not settled yet, the same as a probabilistic classical bit. That picture is wrong in a way that matters: a spinning coin's uncertainty is epistemic (you just don't know the outcome yet, but a definite outcome already exists). A qubit's superposition is not hidden information about a pre-existing 0-or-1 value — there is no fact of the matter about which one it "really" is until it's measured. The clearest evidence this isn't just classical ignorance is interference: two different superposition states can be recombined by a gate so that certain outcomes become impossible, a phenomenon with no classical-probability analogue at all. You'll see this concretely once Unit 2 introduces gates and Unit 6 puts interference to work in an actual algorithm.

3. Why this changes what "state" can hold

A single classical bit stores one of 2 states. Two classical bits store one of 2² = 4 states — but a physical register of two classical bits is only ever in exactly one of those four at a time. A pair of qubits, by contrast, has a state space that is 2² = 4-dimensional as a vector space, and the qubit pair's actual state can carry a nonzero component along every one of those four basis directions simultaneously. Scale this up: n qubits span a 2ⁿ-dimensional space. This exponential scaling of the description is the resource every quantum algorithm in Units 6 and 7 is trying to exploit — and, as Lesson 12 will make painfully clear, also the reason you can't just "read off" all that information for free.

Example — Counting States 3 classical bits: exactly one of 2³ = 8 configurations is realized at any instant (say, 101). 3 qubits: the state lives in an 8-dimensional complex vector space, and a valid state can have a nonzero amplitude on all 8 basis directions |000⟩, |001⟩, …, |111⟩ at once. Unit 4 makes this rigorous with tensor products; for now, just notice the qualitative difference — "one of 8" versus "potentially a combination of all 8."

1. What is the key difference between a qubit in superposition and a classical bit whose value you simply don't know yet (a "probabilistic bit")?

The probabilistic bit's uncertainty is purely about the observer's knowledge — a definite value already exists. A qubit's superposition has no such pre-existing value, and superposition states can interfere with each other in ways no classical probability distribution can reproduce.

2. How many possible definite configurations does a register of 5 classical bits have, and how is that different from what a system of 5 qubits can do?

n classical bits have 2ⁿ possible configurations but occupy exactly one at a time; n qubits span a 2ⁿ-dimensional vector space in which the actual state can have nonzero components along many of the 2ⁿ basis directions simultaneously.

3. Which of these is a valid physical realization of a qubit, according to this lesson?

A qubit is defined mathematically as a two-level quantum system, so any physical system with two relevant quantum levels can host one — this course works with the shared mathematics rather than any single hardware platform (Unit 8 surveys the real options).

4. Fill in the blank: a qubit's state can be a genuine combination of "0" and "1" at once, a phenomenon called ___.

Superposition is the name for exactly this combination — it's the central new idea a qubit adds on top of a classical bit.

5. Fill in the blank: the clearest evidence that a qubit's superposition isn't just classical ignorance about a hidden definite value is the phenomenon of ___, which has no classical-probability analogue.

Interference — amplitudes combining so certain outcomes become impossible — is the signature effect that separates true superposition from classical probabilistic uncertainty.
