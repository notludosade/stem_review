The Bloch Sphere

Every single-qubit state, up to one physically invisible piece of information, is a single point on the surface of an ordinary sphere in ℝ³ — turning Lesson 3's 3-dimensional surface of states into a picture you can actually draw.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 3 established that valid qubit states fill up a 3-dimensional surface, not a single point or a simple curve. That's hard to visualize directly inside ℂ² ≅ ℝ⁴. The Bloch sphere is a way to see that surface as an ordinary 2-dimensional sphere sitting in ordinary 3-dimensional space — at the cost of throwing away exactly one piece of information that Lesson 5 will show never mattered physically in the first place.

1. Every state as two angles

Fact — The Bloch Parametrization Any single-qubit state can be written using two real angles θ ∈ [0, π] and φ ∈ [0, 2π):
|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩

Check this is automatically normalized: |cos(θ/2)|² + |eiφsin(θ/2)|² = cos²(θ/2) + sin²(θ/2) = 1, using |eiφ| = 1 for any real φ. Every choice of (θ, φ) gives a valid state, and — this is the content of Lesson 5 — every physically distinct single-qubit state arises from exactly one point (θ, φ) on the sphere's surface, once a leftover overall phase freedom is set aside.

2. Reading the sphere

Landmarks on the Sphere North pole (θ = 0): |0⟩, for any φ (the φ dependence multiplies a zero coefficient, so it drops out). South pole (θ = π): |1⟩, likewise for any φ. The entire equator (θ = π/2) is the set of equal-weight superpositions (cos(π/4) = sin(π/4) = 1/√2), with φ = 0 giving |+⟩, φ = π giving |−⟩, and every other φ giving a different equal-weight superposition state with its own relative phase.

3. What the sphere does and doesn't represent

Why this is called a "sphere," not a "space" The Bloch sphere pictures only the surface — every point on it is a valid pure state, a state about which there is, in principle, complete information. Unit 8 introduces decoherence, where interaction with an uncontrolled environment leaves a qubit in a statistical mixture of pure states rather than one clean pure state — those mixed states correspond to points in the sphere's interior, with the exact center representing "maximally mixed," i.e., as close to a coin flip as a qubit can get. This course works almost entirely with pure states (the surface) until Unit 8 revisits the interior as a picture of real, imperfect hardware.

One more caveat, worth flagging now and returning to in Unit 4: the Bloch sphere is a single-qubit picture. It does not generalize to a simple geometric picture for two or more qubits — entangled states, which Unit 4 introduces, have no analogous clean sphere. The Bloch sphere's elegance is specifically a single-qubit phenomenon.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the Bloch parametrization |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ)sin(θ/2)|1⟩, what state does θ = 0 give, regardless of φ?

At θ = 0, cos(0) = 1 and sin(0) = 0, so |ψ⟩ = |0⟩ regardless of φ — the coefficient multiplying e^(iφ) is exactly zero, so φ never gets a chance to matter.

2. Where on the Bloch sphere do all the equal-weight superpositions of |0⟩ and |1⟩ live?

θ = π/2 gives cos(π/4) = sin(π/4) = 1/√2, an equal-weight superposition for every φ — so the entire equator is exactly this family, distinguished from each other only by relative phase.

3. What does a point in the interior of the Bloch sphere (not on the surface) represent?

Only the surface represents pure states. The interior represents mixed states — statistical blends of pure states that arise from decoherence, a topic Unit 8 develops in full.

4. Fill in the blank: |+⟩ and |−⟩ both sit on the ___ of the Bloch sphere, since both are equal-weight superpositions.

Both have θ = π/2, placing them on the equator — they differ only in φ, 0 for |+⟩ and π for |−⟩.

5. Fill in the blank: the Bloch sphere gives a clean geometric picture of the state space of exactly ___ — it does not generalize simply to two or more entangled qubits.

The Bloch sphere's elegance is a single-qubit phenomenon; Unit 4's entangled states have no analogous simple sphere picture.
