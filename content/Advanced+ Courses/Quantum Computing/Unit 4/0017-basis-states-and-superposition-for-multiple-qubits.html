Basis States and Superposition for Multiple Qubits

Most valid two-qubit states are not tensor products of two single-qubit states at all — this lesson names the general case, and sets up the specific example that makes the gap unmistakable.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 16 built two-qubit states specifically by combining two independent single-qubit states with ⊗. That's not the most general two-qubit state possible — it's a special case. This lesson states the fully general form, and Lesson 18 exhibits a state that provably falls outside the special case entirely.

1. The general two-qubit state

Definition — General Two-Qubit State A general state of two qubits is |Ψ⟩ = c₀₀|00⟩ + c₀₁|01⟩ + c₁₀|10⟩ + c₁₁|11⟩, for any complex coefficients c₀₀, c₀₁, c₁₀, c₁₁ satisfying |c₀₀|² + |c₀₁|² + |c₁₀|² + |c₁₁|² = 1.

This is exactly Lesson 1.3's normalization condition, generalized from 2 coefficients to 4. The Born rule generalizes the same way: measuring both qubits in the computational basis gives outcome "ab" with probability |cab|², for each of the four possible two-bit outcomes 00, 01, 10, 11.

2. Product states are the special case

Definition — Product State A two-qubit state |Ψ⟩ is a product state if it can be written as |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ for some single-qubit states |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩ — that is, if it factors into "qubit 1's state" combined with "qubit 2's state," independently of each other.

Every product state is a valid two-qubit state (Lesson 16 showed this), but the reverse is not automatic: a general |Ψ⟩ has 4 independent complex coefficients (3 real degrees of freedom after normalization and an overall global phase), while a product state |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ is built from only 2 independent single-qubit states (2 real degrees of freedom each, so 4 total, again minus normalization and phase for each factor) — the counting is close, but it turns out product states genuinely don't cover every possibility. Lesson 18 makes this concrete with a specific state and a short direct proof that no factorization exists.

Example — Checking Probabilities Sum to 1 Consider |Ψ⟩ = (1/2)|00⟩ + (1/2)|01⟩ + (1/2)|10⟩ − (1/2)|11⟩. Check normalization: |1/2|² × 4 = 4 × 1/4 = 1. Valid. The Born rule gives P(00) = P(01) = P(10) = P(11) = 1/4 — each of the four outcomes equally likely, regardless of the minus sign on |11⟩ (which is a relative phase, invisible to computational-basis measurement, exactly Lesson 1.5's principle carried over to two qubits).
Why this matters more than it might seem to A general n-qubit state needs 2ⁿ complex coefficients to specify — for even a modest 20 qubits, that's over a million numbers, and product states (n independent single-qubit states, each needing only 2 numbers) cover only a vanishingly small sliver of that space. Almost every state in the full 2ⁿ-dimensional space is not a product state. Lesson 18 gives this non-product phenomenon a name — entanglement — and Lesson 19 works out exactly what physical consequences it does and doesn't have.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. For a general two-qubit state c₀₀|00⟩ + c₀₁|01⟩ + c₁₀|10⟩ + c₁₁|11⟩, what condition must the coefficients satisfy?

Normalization generalizes directly: the squared magnitudes of all four coefficients must sum to 1, matching the four measurement probabilities summing to 1.

2. What is a "product state"?

A product state is defined by being factorable into two independent single-qubit states combined with the tensor product — this has nothing to do with the number of nonzero coefficients or measurement determinism directly.

3. For |Ψ⟩ = (1/2)|00⟩ + (1/2)|01⟩ + (1/2)|10⟩ − (1/2)|11⟩, what is P(11), the probability of measuring both qubits as 1?

P(11) = |−1/2|² = 1/4 — the minus sign is a relative phase, squared away by the Born rule exactly as it would be for one qubit.

4. Fill in the blank: for an n-qubit state with 2ⁿ complex coefficients c, the normalization condition requires the sum of all |c|² to equal ___.

Regardless of how many qubits are involved, the sum of every squared-magnitude coefficient must equal 1, matching the total measurement probability.

5. Fill in the blank: a multi-qubit state that cannot be factored into a tensor product of individual single-qubit states is called ___.

Entanglement is exactly the name for this non-factorizable phenomenon — Lesson 18 exhibits the first concrete example.
