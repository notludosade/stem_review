Tensor Products: Combining Qubit States

Two qubits don't just sit side by side — their combined state lives in a single, larger vector space, built by an operation Lesson 15 already borrowed a piece of. This lesson makes it precise.

← Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

Every lesson so far has lived in ℂ², the state space of one qubit. Real quantum computers use many qubits together, and their combined state is not simply "qubit 1's state, and separately, qubit 2's state" — it's one single vector in a larger space. The operation that builds that larger space is the tensor product.

1. The tensor product of two vectors

Definition — Tensor Product (⊗) For |ψ⟩ = (α, β)ᵀ and |φ⟩ = (γ, δ)ᵀ, their tensor product is the 4-dimensional vector: |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ = (αγ, αδ, βγ, βδ)ᵀ — multiply |φ⟩'s components by each of |ψ⟩'s components in turn, stacking the results.

This is exactly Lesson 15's preview fact confirmed in full: the joint state of two independently prepared qubits, one in |ψ⟩ and one in |φ⟩, is |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩, living in ℂ² ⊗ ℂ² ≅ ℂ⁴.

2. The two-qubit computational basis

Definition — Two-Qubit Basis States Applying the tensor product to pairs of single-qubit basis states gives four basis vectors for ℂ⁴, written with the shorthand |ab⟩ = |a⟩ ⊗ |b⟩:
|00⟩ = (1,0,0,0)ᵀ |01⟩ = (0,1,0,0)ᵀ |10⟩ = (0,0,1,0)ᵀ |11⟩ = (0,0,0,1)ᵀ

Check one directly: |1⟩ ⊗ |0⟩ = (0,1)ᵀ ⊗ (1,0)ᵀ = (0·1, 0·0, 1·1, 1·0)ᵀ = (0,0,1,0)ᵀ = |10⟩, matching the table. These four vectors are orthonormal (you can verify ⟨ab|cd⟩ = 0 unless a=c and b=d), exactly the standard basis of ℂ⁴.

3. Inner products distribute over the tensor product

Fact ⟨ψ ⊗ φ|χ ⊗ ω⟩ = ⟨ψ|χ⟩⟨φ|ω⟩ — the inner product of two tensor-product states factors into the product of the individual inner products. This is exactly the tool Lesson 15's no-cloning proof relied on.

4. Scaling up to n qubits

Why n qubits give 2ⁿ dimensions Each additional qubit multiplies the state space's dimension by 2: one qubit lives in ℂ² (dimension 2), two qubits in ℂ² ⊗ ℂ² (dimension 4), three qubits in ℂ² ⊗ ℂ² ⊗ ℂ² (dimension 8), and n qubits in a 2ⁿ-dimensional space — Lesson 1.1's claim, now built on solid footing. This exponential growth is exactly the resource every quantum algorithm in Units 6 and 7 is built to exploit, and exactly the reason simulating a modest-sized quantum system on a classical computer becomes intractable so quickly: 50 qubits alone already means over 10¹⁵ complex amplitudes to track.
Example — A Product State Apply H to two separate qubits, each starting at |0⟩: (H|0⟩) ⊗ (H|0⟩) = |+⟩ ⊗ |+⟩ = [(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2] ⊗ [(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2]. Expanding using the tensor product's formula: (1/2)(|00⟩ + |01⟩ + |10⟩ + |11⟩) — an equal superposition of all four two-qubit basis states, each with amplitude 1/2. This particular state is called a product state, since it factors cleanly as (one single-qubit state) ⊗ (another single-qubit state). Lesson 18 constructs a state that provably cannot be factored this way at all.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is |0⟩ ⊗ |1⟩ as a column vector?

(1,0)ᵀ ⊗ (0,1)ᵀ = (1·0, 1·1, 0·0, 0·1)ᵀ = (0,1,0,0)ᵀ = |01⟩.

2. For |ψ⟩ = (α, β)ᵀ and |φ⟩ = (γ, δ)ᵀ, what is |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩?

Multiply |φ⟩'s two components by each of |ψ⟩'s components in turn: α·(γ,δ) stacked on top of β·(γ,δ), giving (αγ, αδ, βγ, βδ)ᵀ.

3. How many dimensions does the state space of 4 qubits have?

Each additional qubit doubles the dimension: 2¹, 2², 2³, 2⁴ = 16 for four qubits.

4. Fill in the blank: 3 qubits live in a ___-dimensional vector space.

2³ = 8 — each qubit contributes a factor of 2 to the total dimension.

5. Fill in the blank: the tensor product of two normalized single-qubit states is automatically ___ as a 4-dimensional vector, since ⟨ψ⊗φ|ψ⊗φ⟩ = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩⟨φ|φ⟩ = 1·1 = 1.

Because inner products of tensor products factor, combining two already-normalized states always produces an already-normalized joint state — no extra rescaling needed.
