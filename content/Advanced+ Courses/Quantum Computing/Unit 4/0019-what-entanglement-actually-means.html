What Entanglement Actually Means (and Doesn't Mean)

Entanglement produces correlations stronger than anything classical — but not the instant-communication superpower popular science often implies. This lesson draws the line precisely, with a computation, not just a warning.

← Lesson 18 Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 18 proved |Φ⁺⟩ is entangled. This lesson asks what that actually buys you — starting with a real, measurable correlation, then immediately fencing off the one conclusion that correlation does not license.

1. What entanglement does mean: perfect correlation

Fact — Correlated Outcomes Take two qubits in state |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, held by two separated parties conventionally named Alice (qubit 1) and Bob (qubit 2). If Alice measures her qubit and gets outcome 0, the joint state collapses to |00⟩ — so if Bob then measures his qubit, he is guaranteed to also get 0. Likewise, Alice getting 1 guarantees Bob will get 1. Their outcomes are perfectly correlated, even though each individual outcome, on its own, is completely random (50/50).

This is genuinely strange by classical standards, but it is real and experimentally confirmed. The strangeness is not that the outcomes match — classical systems can be correlated too (two sealed envelopes, prepared in advance, containing matching numbers). The strangeness, addressed below, is that quantum correlations can be demonstrably stronger than any classical pre-agreed-plan could produce.

2. What entanglement does not mean: faster-than-light signaling

Why entanglement cannot send a message However Alice measures her half of |Φ⁺⟩, and whatever Bob does to his half beforehand, Bob's own measurement statistics — considered on their own, without comparing notes with Alice — never change. There is no operation Alice can perform on her qubit alone that Bob can detect purely by measuring his qubit. This is the no-signaling theorem, and it's why entanglement cannot be used to send information faster than light, regardless of how far apart Alice and Bob are.
Example — Bob's Statistics Don't Depend on Alice at All Start from |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. Suppose Bob applies an X gate to his own qubit before anyone measures anything: (I ⊗ X)|Φ⁺⟩ = (|0⟩ ⊗ X|0⟩ + |1⟩ ⊗ X|1⟩)/√2 = (|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2 = |Ψ⁺⟩. Now compute Alice's own marginal measurement probability from this new state: P(Alice gets 0) = |amplitude on |00⟩|² + |amplitude on |01⟩|² = 0 + 1/2 = 1/2. P(Alice gets 1) = |amplitude on |10⟩|² + |amplitude on |11⟩|² = 1/2 + 0 = 1/2. Still exactly 50/50 — identical to before Bob's X gate. Bob's local action changed the correlation pattern (comparing notes afterward, they'd now find their outcomes always disagree instead of always matching) but changed nothing about what Alice sees on her own. That's the general rule: no local operation on one entangled qubit is detectable by measuring only the other qubit.

3. Entanglement is also not "just classical correlation"

It's tempting to conclude entanglement is nothing more than the sealed-envelope analogy — Alice and Bob's qubits were secretly "pre-programmed" to agree, the same way two envelopes could be pre-filled with matching numbers. This turns out to be provably false. Physicist John Stewart Bell showed (in what's now called Bell's theorem) that any theory where the qubits carry pre-agreed, fixed instructions decided in advance — called hidden variables — makes a specific, testable prediction about certain combinations of correlations (a Bell inequality), and that quantum mechanics predicts those correlations can exceed that bound. Real experiments, run many times with sufficient rigor to close off loopholes, confirm quantum mechanics' prediction and violate the classical bound. This course won't derive a Bell inequality in full, but the conclusion is worth carrying forward precisely: entangled correlations are not merely "hidden classical agreement in disguise" — they are a genuinely different, stronger kind of correlation that no classical pre-arranged plan can reproduce.

1. Alice and Bob share |Φ⁺⟩. Alice measures her qubit and gets outcome 1. What happens if Bob then measures his qubit?

Alice's measurement collapses the joint state to |11⟩, so Bob's subsequent measurement is guaranteed to return 1 — the perfect correlation |Φ⁺⟩ guarantees.

2. Why can't Alice use her measurement on an entangled qubit to send Bob an instant message?

No local action Alice takes changes Bob's own measurement statistics on his qubit alone — the correlation is only visible once the two of them compare results through an ordinary (light-speed-limited) classical channel.

3. Bob applies X to his half of |Φ⁺⟩ before Alice measures her qubit. What is Alice's probability of measuring 0?

Bob's local X gate transforms |Φ⁺⟩ into |Ψ⁺⟩, but Alice's own marginal probability of measuring 0 stays exactly 1/2 either way — no local operation on Bob's qubit is detectable in Alice's statistics alone.

4. Fill in the blank: the principle guaranteeing that no local operation on one entangled qubit can be detected by measuring only the other, distant qubit is called the ___ theorem.

The no-signaling theorem is exactly why entanglement can never be used to transmit information faster than light.

5. Fill in the blank: Bell's theorem rules out theories where entangled particles carry fixed, pre-agreed instructions — known as ___ — decided in advance of any measurement.

Hidden variable theories are exactly the classical "pre-programmed envelope" explanation Bell's theorem experimentally rules out.
