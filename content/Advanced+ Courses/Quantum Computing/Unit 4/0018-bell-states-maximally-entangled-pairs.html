Bell States: Maximally Entangled Pairs

Four specific two-qubit states, and a short direct proof that one of them cannot be written as any product of two single-qubit states at all — entanglement, made completely unambiguous.

← Lesson 17 Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 17 claimed most two-qubit states aren't product states, without proving it for any specific example. This lesson fixes that with the four most famous states in quantum information, named for physicist John Stewart Bell.

1. The four Bell states

Definition — Bell States The four Bell states are:
|Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2  |Φ⁻⟩ = (|00⟩ − |11⟩)/√2
|Ψ⁺⟩ = (|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2  |Ψ⁻⟩ = (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2

All four are properly normalized (each has two nonzero coefficients of magnitude 1/√2, so |1/√2|² + |1/√2|² = 1), and they form an orthonormal basis for ℂ⁴ in their own right, called the Bell basis — an alternative to the computational basis {|00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩}.

2. |Φ⁺⟩ is not a product state

Claim |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 cannot be written as |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ for any single-qubit states |ψ⟩ = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩ and |φ⟩ = c|0⟩ + d|1⟩.
Proof Suppose it could: |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ = ac|00⟩ + ad|01⟩ + bc|10⟩ + bd|11⟩ (Lesson 16's formula). Matching coefficients against |Φ⁺⟩ = (1/√2)|00⟩ + 0|01⟩ + 0|10⟩ + (1/√2)|11⟩ gives four equations: ac = 1/√2, ad = 0, bc = 0, bd = 1/√2. From ac = 1/√2, both a ≠ 0 and c ≠ 0 (their product is nonzero). Since a ≠ 0, the equation ad = 0 forces d = 0. But then bd = b·0 = 0, contradicting bd = 1/√2. No values of a, b, c, d satisfy all four equations simultaneously — no factorization exists. ∎
Definition — Entangled State A multi-qubit state that cannot be written as a tensor product of individual single-qubit states is called entangled. All four Bell states are entangled — the same style of proof works for each (try it for |Ψ⁻⟩ as practice).
Why Bell states are called "maximally" entangled Later, more technical courses quantify entanglement with a number (related to how mixed each qubit looks in isolation, once the other is set aside), and the Bell states sit at the extreme end of that scale — as entangled as two qubits can possibly be. This course won't build that full machinery, but the label "maximally entangled" is standard enough to be worth recognizing by name; it signals that Bell states are the canonical, sharpest example of the phenomenon, not just one instance among many equally strong ones.

1. What is |Φ⁺⟩?

|Φ⁺⟩ is the equal superposition of |00⟩ and |11⟩ with a plus sign — the other options are |Ψ⁺⟩, |Φ⁻⟩, and a (non-entangled) product state, respectively.

2. In the proof that |Φ⁺⟩ isn't a product state, why does ac = 1/√2 force both a ≠ 0 and c ≠ 0?

If either a or c were 0, their product ac would be 0 — but ac is required to equal 1/√2, a nonzero number, so neither factor can be 0.

3. What does it mean for a two-qubit state to be "entangled"?

Entanglement is defined precisely as non-factorizability into a tensor product of single-qubit states — nothing about coefficient count, normalization, or determinism directly.

4. Fill in the blank: there are exactly ___ Bell states.

|Φ⁺⟩, |Φ⁻⟩, |Ψ⁺⟩, and |Ψ⁻⟩ — four states, forming an orthonormal basis of the 4-dimensional two-qubit space.

5. Fill in the blank: the four Bell states form an orthonormal basis of the two-qubit space called the ___.

The Bell basis is a valid alternative to the computational basis {|00⟩,|01⟩,|10⟩,|11⟩} — any two-qubit state can be expressed in either basis.
