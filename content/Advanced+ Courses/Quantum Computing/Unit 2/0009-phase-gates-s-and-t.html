Phase Gates: S and T

A whole family of gates that never touch |0⟩ at all — they only ever dial in a relative phase on |1⟩, and one of them turns out to be the missing ingredient for a universal gate set.

← Lesson 8 Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 7's Z gate multiplies |1⟩'s amplitude by exactly −1, leaving |0⟩ untouched. That's one specific case of a much larger family: gates that leave |0⟩ alone and multiply |1⟩'s amplitude by some complex number of magnitude 1. Two members of that family, S and T, come up often enough to deserve their own names.

1. The general phase gate

Definition — Phase Gate Rφ For any real angle φ, Rφ = [[1, 0], [0, eiφ]]. It acts as Rφ|0⟩ = |0⟩ and Rφ|1⟩ = eiφ|1⟩ — |0⟩'s amplitude is untouched, and |1⟩'s amplitude picks up a relative phase of eiφ.

Every Rφ is unitary: Rφ† = [[1,0],[0,e−iφ]], and Rφ†Rφ = [[1,0],[0,e−iφeiφ]] = [[1,0],[0,1]] = I, since e−iφeiφ = e0 = 1 for any real φ.

2. S and T: two specific angles

Definition — S Gate S = Rπ/2 = [[1, 0], [0, i]]. Sometimes called the "phase gate" without further qualification.
Definition — T Gate T = Rπ/4 = [[1, 0], [0, eiπ/4]]. Also called the "π/8 gate" (a historical naming quirk — the angle in the exponent is π/4, but an older convention wrote the gate's effect on the Bloch sphere as a π/4 rotation measured differently, giving it the "π/8" name that stuck).
Example — Composing Phase Gates Applying S twice: S² = [[1,0],[0,i]]·[[1,0],[0,i]] = [[1,0],[0,i²]] = [[1,0],[0,−1]] = Z. Two S gates in a row give exactly the Z gate — consistent with Rπ/2 composed with itself giving Rπ = Z (adding the angles π/2 + π/2 = π). Similarly, T² = Rπ/4 composed with itself gives Rπ/2 = S. This "adding angles" pattern is general: Rφ₁Rφ₂ = Rφ₁+φ₂, since eiφ₁eiφ₂ = ei(φ₁+φ₂).

3. Why S and T don't show up in Z-basis measurement statistics alone

Why these gates are "silent" until you change basis or interfere Exactly like Z, every Rφ only ever changes a relative phase — it never changes |α|² or |β|², so measuring in the computational basis right after applying S or T shows nothing different happened at all. This can make S and T feel pointless in isolation. They aren't: Lesson 13 shows that measuring in a different basis (built using H, from Lesson 8) makes relative phase directly visible, and Units 6 and 7's algorithms are built almost entirely out of steering relative phases with gates like these so that a later Hadamard turns invisible phase differences into visible, biased measurement probabilities. T in particular will reappear in Unit 5's universal gate set — {H, T, CNOT} together can approximate any quantum circuit at all, a fact that would be false if T were dropped from the toolkit.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does the S gate do to the state |1⟩?

S = R_(π/2) multiplies |1⟩'s amplitude by e^(iπ/2) = i, while leaving |0⟩'s amplitude untouched.

2. What is S², the S gate applied twice?

S = R_(π/2), so S² = R_(π/2+π/2) = R_π = Z — two quarter-turns of relative phase add up to Z's half-turn.

3. Why is measuring in the computational basis unable to detect whether an S or T gate was just applied?

Like Z, both S and T are pure relative-phase gates — they multiply |1⟩'s amplitude by a magnitude-1 complex number, which never changes any squared magnitude, and hence never changes computational-basis measurement probabilities.

4. Fill in the blank: T² (the T gate applied twice) equals the ___ gate.

T = R_(π/4), so T² = R_(π/4+π/4) = R_(π/2) = S — the phase angles add.

5. Fill in the blank: like Z, both S and T are pure ___-phase gates — they never change a computational-basis measurement probability by themselves.

Relative phase is exactly what these gates manipulate — invisible in the computational basis alone, but essential once combined with other gates or a change of measurement basis.
