The Pauli Gates: X, Y, and Z

Three matrices, one for each Bloch-sphere axis — the quantum NOT, the quantum phase flip, and the gate that does a bit of both.

← Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

The three Pauli gates, named for physicist Wolfgang Pauli, are the single most important trio of matrices in this course. They're small enough to memorize, unitary (you can check each one against Lesson 6's definition), and every one of them is its own inverse — applying any Pauli gate twice in a row does nothing at all.

1. The X gate — bit flip

Definition — Pauli X X = [[0, 1], [1, 0]]. Acting on the basis states: X|0⟩ = |1⟩, and X|1⟩ = |0⟩.

X swaps the two basis states — it's exactly the quantum version of the classical NOT gate, and it's often just called the bit-flip gate for that reason. On a general state, X(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) = α|1⟩ + β|0⟩ — the amplitudes swap places.

2. The Z gate — phase flip

Definition — Pauli Z Z = [[1, 0], [0, −1]]. Acting on the basis states: Z|0⟩ = |0⟩, and Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩.

Z leaves |0⟩ completely alone and multiplies |1⟩'s amplitude by −1. On a general state, Z(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) = α|0⟩ − β|1⟩. Notice this does not change the Born-rule probabilities in the computational basis — |α|² and |−β|² = |β|² are the same as before. Z is invisible if you only ever measure in the Z (computational) basis; it's a pure relative-phase operation, in exactly Lesson 1.5's sense, which Lesson 13 will show becomes very visible once you measure in a different basis.

3. The Y gate — both at once

Definition — Pauli Y Y = [[0, −i], [i, 0]]. Acting on the basis states: Y|0⟩ = i|1⟩, and Y|1⟩ = −i|0⟩.

Y swaps the basis states like X does, but also multiplies by ±i along the way — it combines a bit flip and a phase-like factor in one gate. (Up to an overall phase, Y = iXZ; you can verify this by direct matrix multiplication if you'd like the algebra to confirm it.)

Example — Checking Y Is Unitary Y† = conjugate transpose of [[0,−i],[i,0]] is [[0,−i],[i,0]] itself here (conjugating flips the sign of each i, and transposing swaps the off-diagonal positions, which for this particular matrix returns the same matrix). So Y†Y = Y·Y. Direct computation: Y² = [[(0)(0)+(−i)(i), (0)(−i)+(−i)(0)], [(i)(0)+(0)(i), (i)(−i)+(0)(0)]] = [[1, 0], [0, 1]] = I. Y is unitary, and it's its own inverse.

4. All three are self-inverse

Fact X² = Y² = Z² = I. Every Pauli gate undoes itself: apply any one of them twice, and you're back where you started.
Why these are called "rotations" of the Bloch sphere Geometrically, X, Y, and Z each act as a 180° (π) rotation of the Bloch sphere about their own axis — X about the x-axis, Y about the y-axis, Z about the z-axis, using the sphere's natural (x,y,z) coordinates (with |0⟩ and |1⟩ sitting at the north and south poles along z). This is why applying any one twice returns you to your starting point: two 180° turns about the same axis is a full 360° turn, which is the identity. The full derivation uses a rotation-generator formalism beyond this course's scope, but the picture — three perpendicular 180° flips — is worth carrying forward, since Unit 2's circuits are really just sequences of small rotations of a point on this same sphere.

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does the X gate do to a general state α|0⟩ + β|1⟩?

X = [[0,1],[1,0]] swaps the two components of the state vector, exactly matching the classical NOT gate's role of flipping a bit.

2. Why is the Z gate invisible if you only ever measure in the computational (Z) basis?

Z multiplies only the |1⟩ amplitude by −1 — a relative phase change that leaves both squared magnitudes, and hence both computational-basis probabilities, exactly as they were.

3. What is Y|1⟩?

Y = [[0,−i],[i,0]] applied to (0,1)ᵀ gives (−i·1, 0·1)ᵀ = (−i, 0)ᵀ = −i|0⟩.

4. Fill in the blank: because it swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩, the X gate is often called the quantum ___ gate.

X is the direct quantum analogue of the classical NOT gate — both flip a bit's value.

5. Fill in the blank: for every Pauli gate, applying it twice in a row gives the ___ matrix — X² = Y² = Z² = ___.

Every Pauli gate is its own inverse, so squaring any of them returns the identity — geometrically, two 180° Bloch-sphere rotations about the same axis make a full turn.
