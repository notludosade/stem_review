1. Is M = [[1, 1], [1, 1]] a valid quantum gate?

M is real and symmetric, so M† = M. Computing M² gives [[2,2],[2,2]], not the identity matrix — M fails the unitarity test and could not preserve normalization for every input.

2. Why does every valid quantum gate have an inverse that is also a valid quantum gate?

Unitarity directly supplies U⁻¹ = U†, and the conjugate transpose of a unitary matrix is itself unitary — so undoing any gate is always possible with another valid gate.

3. What is X|1⟩?

X = [[0,1],[1,0]] swaps the two basis states, so X|1⟩ = |0⟩.

4. For |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩, answer both parts about Z|ψ⟩:

(a) What does Z do to the |0⟩ component?

(b) Fill in the blank: Z|ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (___)|1⟩.

(a) Z leaves the |0⟩ amplitude untouched. (b) Z multiplies only the |1⟩ amplitude by −1, giving −4/5.

5. What is Y|0⟩?

Y = [[0,−i],[i,0]] applied to (1,0)ᵀ gives (0, i)ᵀ = i|1⟩.

6. Fill in the blank: for any of the three Pauli gates P (X, Y, or Z), P² = ___.

Every Pauli gate is its own inverse, so applying any one of them twice always gives the identity matrix.

7. Answer both parts about the Hadamard gate:

(a) What is H|1⟩?

(b) Fill in the blank: measuring H|1⟩ in the computational basis gives outcome 0 with probability ___.

(a) H|1⟩ = |−⟩. (b) |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 gives |1/√2|² = 1/2 probability for each outcome.

8. What is H², the Hadamard gate applied twice?

H is its own inverse: H² = I, so applying it twice returns any state to exactly where it started.

9. Answer both parts:

(a) What is T², the T gate applied twice?

(b) Fill in the blank: S² = ___.

(a) T = R_(π/4), so T² = R_(π/2) = S. (b) S = R_(π/2), so S² = R_π = Z.

10. What is the final state of the circuit |0⟩ → Z → H → measure?

Z|0⟩ = |0⟩ (unchanged, since Z only affects |1⟩'s amplitude), then H|0⟩ = |+⟩ — trace gates left to right in the order they're applied.
