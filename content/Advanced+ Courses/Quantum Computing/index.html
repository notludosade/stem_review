Quantum Computing

Qubits, superposition, entanglement, and quantum gates — the linear algebra of computing built on a fundamentally different physical model, from a single qubit's state vector to three real algorithms and the hardware racing to run them at scale. Requires Linear Algebra A and Linear Algebra B.

Unit 1 — Qubits & Superposition
Lesson 1

Classical Bits vs. Qubits

A classical bit is a switch that is definitely on or definitely off. A qubit is a vector — and that single change is the whole reason quantum computing needs Linear Algebra A and B as prerequisites.

 Lesson 2

The Qubit State Vector and Ket Notation

Physicists write vectors with a strange-looking bracket, |·⟩, instead of the bold v or arrow-v from Linear Algebra A. It's still just a column vector — the notation is doing real work.

 Lesson 3

Superposition and Normalization

Not every vector in ℂ² is a valid qubit state — exactly one constraint singles out the physical ones, and it's the same constraint that will let Lesson 11 turn amplitudes into probabilities.

 Lesson 4

The Bloch Sphere

Every single-qubit state, up to one physically invisible piece of information, is a single point on the surface of an ordinary sphere in ℝ³.

 Lesson 5

Global Phase vs. Relative Phase

Two different-looking state vectors can be the exact same physical state — the distinction between the phase that never matters and the phase that always matters closes out this unit's dimension count.

Unit 2 — Quantum Gates I
Lesson 6

Quantum Gates as Unitary Matrices

A quantum gate is just a matrix-vector product — but not every matrix is allowed to be one, and the restriction that qualifies a matrix is exactly the property Linear Algebra B called unitary.

 Lesson 7

The Pauli Gates: X, Y, and Z

Three matrices, one for each Bloch-sphere axis — the quantum NOT, the quantum phase flip, and the gate that does a bit of both.

 Lesson 8

The Hadamard Gate

One matrix turns a definite classical-looking bit into a perfect 50/50 superposition — and nearly every quantum algorithm in Units 6 and 7 opens by applying it.

 Lesson 9

Phase Gates: S and T

A whole family of gates that never touch |0⟩ at all — they only ever dial in a relative phase on |1⟩, and one of them turns out to be the missing ingredient for a universal gate set.

 Lesson 10

Single-Qubit Circuit Diagrams

A quantum circuit reads left to right like a sentence, but computes right to left like a matrix product — mixing up that order is the single most common bug in reading a diagram.

Unit 3 — Measurement & Probability
Lesson 11

The Born Rule

The single rule connecting the abstract amplitudes of Unit 1 to something you can actually observe: a probability.

 Lesson 12

Measurement Collapse and Why Measurement Is Destructive

Lesson 11 said what probability you get. This lesson says what happens to the qubit the instant afterward — the single biggest constraint every quantum algorithm has to work around.

 Lesson 13

Measuring in Different Bases

The same physical qubit gives completely different-looking statistics depending on which orthonormal basis you choose to measure it in — and there's a one-gate trick for doing it.

 Lesson 14

Expectation Values

One real number that summarizes an entire measurement's statistics at once — computed directly from the state vector, with no explicit probability list required.

 Lesson 15

The No-Cloning Theorem

Lesson 12 showed measurement destroys a state. This lesson closes off the obvious workaround — "just copy the qubit first" — with a short, clean proof straight from unitarity.

Unit 4 — Multi-Qubit Systems & Entanglement
Lesson 16

Tensor Products: Combining Qubit States

Two qubits don't just sit side by side — their combined state lives in a single, larger vector space, built by an operation Lesson 15 already borrowed a piece of.

 Lesson 17

Basis States and Superposition for Multiple Qubits

Most valid two-qubit states are not tensor products of two single-qubit states at all — this lesson names the general case and sets up the gap Lesson 18 makes unmistakable.

 Lesson 18

Bell States: Maximally Entangled Pairs

Four specific two-qubit states, and a short direct proof that one of them cannot be written as any product of two single-qubit states at all.

 Lesson 19

What Entanglement Actually Means (and Doesn't Mean)

Entanglement produces correlations stronger than anything classical — but not the instant-communication superpower popular science often implies.

 Lesson 20

Measuring Entangled Qubits

The precise mechanics of measuring just one qubit out of a multi-qubit state — a side-by-side computation showing exactly what entanglement adds that a product state never has.

Lesson 21

Every gate in Unit 2 acted on one qubit alone. This one acts on two — and it's the single gate that turns superposition into entanglement.

 Lesson 22

Building Bell States with H and CNOT

Two gates, applied to an ordinary two-bit input, produce all four Bell states on demand — the standard circuit every entanglement-based protocol starts with.

 Lesson 23

Other Multi-Qubit Gates: CZ, SWAP, and Toffoli

One of these three finally answers Lesson 6's open question about whether classical AND has any quantum equivalent at all.

 Lesson 24

Reading and Building Quantum Circuits

One longer circuit, mixing single- and multi-qubit gates, traced completely from |00⟩ to a final measurement — everything this unit has built, in one worked example.

 Lesson 25

Universal Gate Sets

A real quantum computer needs just a small, fixed toolkit that can build (or approximate) any gate at all. This closing lesson names that toolkit.

Unit 6 — Quantum Algorithms I
Lesson 26

Quantum Parallelism

One quantum query can evaluate a function on every input at once — a remarkable fact that, on its own, buys you nothing at all. This lesson shows exactly why.

 Lesson 27

Interference and Phase Kickback

One trick turns "f's value" into a relative phase instead of a measured bit — and a relative phase can be steered by later gates into a genuinely useful interference pattern.

 Lesson 28

The Deutsch Algorithm: A One-Qubit Warm-Up

The smallest possible quantum algorithm that provably beats every classical approach — three gates, one query, a guaranteed correct answer every single time.

 Lesson 29

The Deutsch-Jozsa Algorithm

Lesson 28's exact circuit, scaled up to n input bits — and the "2 versus 1" query gap turns into "exponential versus 1."

 Lesson 30

Query Complexity: Why Deutsch-Jozsa Is a Real Speedup

The precise sense in which "quantum beats classical" was just proven — and an honest account of exactly how far that proof does, and doesn't, reach.
