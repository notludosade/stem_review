Physical Qubit Platforms

Every lesson so far has worked with "a qubit" in the abstract. This lesson surveys what a qubit actually is, physically, across the several genuinely different technologies currently competing to build one at scale.

← Lesson 37 Lesson 39 → Contents Glossary →

Nothing in Units 1 through 7 depended on which physical system realizes a qubit — that was the point of building everything on linear algebra first. This lesson catches up on the physics, surveying the leading approaches without picking a winner (there isn't a settled one yet).

1. Superconducting circuits

Superconducting Qubits Tiny circuits built from Josephson junctions, cooled to millikelvin temperatures (colder than deep space) in a dilution refrigerator, where they exhibit discrete, well-separated quantum energy levels usable as |0⟩ and |1⟩. Gates are applied with microwave pulses. This is the approach used by IBM and Google, among others, and currently has some of the fastest gate speeds of any platform, at the cost of requiring extreme, continuous cooling infrastructure and comparatively short coherence times.

2. Trapped ions

Trapped-Ion Qubits Individual charged atoms (ions), held in place by electromagnetic fields and manipulated with precisely tuned lasers, with |0⟩ and |1⟩ encoded in two of the ion's internal energy levels. Used by companies including IonQ and Quantinuum. Trapped ions typically offer longer coherence times and higher gate fidelities than superconducting circuits, at the cost of slower gate speeds and greater engineering complexity in scaling up the number of ions held and controlled simultaneously.

3. Photonic qubits

Photonic Qubits Qubits encoded directly in properties of light — polarization, or path a photon takes through an optical circuit. Pursued by companies including Xanadu and PsiQuantum. Photonic qubits have a notable advantage: they don't require the extreme cooling superconducting circuits do, and they travel naturally along optical fiber, which is attractive for networking quantum computers together. The tradeoff is that reliably generating, routing, and detecting single photons, and making them interact with each other for two-qubit gates, is its own significant engineering challenge.

4. Neutral atoms

Neutral-Atom Qubits Individual (electrically neutral) atoms held in place by tightly focused laser beams called optical tweezers, arranged in programmable 2D or 3D grids. Pursued by companies including QuEra, Pasqal, and Atom Computing. This approach has drawn attention for its flexible qubit connectivity (tweezers can be physically rearranged) and for scaling to large qubit counts relatively directly, though gate speeds and error rates are still maturing relative to the more established platforms above.

5. Topological qubits — still experimental

Why topological qubits are worth naming even though they don't exist reliably yet Microsoft has pursued a fundamentally different approach: topological qubits, which would encode quantum information in a way intrinsically protected from local noise by the mathematical topology of exotic quasiparticle states, rather than relying entirely on the active error correction Lesson 37 described. If it can be made to work reliably at scale, the appeal is qubits with dramatically better native error resistance, reducing the error-correction overhead every other platform needs. As of this course's writing, topological qubits remain experimental and unproven at scale — worth knowing about as a genuinely different bet on solving Unit 8's noise problem, not as a working technology today.
The Honest Summary No platform is a clear overall winner. Each makes a different tradeoff among coherence time, gate speed, gate fidelity, qubit connectivity, and scalability — and it remains genuinely unresolved which approach (or which combination) will ultimately dominate large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computing. This is an active, fast-moving engineering race, not a settled question, and specific numeric comparisons between platforms tend to become outdated within a year or two as each technology improves.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which platform uses Josephson junctions cooled to millikelvin temperatures?

Superconducting qubits are built from Josephson junctions and require extreme cooling to exhibit usable quantum behavior.

2. Which platform manipulates individual charged atoms using precisely tuned lasers?

Trapped-ion systems hold charged atoms with electromagnetic fields and control them with laser pulses.

3. What is a key advantage of photonic qubits over superconducting circuits?

Avoiding the need for dilution-refrigerator-level cooling and being naturally compatible with optical fiber networking are photonic qubits' distinguishing advantages — they still face their own engineering challenges elsewhere.

4. Fill in the blank: ___ qubits aim to encode information with intrinsic protection from noise, based on the mathematical topology of exotic quasiparticle states, and remain experimental and unproven at scale.

Topological qubits are Microsoft's distinctive bet on solving the noise problem through intrinsic protection rather than active error correction alone.

5. Fill in the blank: qubits built from individual atoms held in programmable grids by optical tweezers are called ___ qubits.

Neutral-atom platforms are notable for flexible, physically reconfigurable qubit connectivity via optical tweezers.
