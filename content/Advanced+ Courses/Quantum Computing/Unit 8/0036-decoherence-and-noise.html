Decoherence and Noise

Every lesson through Unit 7 assumed perfect qubits and perfect gates. Real hardware has neither — this unit takes that assumption away and looks at what's actually true.

← Lesson 35 Lesson 37 → Contents Glossary →

Units 1 through 7 treated qubit states as isolated mathematical objects, evolving under exact unitary gates. Real qubits are physical systems, sitting in a lab, unavoidably in contact with their surroundings — and that contact is the entire subject of this lesson.

1. Decoherence

Definition — Decoherence Decoherence is the loss of a qubit's quantum superposition and phase information due to unwanted interaction with its environment — stray electromagnetic fields, thermal vibrations, imperfect shielding, or any other uncontrolled physical coupling the qubit wasn't supposed to have. Left alone long enough, a qubit initially in a clean pure state (Unit 1's Bloch sphere surface) drifts toward the mixed-state interior Lesson 4 mentioned — its coherent superposition degrading into something closer to a classical statistical mixture.

Decoherence isn't a single well-defined process — it's a catch-all for however the environment happens to leak information out of (or noise into) the qubit. What matters practically is how fast it happens, measured by two standard timescales.

2. T1 and T2

Definition — T1 (Relaxation Time) T1 is the characteristic time for a qubit prepared in |1⟩ to decay toward |0⟩ (the lower-energy state) due to unwanted energy loss to the environment. It measures how long a qubit's basic energy-level information survives.
Definition — T2 (Dephasing Time) T2 is the characteristic time for a qubit's relative phase — Lesson 1.5's crucial quantity, the one interference algorithms in Units 6 and 7 depend on entirely — to randomize due to environmental interaction. T2 is always less than or equal to 2×T1, and in practice is often the more restrictive limit, since relative phase is a more fragile quantity than which basis state a qubit is closer to.

Both are measured in real physical time — typically microseconds to a few milliseconds on today's leading hardware platforms, though exact values vary substantially by platform and continue to improve with better engineering (Lesson 38 surveys the platforms).

3. Gate errors

Why "just run a longer circuit" doesn't scale naively Beyond decoherence during idle time, every physical gate applied to a real qubit is an imperfect approximation of its ideal unitary matrix — a control pulse might be very slightly mistimed or mistuned, introducing a small unintended rotation alongside the intended one. These small errors don't cancel out; they compound. A circuit with 100 gates, each individually correct 99.9% of the time, has a much worse than 99.9% chance of being entirely correct overall — errors accumulate roughly with circuit depth (the number of sequential gate layers) and qubit count together. This is exactly why Unit 7's algorithms, entirely correct on paper, are not simply "ready to run" on today's hardware for large, cryptographically relevant problem sizes: Shor's algorithm on a 2048-bit number needs a circuit far too deep for current error rates to survive without correction.
Putting the Numbers Together Suppose a qubit has T2 = 100 microseconds, and each gate takes 50 nanoseconds. In principle, roughly 2,000 gates could run within one coherence time before phase information is largely lost — a real, meaningful constraint on how deep an uncorrected circuit can be before the answer becomes unreliable. Lesson 37 introduces the standard strategy for pushing past this limit: quantum error correction.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is decoherence?

Decoherence is specifically about unwanted environmental interaction degrading a qubit's quantum information over time — not an intentional operation.

2. What does T1 measure?

T1 (relaxation time) tracks energy loss specifically — a qubit's tendency to decay toward its lower-energy state over time.

3. Why does relative phase (measured by T2) tend to be more fragile than basic energy-level information (measured by T1)?

T2 ≤ 2×T1, and phase information — the exact quantity every interference-based algorithm in Units 6 and 7 depends on — is generally the first thing lost to environmental noise.

4. Fill in the blank: gate errors accumulate as circuit ___ (the number of sequential gate layers) increases, making very long circuits unreliable on noisy hardware.

Circuit depth is the standard term for how many sequential layers of gates a circuit contains — errors compound as this grows.

5. Fill in the blank: unwanted interaction between a qubit and its environment, or imperfections in gate control, is broadly referred to as ___.

Noise is the general term covering both decoherence and gate-level imperfections — the umbrella problem this whole unit addresses.
