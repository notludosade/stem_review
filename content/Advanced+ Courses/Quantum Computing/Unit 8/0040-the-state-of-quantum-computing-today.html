The State of Quantum Computing Today

This closing lesson takes everything Unit 8 has been honest about — noise, error correction's overhead, hardware still maturing — and uses it to give a grounded answer to the question this whole course opened with: where does quantum computing actually stand?

← Lesson 39 Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1.1 promised that a qubit's exponential state space is "the resource every quantum algorithm is trying to exploit." Units 6 and 7 delivered on that promise mathematically. Unit 8 has spent five lessons on the honest complication: exploiting that resource at meaningful scale requires hardware that doesn't fully exist yet. This final lesson puts both halves of that story together.

1. Where hardware actually stands

A Grounded Snapshot As of this course's writing, the field's leading processors have on the order of dozens to a few hundred physical, noisy qubits — squarely in Lesson 39's NISQ era. Genuine fault-tolerant, error-corrected logical qubits, of the kind Lesson 37's threshold theorem shows are eventually achievable, have been demonstrated only in small-scale proof-of-concept experiments, not yet at a size or reliability useful for running Shor's algorithm on a cryptographically significant number or any other large-scale fault-tolerant algorithm. Getting from today's NISQ devices to that future is an active, well-funded, and genuinely uncertain engineering effort — not a solved problem awaiting only patience.

2. Quantum advantage: a real but contested milestone

Why even landmark claims in this field are contested, and that's a healthy sign In 2019, Google reported a "quantum supremacy" (now more often called quantum advantage) result with its Sycamore processor: a specific, somewhat artificial computational task performed on a quantum device, claimed to be infeasible for any classical supercomputer to replicate in a reasonable time. The claim was immediately, publicly scrutinized — researchers subsequently developed improved classical algorithms and simulation techniques that narrowed or disputed the exact scale of the claimed advantage. This back-and-forth is not a sign the field is unreliable; it's exactly how a fast-moving, high-stakes research area is supposed to work — bold claims get checked, refined, and sometimes partially walked back, and the genuine milestones that survive that scrutiny (later, larger quantum advantage demonstrations have followed, on different tasks) are more trustworthy for having been tested this hard.

3. What's already true, and what still isn't

Closing the Loop Back to Lesson 1 Everything Units 1 through 7 built — states, gates, measurement, entanglement, and three real algorithms — is mathematically solid today, not a future promise: it's already the working theoretical foundation researchers use to design new algorithms, understand quantum systems, and push hardware development forward, including one of the most active and best-funded areas of applied linear algebra and physics in the world right now. What isn't yet true is the large-scale, fault-tolerant, cryptographically-relevant hardware Lesson 34's cryptography discussion depends on, or the kind of broadly deployed, unambiguous practical advantage the more breathless popular coverage sometimes implies already exists. Both things are simultaneously accurate: the theory is real and rigorous, built from nothing but linear algebra applied to a genuinely different physical model of computation — and the hardware built to run it at full scale is still being built.

1. What best describes the current scale of leading quantum hardware, as this course describes it?

Today's leading processors sit in the NISQ regime — real, working, and useful for research, but far short of the scale needed for large fault-tolerant algorithms.

2. Why does this lesson treat the scrutiny and pushback on Google's 2019 quantum supremacy claim as a healthy sign, not a failure?

Public scrutiny and follow-up classical algorithms narrowing or challenging the claimed margin is normal, healthy scientific process — not evidence the underlying field is unreliable.

3. Which statement best captures this lesson's closing assessment of quantum computing?

Both halves are true simultaneously: rigorous, already-useful theory, and hardware that is real, actively improving, but not yet at the scale needed for the most consequential applications like Shor's algorithm at cryptographic scale.

4. Fill in the blank: a demonstration that a quantum computer solved some specific task faster than any feasible classical computer could replicate is called a ___ result.

Quantum advantage (formerly more often called quantum supremacy) is the standard term for this kind of milestone claim.

5. Fill in the blank: this entire course was built from nothing but ___, applied to a fundamentally different physical model of computation.

Every result in this course — state vectors, unitary gates, Born-rule probabilities, entanglement, and all three algorithms — traces back to the linear algebra of Linear Algebra A and B, applied to qubits instead of ordinary vectors.
