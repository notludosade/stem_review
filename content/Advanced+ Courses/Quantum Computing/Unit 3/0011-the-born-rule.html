The Born Rule

The single rule connecting the abstract amplitudes of Unit 1 to something you can actually observe: a probability. Everything Unit 1 built toward normalization was quietly setting this rule up.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Every lesson so far has manipulated state vectors and gates without ever asking the practical question: what do you actually see when you look at a qubit? The answer is not "you see α and β directly" — amplitudes are never observed. What you see is one of a discrete set of outcomes, with a probability the amplitudes determine. That connection is the Born rule, named for physicist Max Born, and it is one of the foundational postulates of quantum mechanics — not something derived from anything more basic, but an assumption confirmed by every experiment ever run to test it.

1. The rule

Postulate — The Born Rule For a qubit in state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, measuring in the computational basis gives outcome 0 with probability |α|², and outcome 1 with probability |β|². More generally, for measurement in any orthonormal basis {|e₀⟩, |e₁⟩}, the probability of outcome i is |⟨eᵢ|ψ⟩|².

Lesson 1.3's normalization condition, |α|² + |β|² = 1, is now revealed for what it always was: the requirement that these two probabilities add up to 1, exactly as any two mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes must. Every valid qubit state automatically comes with a well-defined probability distribution over measurement outcomes, no extra bookkeeping required.

Example |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4i/5)|1⟩. P(0) = |3/5|² = 9/25. P(1) = |4i/5|² = |4/5|² · |i|² = 16/25 · 1 = 16/25. Check: 9/25 + 16/25 = 25/25 = 1. Notice the phase of 4i/5 (relative to 3/5) played no role at all in these probabilities — only the magnitude |4/5| mattered. This is the Born rule directly reflecting Lesson 1.5's fact that a single measurement in one fixed basis cannot see relative phase; Lesson 13 shows how changing the measurement basis brings it back into view.

2. General-basis measurement, briefly

The formula |⟨eᵢ|ψ⟩|² is what makes the Born rule work for any choice of measurement basis, not just the computational one. |⟨0|ψ⟩|² = |α|² and |⟨1|ψ⟩|² = |β|² recover the special case above, since ⟨0|ψ⟩ = α and ⟨1|ψ⟩ = β directly (Lesson 2's orthonormality trick). Lesson 13 puts this general formula to work with the |+⟩/|−⟩ basis specifically.

Why the Born rule is stated as a postulate, not proved Quantum mechanics is built from a short list of postulates — statements accepted as starting points because every experimental test of their consequences has confirmed them, not because they're derived from something even more basic. The Born rule is one of these starting points. This isn't unusual for a physical theory: Newton's laws, too, are postulates justified by matching experiment, not proved from pure logic. What is provable, and what this course spends real effort on, is everything that follows once you accept the Born rule — normalization requirements, measurement statistics, and, starting in Unit 6, the entire logic of why certain quantum algorithms outperform every classical one.

1. For |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ measured in the computational basis, what is the probability of measuring outcome 0?

The Born rule states P(0) = |α|², the squared magnitude of the amplitude on |0⟩ — not the amplitude itself, and not its plain magnitude without squaring.

2. For |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)i|1⟩, what is P(1), the probability of measuring outcome 1?

P(1) = |(1/2)i|² = (1/2)² · |i|² = 1/4 · 1 = 1/4. (Check: P(0) = |√3/2|² = 3/4, and 3/4 + 1/4 = 1.)

3. Why is the Born rule described as a "postulate" rather than something proved from more basic principles?

Like Newton's laws in classical mechanics, the Born rule is a foundational postulate — justified by matching every experimental test, not proved from pure logic or more basic axioms.

4. Fill in the blank: for any valid qubit state, P(0) + P(1) = ___, which is exactly why normalization was required in Unit 1.

|α|² + |β|² = 1 is normalization, restated as "these two probabilities sum to 1" — exactly what mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes require.

5. Fill in the blank: the rule stating that measurement probabilities equal squared amplitude magnitudes is called the ___ rule.

Named for physicist Max Born, who first proposed this probabilistic interpretation of the wavefunction's amplitudes.
