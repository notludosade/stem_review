Measuring in Different Bases

The same physical qubit gives completely different-looking statistics depending on which orthonormal basis you choose to measure it in — and there's a one-gate trick for doing it.

← Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Every measurement so far has used the computational basis, {|0⟩, |1⟩}. That was a choice, not a requirement. Lesson 11's general Born rule, P(i) = |⟨eᵢ|ψ⟩|², works for any orthonormal basis {|e₀⟩, |e₁⟩} — and switching bases is exactly how Lesson 1.5's invisible relative phase finally becomes visible.

1. The X basis

Definition — The X Basis The X basis (also called the Hadamard basis) is {|+⟩, |−⟩} — Unit 1's two named equal-weight superposition states. It's a perfectly valid orthonormal basis for ℂ² in its own right: ⟨+|+⟩ = ⟨−|−⟩ = 1 and ⟨+|−⟩ = 0, exactly as required.

"Measuring in the X basis" means treating |+⟩ and |−⟩ as the two possible outcomes, the same way |0⟩ and |1⟩ were the two outcomes for the computational (Z) basis.

2. The measurement-basis trick

Fact — Measuring in the X Basis via H Since H|+⟩ = |0⟩ and H|−⟩ = |1⟩ (Lesson 8's self-inverse property applied to |+⟩ = H|0⟩ and |−⟩ = H|1⟩), measuring |ψ⟩ in the X basis is exactly equivalent to first applying H, then measuring in the computational basis: the "+" outcome corresponds to the computational-basis 0 outcome after H, and "−" corresponds to 1.

This is how X-basis measurement is actually implemented on real hardware, which almost always only measures directly in one fixed basis (usually the computational one) — apply H first, then do the ordinary measurement.

Example — Same State, Different Bases Take |ψ⟩ = |+⟩. Z-basis measurement: P(0) = |⟨0|+⟩|² = |1/√2|² = 1/2, P(1) = 1/2 — a coin flip. X-basis measurement: P(+) = |⟨+|+⟩|² = |1|² = 1, P(−) = |⟨−|+⟩|² = 0 — completely deterministic. The exact same physical state looks like maximal uncertainty in one basis and perfect certainty in another. Now take |ψ⟩ = |0⟩ instead: Z-basis gives P(0) = 1 (certain), while X-basis gives P(+) = |⟨+|0⟩|² = |1/√2|² = 1/2 — the situation is exactly reversed.
Why this finally makes relative phase visible Lesson 11's computational-basis Born rule couldn't distinguish |+⟩ from |−⟩ — both gave a 50/50 split. But X-basis measurement tells them apart instantly: |+⟩ gives P(+) = 1, while |−⟩ gives P(−) = 1 (by the same computation with a sign flip). The relative phase that was invisible in one basis becomes maximally informative in another — this basis-dependence is precisely the mechanism every interference-based quantum algorithm in Units 6 and 7 exploits: steer information into relative phase using gates, then choose a measurement basis (via a final Hadamard, typically) that turns that phase into a biased, informative measurement outcome.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What circuit operation, applied before an ordinary computational-basis measurement, is equivalent to directly measuring in the X basis?

Since H|+⟩ = |0⟩ and H|−⟩ = |1⟩, applying H before a standard computational-basis measurement exactly reproduces X-basis measurement statistics.

2. A qubit in state |+⟩ is measured in the X basis. What is the outcome?

P(+) = |⟨+|+⟩|² = 1 — measuring |+⟩ in its own basis is completely deterministic, exactly parallel to measuring |0⟩ in the computational basis.

3. A qubit in state |0⟩ is measured in the X basis. What is P(+), the probability of outcome "+"?

P(+) = |⟨+|0⟩|² = |1/√2|² = 1/2 — a state that's perfectly certain in the Z basis becomes a coin flip in the X basis, the mirror image of measuring |+⟩ in the Z basis.

4. Fill in the blank: applying H before a computational-basis measurement is equivalent to measuring directly in the ___ basis.

The X basis is exactly {|+⟩, |−⟩}, and H maps these to |0⟩ and |1⟩ respectively, which is why prepending H reproduces X-basis measurement.

5. Fill in the blank: |⟨−|0⟩|² = ___.

⟨−|0⟩ = 1/√2 (the |0⟩-coefficient of |−⟩'s bra), so |⟨−|0⟩|² = 1/2 — |0⟩ splits evenly in the X basis, just like |+⟩ splits evenly in the Z basis.
