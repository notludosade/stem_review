Measurement Collapse and Why Measurement Is Destructive

Lesson 11 said what probability you get. This lesson says what happens to the qubit the instant afterward — and it's the single biggest constraint every quantum algorithm in this course has to work around.

← Lesson 11 Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

The Born rule tells you the odds. It doesn't tell you what the qubit is doing a moment after you measure it. That's a second, separate postulate — and it's the reason a qubit's rich, continuous superposition is so hard to actually use for anything.

1. Collapse

Postulate — Measurement Collapse If a measurement of |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ in the computational basis returns outcome i, the qubit's state immediately becomes |i⟩ — exactly the corresponding basis state, with probability 1 of returning that same outcome i if measured again right away. All information about the other amplitude is gone.

This is not a statement about your knowledge changing — it's a claim that the physical state itself changes, discontinuously, at the moment of measurement. Before measuring |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩, the qubit genuinely carries both amplitudes. The instant a measurement returns outcome 0, the qubit is now, physically, in state |0⟩ — the 4/5 amplitude on |1⟩ isn't hiding somewhere; it's simply gone.

2. Why a single measurement can't tell you α and β

Why "just look at the superposition" doesn't work A single measurement of a single qubit returns exactly one classical bit of information — which outcome occurred — and then destroys the state that produced it. You cannot re-measure the same qubit to get a second, independent look at α and β: after the first measurement, it's already collapsed to |0⟩ or |1⟩, and every subsequent measurement (in the same basis) just returns that same fixed outcome again. There is no way to extract the full continuous information α and β carried, from one qubit, with one measurement. This is precisely why the exponential state space Lesson 1.1 got excited about doesn't hand you an exponential amount of usable information for free — Lesson 26 returns to this exact tension when introducing quantum parallelism.
Example — Recovering Probabilities Statistically Suppose you have access to many independently prepared qubits, all in the identical unknown state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ (not the same physical qubit measured repeatedly — a fresh copy each time). Measuring 1,000 of them and finding roughly 640 give outcome 0 and 360 give outcome 1 lets you estimate |α|² ≈ 0.64, so |α| ≈ 0.8. This recovers the magnitudes of the amplitudes through repeated statistics on identical copies — but it still says nothing about the relative phase between α and β, which the computational-basis Born rule never sees at all (Lesson 11's worked example already showed this). Lesson 15 will show there's an even deeper obstacle to this whole approach: you cannot even manufacture those "many identical copies" from a single unknown qubit in the first place.

3. One measurement, one shot

Fact — Measurement Is Destructive Measuring a qubit is a one-shot, irreversible operation on that specific qubit: the superposition it carried before measurement cannot be recovered afterward, and a second measurement (of the now-collapsed qubit) gives no new information. Contrast this sharply with the gates of Unit 2 — every gate is unitary and reversible; measurement is the one operation in this entire course that is neither.

1. A qubit in state (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩ is measured and the outcome is 0. What is the qubit's state immediately afterward?

Measurement collapse sends the state exactly to |0⟩ once outcome 0 is observed — all information about the original 4/5 amplitude on |1⟩ is gone.

2. If you measure a qubit, get outcome 1, and immediately measure the same qubit again (same basis), what will the second measurement return?

After collapsing to |1⟩, the qubit is now exactly in that basis state, so measuring again (same basis) returns 1 with probability 1 — no new information, since the original superposition is already gone.

3. Why can't a single measurement of a single unknown qubit ever tell you both α and β exactly?

Measurement returns exactly one outcome and then irreversibly collapses the state — there's no way to extract more than that one bit of information from a single measurement of a single qubit.

4. Fill in the blank: the instantaneous change of a qubit's state to a basis state upon measurement is called ___.

Collapse is the name for this discontinuous change — the state genuinely becomes the measured basis state, not merely "appears to."

5. Fill in the blank: to statistically estimate |α|² and |β|² for an unknown state, you need many independently prepared, identical ___ of that state — not repeated measurements of the same one qubit.

Fresh, independently prepared copies are required, since a single qubit only ever yields one measurement before collapsing — Lesson 15 shows this workaround has its own fundamental limit.
