Shor's Algorithm: A Conceptual Introduction

Factoring a large integer, reduced to a single hard sub-problem — and a quantum trick, built from the same interference toolkit as Units 6 and 7, that solves that sub-problem efficiently.

← Lesson 32 Lesson 34 → Contents Glossary →

Shor's algorithm, developed by Peter Shor, factors large integers exponentially faster than any known classical algorithm. This lesson explains what it does and the shape of why it works, without deriving the full number theory involved — that derivation belongs to a dedicated number theory course, and this course's prerequisites (Linear Algebra A and B) don't cover it. What you can fully understand here is exactly where the quantum speedup enters, and why.

1. The problem

Problem — Integer Factoring Given a large composite integer N (say, a product of two large primes, N = p·q), find its prime factors.

No known classical algorithm factors large integers efficiently. The best known classical method, the general number field sieve, runs in sub-exponential time — much better than brute force, but still infeasible for the large numbers (thousands of digits) used in practice. This classical hardness is not proven the way, say, the Deutsch-Jozsa separation is proven — it's a widely held belief, backed by decades of failed attempts to find a fast classical factoring algorithm, and it's exactly what real cryptographic systems currently rely on (Lesson 34 develops this fully).

2. The reduction: factoring via period-finding

Fact — Factoring Reduces to Finding an Order Factoring N can be reduced (via a chain of number-theoretic steps this course won't derive) to a different, more specific problem: given a random integer a with no common factor with N, find the smallest positive integer r — called the order of a modulo N — such that aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N). Once r is known, an efficient classical calculation (using the Euclidean algorithm to compute a couple of greatest common divisors) recovers N's actual factors with good probability.

Put differently: f(x) = aˣ mod N is a periodic function of x, with period exactly r. Finding N's factors comes down to finding that period. Every step of this reduction — from "factor N" down to "find the period of aˣ mod N" — is itself classical and efficient. The one piece that's classically hard is period-finding itself: for large N, no known classical algorithm finds r efficiently.

3. Where the quantum speedup enters

Why a quantum computer can find the period efficiently Finding the period of a periodic function is exactly the kind of task Units 6 and 7's interference toolkit is good at, scaled up. Shor's algorithm prepares a superposition over many values of x, computes aˣ mod N into a second register (an oracle, in this course's language), and then applies the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT) — a generalization of the repeated Hadamard transforms from Lesson 29's Deutsch-Jozsa circuit — to the first register. The QFT is specifically built to reveal periodic structure in a superposition through constructive interference at frequencies matching the true period, in exactly the way Lesson 29's Hadamards concentrated amplitude on the all-zero string when f was constant. This course won't derive the QFT itself, but the underlying principle is one you've already seen twice: interference concentrates amplitude on the answer, and everything else cancels away.
Result — Why This Is Exponentially Faster Shor's algorithm runs in time polynomial in the number of digits of N (equivalently, polynomial in log N) — a dramatic improvement over the best classical algorithm's sub-exponential running time. For a 2048-bit number (roughly 617 decimal digits), the gap between "polynomial in 617" and "sub-exponential in 617" is the difference between practically instant and utterly infeasible, even on the largest classical supercomputers, given enough qubits to run the quantum algorithm.

1. What problem does Shor's algorithm solve?

Shor's algorithm is specifically a factoring algorithm — Grover's algorithm (Lessons 31-32) handles unstructured search, a different problem entirely.

2. Factoring N is reduced to finding what, for a random a coprime to N?

The order-finding reduction is the key classical-to-quantum handoff: once r is known, efficient classical arithmetic (via the Euclidean algorithm) recovers N's factors.

3. What quantum tool does Shor's algorithm use to find this order efficiently?

The QFT is specifically designed to reveal periodic structure through interference — the same principle behind Deutsch-Jozsa's Hadamard-based amplitude concentration, generalized.

Polynomial-time scaling in log N is what makes Shor's algorithm practical for numbers far too large for any classical factoring approach.
