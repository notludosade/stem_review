Why Shor's Algorithm Matters for Cryptography

One of the internet's most widely used security guarantees rests entirely on classical factoring being hard — which is precisely the assumption Lesson 33's algorithm undermines, if the hardware ever catches up.

← Lesson 33 Lesson 35 → Contents Glossary →

Shor's algorithm isn't just a mathematical curiosity — it targets the exact assumption an enormous share of today's digital security is built on.

1. RSA and the factoring assumption

Background — RSA RSA is a widely used public-key cryptosystem: it publishes a large composite number N = p·q (p and q both large primes, kept secret) as part of a public key, and its security rests entirely on the assumption that factoring N back into p and q is computationally infeasible for anyone without the secret primes. RSA underlies a large share of secure web traffic, digital signatures, and encrypted communications in use today.
Why Shor's algorithm is a direct threat to this assumption RSA's security isn't proven to be unbreakable — it's secure only as long as factoring stays classically hard, exactly the assumption Lesson 33 discussed. A sufficiently large, sufficiently reliable quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could factor N efficiently, recovering p and q directly and breaking RSA completely — not weakening it, breaking it outright. A closely related quantum algorithm (also due to Shor, using the same period-finding and Quantum Fourier Transform machinery) efficiently solves the discrete logarithm problem, which similarly undermines Diffie-Hellman key exchange and elliptic-curve cryptography — meaning essentially all of today's standard public-key cryptography, not just RSA specifically, is vulnerable in principle.

2. The practical gap: hardware, not mathematics

Caveat — This Threat Is Not Yet Realized Running Shor's algorithm on cryptographically relevant key sizes (2048 bits or larger) requires thousands of stable, error-corrected logical qubits — not the noisy physical qubits current hardware provides, but qubits protected by the kind of error correction Unit 8 covers, at a scale far beyond anything built today. This is a hardware and engineering gap, not a mathematical one: the algorithm is proven correct and efficient in principle, but no existing quantum computer is anywhere near large or reliable enough to run it on numbers of real cryptographic size.

3. Preparing anyway: post-quantum cryptography

Definition — Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) Post-quantum cryptography refers to cryptographic schemes designed to remain secure even against an attacker with a large-scale quantum computer — typically built on mathematical problems (like certain lattice problems) with no known efficient quantum attack, unlike factoring or discrete log.

Because encrypted data intercepted and stored today could potentially be decrypted once large-scale quantum computers exist years from now (a risk sometimes called "harvest now, decrypt later"), transitioning to post-quantum-secure algorithms is already underway, well ahead of any working large-scale quantum computer. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) ran a multi-year public competition to select and standardize post-quantum cryptographic algorithms, publishing its first finalized standards in 2024 — real, deployed infrastructure work motivated directly by the algorithm this unit just derived.

The Bigger Picture Shor's algorithm is the clearest example in this entire course of quantum computing's stakes reaching outside the classroom: a purely theoretical result about period-finding and interference, proven decades before hardware existed that could plausibly run it at scale, has already reshaped a major area of applied cryptography and standards-setting — in anticipation of hardware that, per Unit 8, still doesn't exist yet at the necessary scale.
