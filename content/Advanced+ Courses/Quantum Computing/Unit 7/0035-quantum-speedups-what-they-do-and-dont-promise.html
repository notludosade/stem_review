Quantum Speedups: What They Do and Don't Promise

Three algorithms, three very different kinds of speedup — and one honest, direct correction of the most common misconception about what quantum computers are actually good for.

← Lesson 34 Unit 8, Lesson 36 → Contents Glossary →

This closing lesson of Units 6 and 7's algorithm sequence adds no new algorithm. Instead, it retraces the three you've built — Deutsch-Jozsa, Grover, Shor — side by side, and heads off directly the single most common misunderstanding people carry about what "quantum computer" means.

1. Three algorithms, three profiles

Summary — What Each Algorithm Achieves Deutsch-Jozsa (Unit 6): exponential speedup, but for a promise problem constructed specifically to demonstrate the separation — a proof of concept, not a practical tool. Grover's algorithm (Lessons 31-32): a more modest quadratic speedup, but for unstructured search, a problem that shows up broadly across computing. Shor's algorithm (Lessons 33-34): exponential speedup for integer factoring, a problem with direct, high-stakes practical importance — but requiring hardware (fault-tolerant, error-corrected, large-scale) that does not exist yet.

Notice the pattern doesn't line up neatly: the biggest speedup (Deutsch-Jozsa's exponential gap) is the least practically useful, while the most broadly useful problem (Grover's search) gets only a quadratic speedup, and the most practically consequential result (Shor's) is exponential but hardware-limited. There's no simple rule like "bigger speedup means more useful" — each result has to be evaluated on its own terms.

2. The misconception this course is built to correct

Why quantum computers are not "faster at everything" Popular coverage of quantum computing often implies a general-purpose speed boost — as if a quantum computer were simply a faster classical computer for any task at all. Nothing in this course supports that picture, and nothing in the actual research literature does either. Every speedup studied here comes from exploiting a very specific mathematical structure: Deutsch-Jozsa and Grover both use interference against an oracle's hidden structure; Shor's algorithm uses interference specifically tuned to detect periodicity. For the vast majority of everyday computational tasks — sorting a list, running a word processor, rendering a video game, training most machine learning models as currently done — no known quantum algorithm offers any advantage at all, and for many such tasks, none is expected to exist. Quantum computers are not general accelerators; they are specialized tools that outperform classical computers only on problems whose structure happens to be exploitable by interference, superposition, and entanglement in the specific ways this course has built up, lesson by lesson.
A Useful Mental Model Going Forward Think of a quantum computer the way you might think of a specialized coprocessor — a GPU is extraordinary at the narrow, highly parallel arithmetic of rendering graphics or training certain neural networks, but nobody expects a GPU to run a web browser faster than a CPU. Quantum computers are similarly narrow, just along a different axis: they excel specifically at problems with exploitable interference structure (search, periodicity, and — an application this course hasn't covered — simulating other quantum systems, widely expected to be one of the most practically important uses of quantum computers once the hardware matures). Outside that narrow set of structures, a quantum computer offers no help.

1. Which of this unit's algorithms offers an exponential speedup for a problem with direct, high-stakes practical importance?

Shor's algorithm is exponential and practically important (cryptography); Deutsch-Jozsa is also exponential but for an artificial promise problem; Grover's algorithm is "only" quadratic.

2. Does a quantum computer provide a speedup for every computational problem?

Every speedup in this course came from exploiting a specific mathematical structure via interference — there is no general-purpose quantum speedup for arbitrary computation.

3. What analogy does this lesson use to describe how quantum computers relate to classical ones?

The GPU analogy captures the "narrow but powerful within its niche" character of every quantum speedup studied in this course.

4. Fill in the blank: Grover's algorithm provides a ___ speedup over classical unstructured search.

O(√N) versus O(N) is exactly a quadratic relationship — smaller than an exponential gap, but for a far more broadly applicable problem.

5. Fill in the blank: both Deutsch-Jozsa and Shor's algorithm provide ___ speedups over their best known classical counterparts, in their respective domains.

Both achieve exponential gaps, though only Shor's has the direct practical stakes discussed in Lesson 34.
