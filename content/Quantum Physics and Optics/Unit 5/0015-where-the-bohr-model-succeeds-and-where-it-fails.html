Where the Bohr Model Succeeds and Where It Fails

Bohr's model nailed hydrogen's spectrum almost perfectly — and then failed almost completely for every other atom on the periodic table. Both facts matter for what comes next.

← Lesson 14 Unit 6, Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

What the Bohr Model Gets Right It correctly predicts hydrogen's (and other one-electron ions', like He⁺) spectral line wavelengths to high precision. It introduced the essential idea of quantized energy levels, which survives — largely unchanged in spirit — all the way into full quantum mechanics (Unit 6). Both are genuine, falsifiable successes.
Why the Bohr model is a stepping-stone, not the final picture The model fails for any atom with more than one electron — it has no way to account for electron-electron repulsion at all, and predicted spectral lines for multi-electron atoms mismatch measured ones substantially. It doesn't explain why angular momentum should be quantized as L = nħ — that's postulated, not derived, from anything deeper. And it treats the electron as traveling along a definite circular orbit at every instant, a strictly classical trajectory picture that the uncertainty principle (Unit 4) already rules out in general: a definite orbit implies simultaneously well-defined position and momentum at every point, which quantum mechanics says isn't possible. The Bohr model correctly predicts hydrogen's spectral lines, but it is a semiclassical patch — ordinary Newtonian orbits with one quantum rule bolted on — not a self-consistent quantum theory. It is superseded by the full quantum-mechanical treatment beginning in Unit 6, which replaces "electron in a definite orbit" with "electron described by a wavefunction," and which correctly extends to every atom, not just hydrogen.
Example: helium breaks the pattern Naively extending Bohr's formula to helium (Z = 2, one electron removed to make He⁺) still works reasonably for that ion — but neutral helium, with two electrons interacting with each other as well as the nucleus, shows measured spectral lines that the simple Bohr approach badly mismatches. Electron-electron repulsion, entirely absent from the model, shifts the real energy levels substantially.

1. Does the Bohr model correctly predict the spectral lines of multi-electron atoms, like neutral helium or carbon?

The model's success is essentially limited to hydrogen (and hydrogen-like one-electron ions) — it fails once electron-electron interactions matter.

2. Is the Bohr model the final, correct picture of the atom that full quantum mechanics later confirmed?

The Bohr model's quantized-energy-level idea survives into full quantum mechanics, but its "definite classical orbit" picture does not — that specific claim is replaced by the wavefunction description.

3. Why does the Bohr model's picture of a definite circular orbit conflict with the Heisenberg uncertainty principle?

Tracing a definite orbital path moment to moment requires exact position and momentum together — precisely what Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 forbids.

4. Fill in the blank: the one core idea from the Bohr model that survives, essentially intact, into full quantum mechanics is ___.

Quantized energy levels remain a core feature of the full quantum treatment, even though the "definite orbit" picture does not.
