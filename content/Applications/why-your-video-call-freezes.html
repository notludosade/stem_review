Why Your Video Call Freezes

Your coworker’s face freezes mid-sentence while the audio keeps going. The connection did not simply become “slow”: latency, jitter, packet loss, and the transport strategy are failing in different ways.

← Applications STEM+ Home →

The Concept, From Computer Networking Fundamentals Real-time calls divide audio and video into packets. Latency measures delay, jitter measures variation in arrival time, and packet loss means some data never arrives. A short jitter buffer can reorder slightly late packets, but it cannot wait indefinitely without turning conversation into a delayed exchange.

Why video often fails before audio

Video needs far more bandwidth than speech. When capacity drops, a call can lower resolution, skip frames, or preserve the smaller audio stream first. Missing video data may corrupt part of a frame until a fresh reference frame arrives; audio codecs can often conceal a brief gap. That is why “frozen face, continuing voice” is a sensible degradation strategy rather than a mystery.

Why reliability can make real-time media worse TCP guarantees ordered delivery, so one missing packet can hold newer packets behind it while a replacement travels across the network. For a file, waiting is correct. For a conversation, an old video packet arriving perfectly after its display deadline is nearly useless. Real-time transports commonly favor timeliness and adapt around loss instead.

Check your understanding

1. What is jitter?

A jitter buffer smooths modest arrival-time variation by delaying playback briefly.

2. Why might a call prefer a missing frame over retransmitting it until it arrives?

Real-time media values timely delivery; file transfer values complete delivery.
