The Concept, From Discrete MathIntersections are vertices, road segments are edges, and each edge has a weight such as predicted travel time. Dijkstra’s algorithm repeatedly settles the unvisited vertex with the smallest known total cost, then relaxes its outgoing edges. With nonnegative weights, the settled distance is final.

Fewest turns is not necessarily fastest

A path’s cost is the sum of its edge weights. One long congested road may cost more than three short clear roads, so breadth-first search is insufficient unless every edge has the same weight. Real systems use hierarchical road networks, bidirectional search, A* heuristics, and precomputation because a continent-sized graph is too large to scan naively for every request.

The objective function shapes the routeOptimizing only estimated time can send many drivers through residential streets or ignore tolls, safety, accessibility, emissions, and turn restrictions. Routing is both an algorithm and a policy choice about which costs count.

Check your understanding

1. Why does standard Dijkstra’s algorithm require nonnegative edge weights?

The greedy finalization step is justified only when extending a path cannot reduce its cost.

2. When does breadth-first search alone find a minimum-cost route?

Then minimizing edge count also minimizes total cost; unequal weights require weighted shortest-path reasoning.
