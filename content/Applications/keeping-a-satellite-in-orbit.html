Keeping a Satellite in Orbit

An orbit is continuous free fall: gravity bends a satellite’s velocity just enough that Earth’s surface curves away beneath it.

← Applications STEM+ Home →

The Concept, From AP Physics C: MechanicsFor a circular orbit, Newtonian gravity supplies centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r. Therefore v = √(GM/r), and T = 2π√(r³/GM). Here r is measured from Earth’s center—not from its surface—and the satellite’s mass cancels.

Higher means slower and longer

At about 400 km altitude, a circular-orbit model gives roughly 7.67 km/s and a 92-minute period. At geostationary altitude near 35,786 km, it gives about 3.07 km/s and a period near one sidereal day. A geostationary satellite must also orbit eastward above the equator so its angular motion matches Earth’s rotation.

Circular two-body motion is the starting modelReal missions account for atmospheric drag, Earth’s nonspherical gravity field, the Moon and Sun, radiation pressure, launch errors, and collision avoidance. Low-orbit satellites need station-keeping and eventually decay; transfers between orbits require burns that change orbital energy and angular momentum.

Check your understanding

1. If circular-orbit radius increases, what happens to orbital speed?

From v = √(GM/r), a larger denominator produces a smaller circular speed.

2. Why must orbital radius be measured from Earth’s center?

For a spherically symmetric Earth, external gravity acts as though Earth’s mass were concentrated at its center.
