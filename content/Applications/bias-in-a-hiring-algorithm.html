The Bias Hiding in a Hiring Algorithm

A model can omit protected attributes and still reproduce their effects through labels, proxies, and unequal historical opportunity.

← Applications STEM+ Home →

The Concept, From Computer Programming EthicsA selection rate is selections divided by applicants for a group. Under the U.S. Uniform Guidelines’ four-fifths rule, a group rate below 80% of the highest group’s rate is generally evidence of adverse impact. It is a screening rule—not a mathematical proof that a process is fair, unfair, or legally valid.

Removing a column does not remove its history

A model trained to imitate past hiring decisions learns patterns in those decisions. ZIP code, school, employment gaps, word choice, and job history can correlate with protected attributes or unequal access. Even a label such as “successful employee” may encode biased performance reviews. Auditing therefore examines data provenance, subgroup error and selection rates, feature effects, and the consequences of false positives and false negatives.

Fairness metrics can conflictEqual selection rates, equal error rates, and calibrated risk scores answer different questions and may be impossible to satisfy simultaneously when base rates differ. Teams must document the decision context, validate job relevance, let people contest errors, monitor drift, and involve legal and domain experts.

Check your understanding

1. Group A has a 45% selection rate and the reference group has 60%. What is the impact ratio?

0.45/0.60 = 0.75. That falls below 0.80 and calls for investigation.

2. Why can deleting a protected-attribute column fail to remove bias?

Proxy variables and biased targets can reproduce disparities even without an explicit protected field.
