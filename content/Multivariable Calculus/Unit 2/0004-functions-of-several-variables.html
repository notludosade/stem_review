Functions of Several Variables

Every function in AP Calculus BC took one number in and gave one number out. Now the input itself becomes a vector — and reading the resulting surface takes a new kind of picture.

1. A function with two inputs

Definition — Function of Several Variables A function of two variables z = f(x, y) assigns a single output number to every point (x, y) in some domain, a region of the xy-plane. A function of three variables w = f(x, y, z) does the same over a domain in space. The graph of z = f(x, y) is the surface of all points (x, y, f(x,y)) in three dimensions — the direct extension of "graph of y = f(x)" one dimension up.
Method — Reading a Surface via Level Curves A level curve f(x, y) = k is the set of every input point where the output is exactly k — a horizontal "slice" of the surface at height k, projected straight down into the xy-plane. Drawing several level curves at evenly spaced k values (a contour map) shows a 3D surface's shape on flat paper: curves packed close together mean the surface is climbing steeply; curves spread far apart mean it's nearly flat.
Worked Example f(x, y) = x² + y² is a paraboloid opening upward, with domain all of ℝ² and range [0, ∞). Its level curves f(x,y) = k are x² + y² = k — circles of radius √k centered at the origin, for any k ≥ 0. At k = 4, the level curve is the circle x² + y² = 4, radius 2. Since consecutive circles (radius 1, 2, 3, 4, …) get farther apart near the origin and the surface itself flattens out there, closely-packed rings far from center vs. widely-spaced rings near center correctly signal a bowl that's steep at the edges and flat at the bottom.
Why This Matters Temperature across a room, elevation across a landscape, profit as a function of price and quantity produced — nearly every real quantity depends on more than one input simultaneously. Level curves are exactly the topographic-map trick: they let a genuinely 3D shape be read accurately off a 2D picture, and every tool this unit builds (partial derivatives, gradients, tangent planes) is defined so it can be seen directly on a contour map like this one.

Check your recall

1. The domain of a two-variable function z = f(x, y) is naturally a region in:

Every input is a pair (x, y), so the domain lives in the two-dimensional xy-plane; the single output z is what stacks a third dimension on top to form the graph.

2. For f(x, y) = x² + y², the level curve f(x,y) = 9 is a circle of radius ___

x² + y² = 9 is a circle of radius √9 = 3 centered at the origin.

3. A level curve f(x, y) = k represents:

A level curve is a horizontal slice — every (x,y) sharing the same output value k, projected down into the domain plane.

4. On a contour map, level curves packed tightly together indicate that the surface is:

Closely spaced level curves mean the output changes by the same fixed amount k over a very short distance — a steep climb, exactly like tightly packed contour lines on a topographic map.

Every level curve of f(x,y) = x² + y² is a circle, and they never cross each other.
