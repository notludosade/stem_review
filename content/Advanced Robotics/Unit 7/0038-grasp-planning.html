Grasp Planning — Choosing a Grasp

A gripper that closes in the wrong place fails no matter how well-built it is. Deciding where and how to grasp is its own planning problem.

← Lesson 37 Lesson 39 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 37 covered the hardware that closes around an object. This lesson covers the decision that has to happen first: given an object's shape (and often its estimated weight and material), where exactly should the gripper make contact, and in what orientation?

Definition Grasp planning is the problem of selecting a grasp — a specific contact configuration (where the gripper's fingers touch the object, and the gripper's approach pose) that is expected to hold the object securely and allow it to be lifted, moved, and placed as intended. A good grasp candidate must be both physically reachable (Unit 1's inverse kinematics has to have a solution for the arm to get there) and mechanically sound (the actual subject of this lesson).
Why "grasp the middle" isn't a general strategy An object's center is a reasonable default for a symmetric, uniformly weighted item, but real objects break that assumption constantly: an off-center weight distribution (a mug, handle-heavy or full of liquid on one side) means the true center of mass isn't the geometric center, and grasping the wrong point lets the object rotate or tip out of the grip under gravity once lifted. A good grasp plan reasons about the object's actual mass distribution, not just its outline — which is part of why grasp planning is genuinely hard for irregular or unfamiliar objects, and an active area of robotics research beyond this course's scope.
Example A robot picking a coffee mug by its rim, opposite the handle, might have a geometrically sensible-looking grasp — two contact points a reasonable distance apart, well within the gripper's opening range — that nonetheless fails once lifted, because the handle's extra mass pulls the mug's true center of gravity off to one side, rotating it out of an otherwise secure-looking grip. Grasping across the mug's body, straddling its actual center of mass, is far more likely to hold securely through the lift.

Real grasp-planning systems typically generate several candidate grasps for an object (from its perceived shape — Unit 2's cameras and depth sensors doing exactly the perception job they were built for), score each candidate on criteria like reachability, expected stability, and clearance from surrounding objects or surfaces, and select the highest-scoring one to execute. This connects manipulation directly back to perception: a grasp plan is only as good as the shape and pose estimate it was computed from, the same "garbage in, garbage out" dependency Unit 2 and Unit 3 already established for sensing and localization.

A selected grasp still has to actually hold once contact is made and force is applied — which brings this unit to the physics underneath "secure," the next lesson's subject.

1. What must a good candidate grasp satisfy, according to this lesson?

A grasp candidate needs both a reachable arm configuration and sound mechanics — satisfying only one isn't enough.

2. Why can 'grasp the geometric center' fail for an object like a mug with a handle?

Real objects often have mass distributed unevenly, so grasp planning must reason about actual center of mass, not just outline shape, to avoid the object rotating out of the grip.

3. How does grasp planning connect directly back to Unit 2's perception lessons?

Candidate grasps are generated from a perceived shape estimate — the same 'garbage in, garbage out' dependency on sensing quality that localization and mapping already established.
