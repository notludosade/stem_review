Grippers and End-Effectors

The end-effector was a name so far — this lesson gives it real hardware, and a first sense of why 'just close the fingers' hides real design decisions.

← Lesson 36 Lesson 38 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 1 introduced the end-effector as the working tip of a kinematic chain, without saying much about what it actually is. This lesson covers the most common category: grippers, mechanical hands built specifically to grasp objects.

Definition A gripper is an end-effector with movable parts — fingers or jaws — that close around an object to hold it through friction and enclosure. A two-finger (parallel-jaw) gripper is the simplest and most common design: two fingers that move directly toward or away from each other, gripping an object between them.
Why finger count and shape are real design trade-offs, not arbitrary choices A two-finger parallel gripper is simple, fast, and cheap, but limited: it grasps well along one consistent direction and struggles with oddly shaped or very small/large objects relative to its opening range. A three-finger gripper (arranged around an object rather than opposite each other) can conform to a wider range of shapes and grasp more securely against rotation, at the cost of more actuators, more complexity, and slower operation. Neither is "the correct gripper" — the choice depends entirely on what the robot needs to pick up, exactly the way Unit 1's arm design depended on the target workspace.
Example A warehouse robot handling uniform rectangular boxes is well served by a simple two-finger parallel gripper — boxes are a predictable shape, and the gripper's one consistent grasping direction covers the whole task. The same gripper would struggle with a warehouse that also ships loose produce, irregular parts, or fragile items of wildly varying size — tasks that call for a fundamentally different end-effector design, which Lesson 40 covers directly.

Grip force matters as much as finger shape. A gripper needs enough force to prevent the object from slipping under gravity or acceleration, but not so much that it damages a fragile object or stalls the gripper's own motor — a balance Lesson 39's discussion of friction and grasp stability makes precise. Some grippers include simple contact or force sensors specifically to help strike that balance, closing the loop (in exactly the sense Unit 1's opening lesson defined) between commanded grip force and actual contact conditions.

Before deciding how hard to grip, though, there's a prior question this lesson hasn't addressed: given an object's shape, where on it should the gripper even close? That's the next lesson's entire subject.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is a two-finger (parallel-jaw) gripper?

Parallel-jaw grippers are the simplest and most common design: two fingers closing along a single direction.

2. What is the main trade-off between a two-finger gripper and a three-finger gripper?

More fingers generally means more grasping versatility and stability against rotation, but at real hardware and complexity cost — a genuine design trade-off.

3. Why does grip force need to be carefully balanced rather than simply maximized?

Grip force sits on a balance between preventing slip and avoiding damage or motor stall — too little or too much are both failures.

4. Fill in the blank: an end-effector with movable fingers or jaws that close around an object to hold it is called a ___.

Grippers are the most common category of end-effector for grasping tasks.

5. Fill in the blank: the simplest and most common gripper design, with two fingers moving directly toward or away from each other, is called a two-finger or ___-jaw gripper.

Parallel-jaw is the standard term for this common, simple gripper design.
