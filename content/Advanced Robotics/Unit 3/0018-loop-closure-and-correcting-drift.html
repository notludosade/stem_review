Loop Closure and Correcting Drift

Recognizing 'I have been exactly here before' is a small-sounding event that unlocks a correction across an entire path already driven.

← Lesson 17 Unit 4, Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

The last lesson named loop closure as the mechanism that makes SLAM practically useful, not just conceptually elegant. This lesson looks at what a loop closure actually requires, and what happens to the map and pose estimate once one is found.

Definition A loop closure is a detected match between the robot's current sensor reading and a previously recorded observation from an earlier point in its path, indicating the robot has returned to (or near) a place it has already mapped. Detecting one requires place recognition: comparing current LIDAR shapes, visual features, or other sensor signatures against a library of previously recorded ones, well enough to recognize a genuine revisit despite the drifted position estimate suggesting the robot is somewhere else.
Why loop closure corrects the whole path, not just the current position Once a loop closure is confirmed, the robot knows two things should be true simultaneously: its current estimated pose, and the pose it had the first time it saw this same place — and these two, after a long loop, typically disagree because of accumulated drift. SLAM doesn't just snap the current position to match; it distributes that correction backward across every pose estimate recorded since the loop began, adjusting the whole chain so it's internally consistent again. This is why closing one loop can visibly straighten an entire previously-crooked map, not just fix the newest data point.
Example Picture a hallway loop mapped with accumulated drift: by the time the robot returns to its starting doorway, dead reckoning places it 1.5 m away from where the doorway actually is on the map, even though the robot is standing right in the doorway again. Recognizing the doorway (place recognition) tells SLAM these two pose estimates — "here, now" and "here, when I first passed through" — represent the same real location. Redistributing that 1.5 m discrepancy across the whole loop's chain of poses turns a map with a visible seam into one where the hallway forms a single consistent loop.

Loop closure is also where SLAM can fail in an interesting way: a false loop closure — matching two different places that happen to look similar — can corrupt a map by forcing two genuinely different locations to be treated as one. Real SLAM systems have to weigh candidate matches carefully rather than accepting every apparent match, but the details of that safeguard are beyond this course's scope.

This closes Unit 3. A robot can now build a map, track its position within that map, and correct for the drift dead reckoning alone can't avoid. Unit 4 assumes exactly this: given a map and a known position on it, how does a robot decide where to go?
