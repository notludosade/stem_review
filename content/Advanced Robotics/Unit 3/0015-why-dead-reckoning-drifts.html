Why Dead Reckoning Drifts

Small, honest measurement errors don't cancel out when you're adding them up in sequence — they compound, and the longer you drive, the worse it gets.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 11 distinguished bias from noise in a single sensor reading. Dead reckoning's core weakness is what happens when you take a long sequence of readings, each with a little of both, and add them all together.

Definition Drift is the growth of position error in a dead-reckoning estimate over time, caused by small per-step errors accumulating rather than canceling out. Because each new position estimate is built by adding an increment onto the previous (already slightly wrong) estimate, dead reckoning has no mechanism to notice that it has gone wrong — every step's error becomes part of the foundation the next step builds on.
Why errors compound instead of averaging out Lesson 11's noise-averaging argument relied on repeated measurements of the same fixed quantity — errors scattered around one true value cancel when averaged. Dead reckoning is different: each step measures a different quantity (this step's motion, not last step's), and each estimate is built directly on the last one. There's no fixed reference to average back toward — a wrong turn-angle estimate early on doesn't just add position error, it also points every subsequent "forward" motion in a slightly wrong direction, so the error compounds geometrically rather than statistically canceling.
Example Suppose a robot's heading estimate is off by just 1° due to a small per-step turning error. Over a straight 10 m drive, that 1° heading error alone produces a sideways position error of roughly 10 m × sin(1°) ≈ 0.17 m — noticeable, but perhaps tolerable. Over 100 m, the same 1° error produces roughly 1.7 m of sideways drift — and that's from a single small heading error alone, before accounting for the additional errors accumulating in every other step along the way. Position drift in dead reckoning generally grows roughly in proportion to distance traveled, not to time elapsed.

This is not a flaw specific to bad hardware — it is a structural property of any sensing scheme that only ever measures change from the last known state and never checks itself against an independent, fixed reference. Better encoders (Lesson 8's higher resolution) slow the drift; they cannot eliminate it. Something outside the accumulating chain is required to correct it — which is exactly what maps and SLAM, the rest of this unit, provide.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does dead-reckoning error grow over time instead of averaging out the way repeated measurements of the same fixed quantity do?

Unlike repeated measurements of one fixed quantity, each dead-reckoning step measures a different increment and builds directly on the prior (already imperfect) estimate — errors compound rather than cancel.

2. A small constant heading error compounds into what kind of position error over a long, straight drive?

A constant heading error points every increment of forward motion slightly off-course, producing a sideways drift that grows roughly in proportion to total distance traveled.

3. What can reduce dead-reckoning drift without eliminating it entirely?

Better encoders reduce the size of each per-step error, slowing the rate of drift — but they don't provide the independent, fixed reference needed to eliminate accumulated error entirely.

4. Fill in the blank: the growth of dead-reckoning position error over time, caused by small per-step errors accumulating, is called ___.

Drift is the standard term for this unbounded, accumulating position error.

5. Fill in the blank: dead-reckoning position drift generally grows roughly in proportion to distance ___, not to time elapsed alone.

Since each increment of motion carries its own small error, more distance covered means more accumulated error, roughly independent of how quickly that distance was covered.
