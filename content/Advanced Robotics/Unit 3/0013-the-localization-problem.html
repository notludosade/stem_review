The Localization Problem

Every plan, every map, every sensor conversion in this course assumes the robot knows where it is. This unit is about how shaky that assumption really is.

← Unit 2, Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 1 built forward and inverse kinematics on top of a robot's own joint sensors, which are trustworthy over short timescales. Unit 2 built up to sensor fusion for combining imperfect readings. Both units quietly assumed something bigger: that the robot has a reliable answer to "where am I, in the world?" This unit is about how that answer is actually obtained — and how fragile it is.

Definition Localization is the problem of estimating a robot's own pose (position and orientation, from Lesson 3) within a known or partially known environment, using noisy sensor data. It is a genuinely different problem from forward kinematics: forward kinematics tells you where your end-effector is relative to your own base, using joint sensors you fully trust; localization asks where your whole robot is relative to the world, using sensors that are only approximately trustworthy.
Why localization can't just be "read a GPS" Outdoors, GPS gives an approximate world-frame position — but its accuracy (often several meters) is too coarse for tasks like navigating a narrow warehouse aisle, and it doesn't work indoors or in dense urban canyons at all. Most robots have to build their own position estimate from the sensors covered in Unit 2 — wheel encoders, LIDAR, cameras — none of which report position directly. Localization is the process of turning those indirect readings into a trustworthy pose estimate.

There are two dominant strategies, and the rest of this unit is organized around them. The first, dead reckoning, builds a position estimate purely from the robot's own motion (Lesson 14) — simple, but it drifts, as Lesson 15 will show concretely. The second requires the robot to also recognize features of its surroundings and match them against a map, which is powerful but raises an obvious chicken-and-egg problem if no map exists yet — the problem Lesson 17's SLAM addresses directly.

Example A warehouse robot that only trusts its wheel encoders can estimate "I've driven 40 m forward and turned left twice" — but after enough turns and distance, small per-step errors compound into a real, uncorrected mismatch between where the robot thinks it is and where it actually is. The same robot, if it can also recognize a specific shelving unit it has seen before and knows that unit's true position on a stored map, can snap its estimate back toward the truth — correcting the accumulated error, not just accepting it.

This lesson set the stage without solving anything yet. The next lesson makes dead reckoning concrete — exactly how a running position estimate gets built from wheel motion alone.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. How does localization differ from the forward kinematics covered in Unit 1?

Forward kinematics is a direct, trusted computation relative to the robot's own base. Localization estimates the robot's pose in the world using inherently noisy, indirect sensor data.

2. Why isn't GPS alone a complete solution to robot localization?

GPS's meter-scale accuracy and indoor/urban-canyon unavailability mean most robots must build their own pose estimate from onboard sensors.

3. What is the fundamental trade-off between the two localization strategies this lesson previews?

Dead reckoning alone accumulates uncorrected error; recognizing mapped features lets a robot snap its estimate back toward the truth, at the cost of needing a map (or building one, which SLAM addresses).

4. Fill in the blank: the problem of estimating a robot's own pose within an environment using noisy sensor data is called ___.

Localization is the standard term for this pose-estimation problem.

5. Fill in the blank: building a position estimate purely from a robot's own measured motion, without reference to external landmarks, is called dead ___.

Dead reckoning estimates position from motion alone — the subject of the next lesson.
