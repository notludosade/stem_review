Odometry and Dead Reckoning

Add up every small motion since the start, and you get an estimate of where you are now — no landmarks required, and no correction either.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

The simplest way to estimate a robot's current position is to never look outward at all: just keep a running total of every small motion made since a known starting point. This is dead reckoning, and its robotic implementation, built from the wheel encoders in Lesson 8, is called odometry.

Definition Dead reckoning is estimating current position by accumulating a sequence of small, measured motions from a known starting pose, without reference to external landmarks. Odometry is dead reckoning specifically built from a wheeled robot's own encoder readings — converting wheel rotation into an estimate of how far and which way the robot has moved.

At each short time step, the robot estimates a small change in position and orientation (Δx, Δy, Δθ) from its wheel encoders, then adds that increment onto its running pose estimate. Repeat this thousands of times a second, and the robot maintains a continuously updated best guess of its own (x, y, θ) — all without ever needing to recognize anything in its surroundings.

Why odometry lives in its own coordinate frame Because odometry is built purely from accumulated motion, it defines its own local reference — often literally called the odom frame — anchored wherever the robot started, not tied to any fixed external landmark. This frame is smooth (no sudden jumps between updates) and updates fast, which is genuinely useful for short-term motion control (Unit 5 depends on exactly this). What it is not is accurate over the long run, for the reason the next lesson makes precise.
Example A robot starts at (0, 0, 0°). Over one time step it estimates it moved 0.1 m forward with no turn: new pose ≈ (0.1, 0, 0°). Over the next step, it estimates 0.1 m forward while turning 5°: new pose ≈ (0.1 + 0.1·cos(5°), 0 + 0.1·sin(5°), 5°) ≈ (0.200, 0.009, 5°). Each step is a small, mechanical update — position ≈ old position + Δposition, angle ≈ old angle + Δangle — repeated continuously as the robot drives.

Odometry's genuine strength is that it requires nothing beyond the robot's own sensors — no map, no recognizable landmarks, no external infrastructure. Its genuine weakness, covered next, is that every one of those small per-step estimates has some error, and dead reckoning has no built-in way to notice or correct for it.

1. What is dead reckoning?

Dead reckoning builds a position estimate purely from accumulated motion, without reference to external landmarks.

2. What is odometry, specifically?

Odometry is the wheel-encoder-based implementation of dead reckoning: converting wheel rotation into an incremental position and orientation estimate.

3. Why is odometry's reference frame described as anchored wherever the robot started, rather than tied to a fixed external landmark?

Since odometry only accumulates the robot's own measured motion, its frame is naturally anchored to wherever tracking began, not to any external reference point.

4. Fill in the blank: dead reckoning built specifically from a wheeled robot's encoder readings is called ___.

Odometry is the standard term for wheel-encoder-based dead reckoning.

5. A robot at (0, 0, 0°) estimates one time step of 0.2 m forward motion with no turn. Fill in the blank: its new estimated x-position is ___ m.

With no turn, the full 0.2 m of forward motion adds directly to the x-coordinate: 0 + 0.2 = 0.2 m.
