Replanning and Dynamic Obstacles

Sometimes steering around an obstacle isn't enough — the whole plan has to be thrown out and recomputed, on the fly, without stopping the robot's mission.

← Lesson 23 Unit 5, Lesson 25 → Contents Glossary →

Local avoidance (Lesson 23) handles small, local mismatches between plan and reality. Some mismatches are too big for that: a corridor the global plan relied on is now completely blocked, or an obstacle is moving in a way that makes "steer slightly around it" insufficient. This lesson covers replanning — recomputing all or part of the route mid-mission.

Definition Replanning is recomputing a new global path (or a significant portion of one) after detecting that the current plan is no longer valid or safe to continue, typically triggered by a newly discovered obstacle that fully blocks the planned route, or by a moving obstacle whose future position can't be handled through local avoidance alone.
Why moving obstacles are a genuinely different problem Lesson 22's A* assumed a static map — every obstacle stays put while the algorithm searches. A person walking through a warehouse aisle violates that assumption directly: by the time the robot reaches where the person was sensed, they may no longer be there — or may now be exactly where the robot was about to go. Handling this well means reasoning not just about where an obstacle currently is, but roughly where it's likely to be by the time the robot would reach that point — treating time, not just space, as part of the planning problem.
Example A robot's global plan routes it through a specific doorway. On approach, its sensors detect the doorway is now fully blocked by a stack of pallets — not a small nudge-around-it obstacle, but a complete route failure. Local avoidance alone can't solve this; there's no way through that doorway at all. The robot triggers a full replan: A* runs again on an updated map (this new obstacle now marked, as in Unit 3's occupancy grid), most likely finding a route through a different aisle instead.

Real systems make replanning practical by not always starting from scratch: incremental variants of A*-style search can reuse most of a previous search's work when only a small part of the map has changed, rather than recomputing the entire graph from nothing every time. The details of those incremental algorithms are beyond this course's scope, but the goal they serve is exactly what a real deployed robot needs — global planning, local avoidance, and the ability to fully rethink the route, all working together, fast enough to run continuously.

This closes Unit 4. A robot can now be given a map and a goal and compute a safe route to it, adapting as reality departs from what the map predicted. Unit 5 picks up exactly where this leaves off: turning a planned path — a sequence of graph nodes — into actual motor commands.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What typically triggers replanning, as distinct from ordinary local obstacle avoidance?

Replanning is reserved for mismatches serious enough that steering around them locally isn't sufficient — a fully blocked route or an obstacle whose motion needs to be anticipated.

2. Why does a moving obstacle violate a core assumption behind Lesson 22's A* search?

A* searches a static graph; a moving obstacle changes position over time, so planning around it well requires reasoning about where it's likely to be by the time the robot would reach that point, not just where it is now.

3. Why do real systems often use incremental replanning algorithms rather than always rerunning A* completely from scratch?

Reusing prior search work when the map has only changed slightly avoids the cost of recomputing the entire graph from nothing, which matters for running replanning fast enough in real time.

4. Fill in the blank: recomputing a new global path after detecting the current one is no longer valid or safe is called ___.

Replanning is triggered by route-invalidating discoveries, distinct from the smaller local corrections of ordinary obstacle avoidance.

5. Fill in the blank: planning around a moving obstacle well requires reasoning about ___ as well as space — where the obstacle is likely to be by the time the robot arrives.

Moving obstacles turn planning into a problem that includes time, not just a static spatial map.
