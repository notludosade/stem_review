Cameras and Visual Perception

A camera hands a robot enormous amounts of information and almost none of it for free — depth, in particular, has to be earned back through computation.

← Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

A camera captures a two-dimensional grid of pixels, each recording the color and brightness of light arriving from one direction. That single sentence hides the whole reason vision is treated as hard in robotics: a 2D image is a projection of a 3D scene, and that projection throws depth information away by construction.

Definition A camera image is a grid of pixels, each storing a color or intensity value sampled from the light hitting that point on the sensor. Unlike a LIDAR return, a single pixel carries no direct distance information — the same pixel color could come from a nearby dim surface or a bright distant one.
Why vision is a genuinely harder inverse problem LIDAR asks a direct question (how long did this pulse take to return?) and gets a direct answer (a distance). A camera asks an indirect question (what pattern of light arrived?) and has to work backward — inferring edges, shapes, objects, and eventually distances from pixel patterns alone. That backward inference is exactly the kind of "recover the cause from the effect" problem this course's inverse kinematics lesson introduced in miniature, except vision has to do it from millions of pixels instead of a handful of joint angles, using computer vision or machine learning methods well beyond this course's scope.

Two specific challenges make this worse than it might first sound. Lighting changes what a pixel reports even when nothing in the scene has moved — the same surface looks different at noon than at dusk. Occlusion means an object can be partly or fully hidden behind another, so "not visible in this frame" doesn't mean "not there." Both are facts about the physical world a camera has no way to see past.

Example A single ordinary camera (a "monocular" camera) genuinely cannot tell, from one image alone, whether it's looking at a real 30 cm box 2 m away or a 15 cm toy box 1 m away — both project to the identical size and shape on the sensor. A stereo camera (two cameras a known distance apart, like human eyes) resolves this by comparing the same point's position in both images; the shift between them (the disparity) is larger for close objects and smaller for far ones, which recovers actual depth through geometry rather than a single ambiguous image.

Despite all of this, cameras remain indispensable specifically because of what LIDAR can't do at all: recognizing what something is, reading text or signs, distinguishing a person from a shopping cart. The trade-off running through this whole unit — precise-but-narrow versus rich-but-ambiguous — is exactly why the next two lessons exist: first the honest cost of that ambiguity (noise and uncertainty in general), then the fix (combining sensors).

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does a single camera pixel carry no direct distance information, unlike a single LIDAR return?

A camera image is a 2D projection of a 3D scene — depth is thrown away by the projection itself, unlike LIDAR's direct time-of-flight distance.

2. Why can a monocular (single) camera not distinguish a large distant box from a small nearby box of proportional size, from one image alone?

Without a second viewpoint or another depth cue, a single image is genuinely ambiguous about absolute scale and distance — larger-and-farther looks identical to smaller-and-nearer.

3. How does a stereo camera recover depth that a single (monocular) camera cannot?

Disparity — how much a point's apparent position shifts between the two camera views — is larger for close objects and smaller for far ones, recovering depth through geometry.

4. Fill in the blank: an object partly or fully hidden behind another object in a camera's view is a case of ___.

Occlusion means 'not visible in this frame' does not mean 'not present in the scene.'

5. Fill in the blank: in a stereo camera, the shift in a point's apparent position between the two images is called the ___.

Disparity is larger for nearby objects and smaller for distant ones, which is how stereo vision recovers depth geometrically.
