Wheel Encoders and Proprioception

Counting wheel rotations sounds like it should give exact distance traveled. It doesn't — and the gap matters more than it looks.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

A wheel encoder is the most common way a mobile robot measures its own motion, and it's a good first example of proprioception because its failure mode is so easy to picture directly.

Definition A wheel encoder is a sensor attached to a wheel's axle that counts discrete ticks as the wheel rotates, with a fixed number of ticks corresponding to one full revolution (its resolution). Counting ticks over time gives an estimate of how far, and how fast, the wheel has turned.
distance traveled ≈ (ticks counted / ticks per revolution) × wheel circumference
Worked example A wheel with a 0.3 m circumference is attached to an encoder with a resolution of 360 ticks per revolution. The encoder counts 900 ticks. Revolutions turned = 900 / 360 = 2.5. Distance traveled ≈ 2.5 × 0.3 m = 0.75 m. A finer-resolution encoder (say, 1440 ticks per revolution) gives a more precise estimate of partial revolutions, but does not fix the deeper problem below.
Why this estimate quietly assumes something false The formula above assumes that every bit of wheel rotation converts directly into forward motion — that the wheel rolls without slipping. On a wet floor, loose gravel, or carpet, a wheel can spin without the robot actually covering that much ground, or vice versa if it's briefly dragged. Tire deformation, uneven ground, and manufacturing tolerances in the wheel's actual diameter add further small errors. None of these show up in the raw tick count — the sensor faithfully reports how much the wheel turned, which is subtly not the same thing as how far the robot moved.

Individually, each of these errors is small. The problem — covered properly in the next unit — is that they don't cancel out over time; they accumulate. A wheel encoder is a good proprioceptive sensor for short-term, high-frequency estimates of motion, and a systematically unreliable one for knowing exactly where a robot is after a long drive.

This lesson deliberately previewed a problem it doesn't solve: encoders are the raw input to odometry, the running estimate of a robot's position built from exactly this kind of wheel-rotation counting — and Unit 3 is dedicated to what happens to that estimate over time.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a wheel encoder directly measure?

An encoder counts ticks per rotation; distance traveled is only an estimate computed from that tick count and the wheel's circumference.

2. Why doesn't wheel-slip on a wet floor show up directly in an encoder's raw tick count?

The encoder measures wheel rotation, not ground truth motion — if the wheel spins without traction, ticks accumulate with no matching distance covered.

3. Why is a wheel encoder described as a good sensor for short-term motion estimates but unreliable for knowing exact position after a long drive?

Individually small errors compound over a long drive rather than averaging out, which is exactly the dead-reckoning drift problem Unit 3 covers in depth.

4. Fill in the blank: the number of encoder ticks corresponding to one full wheel revolution is called the encoder's ___.

Resolution describes how finely the encoder can distinguish partial rotations — more ticks per revolution means finer precision.

5. For a wheel with 0.4 m circumference and an encoder with 200 ticks per revolution, if the encoder counts 100 ticks, fill in the blank: the estimated distance traveled is ___ m.

Revolutions = 100/200 = 0.5. Distance = 0.5 × 0.4 m = 0.2 m.
