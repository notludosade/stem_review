1. A sensor that measures a robot's own wheel rotation, as opposed to distance to an obstacle, is:

Proprioceptive sensors measure the robot's own internal state, such as wheel rotation; exteroceptive sensors measure the external environment.

2. Fill in the blank: a wheel encoder counts discrete ___ as the wheel rotates, using a fixed number per revolution to estimate distance traveled.

Ticks are the discrete counted units an encoder reports; ticks per revolution is its resolution.

3. Answer both parts about a wheel encoder:

(a) A wheel with 0.5 m circumference has an encoder with 500 ticks/revolution. The encoder counts 1000 ticks. What distance is estimated?

(b) Fill in the blank: this estimate assumes the wheel rolls without ___.

(a) Revolutions = 1000/500 = 2; distance = 2 × 0.5 m = 1.0 m. (b) The formula assumes no wheel slip, which is often false in practice.

4. How does LIDAR determine the distance to a surface?

LIDAR uses time-of-flight: the round-trip time of a laser pulse, converted to distance via the speed of light.

5. Why might a LIDAR scan fail to return a clean, accurate distance for a mirror or polished metal surface?

Reflective surfaces are a known LIDAR weakness — the beam may bounce away rather than return directly, producing no clean reading or a misleading one.

6. Answer both parts about cameras:

(a) Why can't a single (monocular) camera directly measure distance to an object from one image alone?

(b) Fill in the blank: a camera pair a known distance apart can recover depth using the ___ between matching points in the two images.

(a) A 2D projection of a 3D scene throws away depth by construction — the same image could come from many different real-world distances. (b) Disparity (the shift between matching points in two views) reveals depth geometrically.

7. Fill in the blank: when an object is partly or fully hidden behind another object in a camera's view, this is called ___.

Occlusion means an object is blocked from view by another object, not that it's absent from the scene.

8. A distance sensor consistently reads 15 cm too far on every single measurement, regardless of how many readings are averaged. This is an example of:

A consistent, repeatable offset in one direction — unaffected by averaging — is the definition of bias, not random noise.

9. Why does averaging repeated readings reduce the effect of noise but not bias?

Noise scatters above and below the true value and partly cancels when averaged; bias is a consistent offset that averaging cannot remove.

10. Why are complementary sensors (rather than two identical sensors) generally the more powerful combination for sensor fusion?

Complementary sensors (e.g., a fast-but-drifting encoder and a slower but non-drifting position fix) cover each other's specific weaknesses, unlike two sensors sharing the same blind spot.
