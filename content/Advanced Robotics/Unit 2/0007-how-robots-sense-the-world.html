How Robots Sense the World

A robot never has direct access to the truth about itself or its surroundings — only measurements, and measurements are never perfect.

← Unit 1, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 1 assumed a robot always knows its own joint angles exactly. In reality, every one of those values comes from a sensor, and every sensor reading is an imperfect stand-in for the true quantity it's measuring. This unit is about that gap — what robots can sense, and why the raw numbers coming back are never quite the whole truth.

Definition Proprioceptive sensors measure the robot's own internal state — joint angles, wheel rotation, battery voltage. Exteroceptive sensors measure the external world — distance to obstacles, camera images, ambient light. A robot needs both: proprioception to know what it's doing, exteroception to know what's around it.

This unit covers three exteroceptive-leaning sensors in turn — wheel encoders (technically proprioceptive, but the foundation for a lot of what follows), LIDAR, and cameras — chosen because together they cover the three dominant ways robots perceive: measuring their own motion, measuring distance directly, and measuring rich visual detail indirectly.

Why "just read the sensor" isn't the whole job Every physical sensor has limited range, limited resolution, some delay before a reading is available, and some amount of random error on top of the true value. None of this is a defect to be engineered away entirely — it's a permanent fact about measuring a physical quantity with a physical device. A robot's software has to be built assuming its sensor data is noisy and incomplete, not assuming it's ground truth.
Example A wheel encoder reports how far a wheel has turned — but not whether the wheel slipped on a wet floor without the robot actually moving that far. A camera reports rich color and texture — but nothing directly about how far away anything is. A LIDAR reports precise distances — but nothing about color, or what an object is. Every sensor answers a narrow question well and says nothing at all about everything else.

That narrowness is exactly why real robots carry multiple sensors rather than one "best" one, and why Lesson 12 of this unit is dedicated entirely to combining them. First, though: three lessons on what each sensor actually measures, and specifically how each one fails.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the difference between a proprioceptive sensor and an exteroceptive sensor?

Proprioception is about the robot's own state (joint angles, wheel rotation); exteroception is about the environment around it.

2. Why does this lesson insist that sensor noise and incompleteness are not simply defects to be engineered away?

No physical sensor is perfect — software has to be built assuming imperfect data is the normal case, not an occasional failure.

3. Why do real robots typically carry multiple different kinds of sensors instead of one 'best' sensor?

A camera doesn't measure distance well; a LIDAR doesn't measure color or identity. Combining sensors covers more of what a robot needs to know.

4. Fill in the blank: sensors that measure the robot's own internal state, like joint angles or wheel rotation, are called ___ sensors.

Proprioceptive sensors report on the robot's own body and motion, as opposed to the external environment.

5. Fill in the blank: sensors that measure the external world, like distance to obstacles or camera images, are called ___ sensors.

Exteroceptive sensors report on the environment surrounding the robot, as opposed to the robot's own internal state.
