Sensor Fusion — Combining Imperfect Information

Two mediocre sensors, combined the right way, can beat one excellent sensor — because their weaknesses rarely overlap.

← Lesson 11 Unit 3, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Every sensor this unit has covered is good at something specific and blind to something else. Sensor fusion is the idea of combining readings from multiple sensors to get a better estimate than any single sensor could provide alone — not by picking the "best" one, but by leaning on each sensor exactly where it's strong.

Definition Sensor fusion is the process of combining measurements from two or more sensors into a single, more reliable estimate of some quantity — typically because the sensors have complementary strengths and weaknesses (each is strong where the other is weak) or redundant coverage (both measure the same thing, catching each other's outright errors).
Why complementary sensors are the more powerful combination Redundant sensors (two of the same kind) help catch outright failures, but don't fix a shared blind spot — two cameras still can't measure distance well head-on. Complementary sensors are more powerful because their errors don't line up: an encoder's motion estimate updates fast but drifts (Lesson 8), while a LIDAR-based position fix updates slower but doesn't drift the same way. Fused together, the combination can track fast motion moment-to-moment using the encoder while periodically correcting accumulated drift using the LIDAR fix — better than either alone, for exactly the reason neither one's weakness is shared by the other.
Example A robot vacuum's wheel encoders (Lesson 8) update at high frequency and give a smooth, responsive estimate of how the robot has moved in the last instant — but they drift as slip errors accumulate. A camera-based position fix (something Unit 3's SLAM lessons will explain properly) is slower to compute and less frequent, but doesn't accumulate the same kind of drift over time. Fusing the two — trusting the encoder for fast, moment-to-moment motion and periodically pulling the running estimate back toward the camera's steadier fix — gives smoother and more accurate tracking than either sensor running alone.

This lesson deliberately stopped short of the actual mathematics of fusion — the standard tool, called a Kalman filter (and its variants), formally weighs each sensor's contribution by how much to trust it at that instant, using the noise characteristics from Lesson 11. That machinery is beyond this course's scope; the idea to carry forward is simpler and more durable: no single sensor has to be trusted completely, because its weaknesses can be covered by a different sensor's strengths.

This closes Unit 2. Robots now have a way to sense their own motion and their surroundings, imperfectly, and a way to combine those imperfect readings into something more trustworthy. Unit 3 puts that combination to its first real job: figuring out where the robot actually is.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is sensor fusion?

Sensor fusion combines readings from multiple sensors — often with complementary strengths and weaknesses — into a single better estimate.

2. Why are complementary sensors generally more powerful to combine than two redundant sensors of the same kind?

Two sensors with different, non-overlapping weaknesses (like a fast-but-drifting encoder and a slow-but-stable position fix) compensate for each other; two identical sensors share the same blind spot.

3. In the robot vacuum fusion example, what role does the wheel encoder play compared to the camera-based position fix?

The encoder is fast but drifts over time; the camera-based fix is slower but doesn't share that drift, so combining them gives both responsiveness and long-term accuracy.

4. Fill in the blank: sensors with strengths and weaknesses that don't overlap — each strong where the other is weak — are described as ___ to each other.

Complementary sensors cover each other's blind spots, which is why fusing them tends to outperform either sensor alone.

5. Fill in the blank: the standard mathematical tool for formally weighing each sensor's contribution to a fused estimate, based on how much to trust it, is called a ___ filter.

The Kalman filter (and its variants) is the standard formal tool for sensor fusion, though this course kept its mathematics out of scope.
