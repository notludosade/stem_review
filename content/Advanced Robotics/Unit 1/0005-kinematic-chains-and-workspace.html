Kinematic Chains and Workspace

Link lengths and joint limits carve out a fixed region the end-effector can ever reach — and everything outside it is off the table, permanently.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Forward kinematics answers "where does the end-effector go for these particular joint values?" Sweep every joint through every value it's allowed to take, and the set of all reachable end-effector positions is the arm's workspace — a single, fixed property of the arm's geometry, decided the moment its link lengths and joint limits are chosen, long before any control software runs.

Definition A robot arm's workspace is the complete set of end-effector positions reachable by some combination of valid joint values. The dexterous workspace is the (usually smaller) subset reachable at every orientation, not just some orientation — a stricter, often more practically relevant standard for tasks like inserting a part that must arrive facing a specific way.

For the 2-link planar arm from the last lesson, the workspace has a clean geometric shape. With no joint limits, the reachable region is an annulus (a ring) centered on the base: the outer radius is L1 + L2 (arm fully extended), and the inner radius is |L1 − L2| (arm folded as tightly as geometry allows). Nothing closer than the inner radius or farther than the outer radius is reachable — not a limitation of the control software, a hard fact about the geometry.

Why this is a design decision, not a software problem If a warehouse arm needs to reach into a bin 1.2 m away, no amount of clever control code fixes an arm whose L1 + L2 only totals 1.0 m — the workspace physically doesn't extend that far. Link lengths, joint types, and joint limits have to be chosen at design time with the target workspace already in mind, the same way Engineering 1 treated constraints as the substance of a problem rather than an afterthought to route around later.

Joint limits shrink the workspace further and can break its clean shape entirely — real joints rarely rotate a full 360°, and a limit at, say, ±150° instead of ±180° carves a wedge out of the full annulus. Redundant arms (recall Lesson 2's 7-DOF human arm) don't expand the workspace's outer boundary, but they do let the arm reach many points in more than one way, which becomes directly useful once obstacles are in the picture (Unit 4).

Example Two arms with the same total reach (L1 + L2 = 0.8 m) but different individual link lengths have different-shaped workspaces. An arm with L1 = 0.4 m, L2 = 0.4 m has an inner radius of |0.4 − 0.4| = 0 m — it can fold all the way back to its own base and reach every point in a full disk out to 0.8 m. An arm with L1 = 0.7 m, L2 = 0.1 m has an inner radius of |0.7 − 0.1| = 0.6 m — a much larger unreachable "hole" near the base, even though both arms have identical total reach.

The next lesson flips the question this whole unit has been building toward: instead of "where does the end-effector go for these joint values," it asks "what joint values put the end-effector exactly where I want it" — a much harder question, and the reason inverse kinematics gets its own lesson.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is a robot arm's workspace?

Workspace is defined in terms of reachable end-effector positions, not joint angles or robot footprint.

2. Two 2-link planar arms have the same total reach (L1 + L2), but different individual link lengths. Do they necessarily have the same workspace shape?

The outer radius depends only on the sum, but the inner radius depends on the difference |L1 − L2| — equal links give a fully reachable disk, very unequal links leave a large unreachable hole near the base.

3. A warehouse arm needs to reach 1.2 m from its base, but its two links only sum to 1.0 m of total length. What fixes this?

Workspace boundaries are set by link lengths and joint limits at design time — a geometric fact no control software can override.

4. Fill in the blank: the subset of a workspace reachable at every orientation, not just some orientation, is called the ___ workspace.

Dexterous workspace is the stricter standard: reachable with full orientation freedom, not just reachable at all.

5. For a 2-link planar arm with L1 = 0.6 m and L2 = 0.2 m, fill in the blank: the workspace's inner radius is ___ m.

Inner radius = |L1 − L2| = |0.6 − 0.2| = 0.4 m.
