What Makes a Robot a Robot?

Every robot is a machine, but not every machine is a robot — the difference is a loop that closes over what it senses.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Engineering 1 gave you the design process, and Computer Programming 1 gave you loops, conditionals, and functions. This course puts both to work on one specific kind of system: a robot. Before building anything, it's worth being precise about what that word actually commits you to — because "robot" gets used loosely enough in everyday speech that the engineering definition is easy to miss.

A body, a way to move it, a way to sense, and a way to decide

Definition A robot is a physical system built from four parts working together: a body made of rigid links connected by movable joints, actuators that supply the force or torque to move those joints, sensors that measure the robot's own state and its surroundings, and a controller that repeatedly decides what to do next based on what the sensors report.

That last part is the one people skip past. A wind-up toy car has a body and an actuator (the spring), but no sensors and no decision loop — it does the same thing every time regardless of what's in front of it. A robot, by contrast, is defined by that closed loop: sense, decide, act, and sense again. Everything this course covers — kinematics, perception, localization, planning, control, autonomy, manipulation — is really just filling in different pieces of that one loop in more and more detail.

Why the loop matters more than the hardware A CNC milling machine and a six-jointed robot arm can be built from nearly identical hardware — the same kinds of links, joints, and motors. What makes one a "robot" in the fuller sense and the other closer to automated machinery is what happens when the world doesn't match expectations. A CNC machine follows its pre-programmed toolpath blindly; if the workpiece has shifted, it cuts in the wrong place. A robot arm with force or vision feedback can notice the mismatch and correct for it. The hardware is similar; the loop is what changes the behavior.

This is also why Advanced Robotics builds directly on a prerequisite you already have: sensors, actuators, and control loops are Mechatronics-level fundamentals, and a robot is what you get when you compose many of those loops — one per joint, plus higher-level loops for navigation and decision-making — into a single integrated system that has to work as a whole, not as isolated parts.

Links and joints: the kinematic chain

The physical body of almost every robot — a manipulator arm, a mobile rover, a legged robot — is a series of rigid links connected by joints, called a kinematic chain. A joint is either revolute (it rotates, like an elbow) or prismatic (it slides along a line, like a piston). Chaining several of these together, base to tip, is what lets a small number of motors produce a rich range of positions and orientations at the far end of the chain — the part of the robot that actually does the work, called the end-effector.

Example A warehouse picking arm bolted to the floor is a kinematic chain: a fixed base, several revolute joints, rigid links between them, and a gripper (the end-effector) at the tip. A conveyor belt sorting the same packages is not a kinematic chain in this sense — it has one degree of motion (the belt), no joints connecting separate rigid bodies, and — critically — no sensing loop deciding where to place anything. It moves; it doesn't decide.

The next lesson gives this idea a number: exactly how much a kinematic chain can move is captured by its degrees of freedom, and that number turns out to drive almost every other design decision about a robot's body.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What distinguishes a robot from a simple automated machine like a wind-up toy?

The defining feature is the closed sense-decide-act loop. A wind-up toy has an actuator (the spring) but no sensing or decision-making, so it can't respond to a changed environment.

2. A CNC milling machine and a six-jointed robot arm can share nearly identical hardware. What most separates them, in the sense this lesson uses?

Hardware alone doesn't decide the label — a CNC machine that blindly follows a preset path versus an arm that senses and corrects is a difference in the control loop, not the mechanism.

3. In the kinematic-chain vocabulary of this lesson, what is a prismatic joint?

Prismatic joints slide linearly; revolute joints rotate. Both connect rigid links into a kinematic chain.

4. Fill in the blank: the part of a robot's kinematic chain that actually does the work — a gripper, a welding torch, a paint nozzle — is called the ___.

The end-effector is the working tip of the kinematic chain, at the far end from the fixed base.

5. Fill in the blank: a series of rigid links connected by movable joints, from a robot's base to its end-effector, is called a kinematic ___.

Kinematic chain is the standard term for this base-to-tip series of links and joints.
