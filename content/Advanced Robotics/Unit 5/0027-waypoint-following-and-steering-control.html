Waypoint Following and Steering Control

Knowing where you're supposed to be is only half the job — the other half is deciding, moment to moment, how hard to steer to actually get there.

← Lesson 26 Lesson 28 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 26 produced a timed trajectory: a target position for every instant. This lesson covers the steering side — the ongoing decision of how to turn the robot's wheels (or joints) so it actually tracks that moving target, not just aims at a fixed point once and hopes.

Definition Waypoint following is the general task of steering a robot through a sequence of intended positions in order. A common concrete strategy, pure pursuit, has the robot continuously aim toward a point on the path a fixed lookahead distance ahead of its current position — not the very next waypoint, and not the final goal, but a point that "chases" along the path as the robot advances.
Why aiming at a moving lookahead point beats aiming at the next fixed waypoint Steering directly at the next waypoint produces a path that cuts corners sharply and can oscillate — small position errors cause abrupt steering corrections, which cause more error, in an unstable back-and-forth. A lookahead point that moves smoothly along the path as the robot progresses produces gentler, more stable steering commands, and the size of the lookahead distance directly trades off responsiveness against smoothness: a short lookahead tracks the path tightly but can be twitchy; a long lookahead is smoother but cuts corners more.
Example A robot following a curving path with a 0.5 m lookahead distance continuously finds the point on the path 0.5 m ahead of its current position and steers toward it. As the robot moves, that target point advances along the curve too, always staying 0.5 m ahead — the robot is perpetually chasing a moving target rather than a sequence of fixed ones, which is exactly why the resulting steering commands are smooth rather than jerky.

Waypoint following gets the robot moving in roughly the right direction, continuously. What it doesn't do on its own is guarantee the robot stays precisely on the intended trajectory from Lesson 26 — small errors from wheel slip, timing mismatches, or an imperfect lookahead choice still accumulate. Closing that gap with real feedback is the next lesson's job.

1. What does the pure pursuit strategy have the robot steer toward?

Pure pursuit continuously aims at a point that advances along the path as the robot progresses, a fixed distance ahead — not a single fixed waypoint.

2. Why does steering directly at the next fixed waypoint tend to produce jerky or oscillating motion?

Aiming at a fixed point rather than a smoothly advancing target makes steering commands abrupt and prone to oscillation as the robot approaches and passes each waypoint.

3. What is the trade-off in choosing a pure pursuit controller's lookahead distance?

Lookahead distance directly trades path-tracking tightness against steering smoothness — a core tuning parameter for pure pursuit.

4. Fill in the blank: the general task of steering a robot through a sequence of intended positions in order is called ___ following.

Waypoint following is the broad task; pure pursuit is one common concrete strategy for accomplishing it.

5. Fill in the blank: in pure pursuit, the fixed distance ahead of the robot used to select the target point on the path is called the ___ distance.

Lookahead distance is the key tuning parameter of the pure pursuit steering strategy.
