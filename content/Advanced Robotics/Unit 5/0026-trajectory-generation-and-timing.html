Trajectory Generation and Timing

A path says where. A trajectory says where, and exactly when — and that second piece is what a motion controller actually needs.

← Lesson 25 Lesson 27 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 25 pointed out that a path alone has no timing. Trajectory generation is the process of adding that timing back in — turning a path into a specific function of time the robot can be commanded to follow.

Definition A trajectory is a path together with a specified timing — a function that gives the robot's intended position (and often velocity) at every instant in time, not just a sequence of points with no schedule attached. Trajectory generation is the process of computing this timing, typically respecting limits on the robot's maximum velocity and maximum acceleration.
Why acceleration limits matter as much as velocity limits A trajectory that commands a robot to jump instantly from standstill to top speed isn't physically achievable — real motors and wheels take time to change speed, and commanding an impossible instantaneous change just means the robot falls behind the commanded trajectory immediately. A well-generated trajectory ramps up smoothly (bounded by maximum acceleration), holds a cruising speed (bounded by maximum velocity), and ramps back down before reaching a stopping point — often called a trapezoidal velocity profile, from the shape it traces when velocity is plotted against time.
Worked example A robot needs to travel 3 m along a straight path leg, with a maximum velocity of 1 m/s and maximum acceleration of 0.5 m/s². Ramping from 0 to 1 m/s at 0.5 m/s² takes 2 s and covers 0.5·0.5·2² = 1 m. By symmetry, ramping back down from 1 m/s to 0 takes another 2 s and covers another 1 m. That leaves 3 − 1 − 1 = 1 m to cover at the 1 m/s cruise speed, taking 1 s. Total trip time: 2 + 1 + 2 = 5 s — a complete timing schedule the robot can actually execute, respecting both limits throughout.

Trajectory generation gives the robot a target position (and velocity) for every instant — the "where should I be right now" the next lesson's controller needs in order to know whether the robot is actually keeping up with that schedule, and if not, how to correct.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What distinguishes a trajectory from a plain path?

A path is just a sequence of positions; a trajectory specifies exactly when the robot should be at each one, respecting velocity and acceleration limits.

2. Why can't a trajectory command a robot to jump instantly from standstill to top speed?

Physical acceleration limits mean speed changes take real time — commanding an instantaneous jump is simply not achievable, so a good trajectory ramps up smoothly instead.

3. In a trapezoidal velocity profile, what do the three segments represent?

The trapezoid shape (velocity vs. time) comes from accelerating up to a cruise speed, holding it, then decelerating back down — all within the robot's physical limits.

4. Fill in the blank: a path together with specified timing — position (and often velocity) as a function of time — is called a ___.

Trajectory is the timed version of a path, which is what a motion controller actually needs to follow.

5. Fill in the blank: the velocity-vs-time shape produced by ramping up, cruising, and ramping down is often called a ___ velocity profile.

Trapezoidal velocity profiles are a standard, simple way to respect both velocity and acceleration limits.
