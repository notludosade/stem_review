1. What is the role of motion control in this course's pipeline?

Motion control turns a planned path (Unit 4's output) into the wheel/motor commands that actually execute it.

2. Fill in the blank: the key thing a trajectory adds that a plain path lacks is ___.

A path is just positions; a trajectory adds a schedule of when the robot should be at each position.

3. Answer both parts about trajectory generation:

(a) Why can't a trajectory command an instant jump from standstill to top speed?

(b) Fill in the blank: the ramp-up, cruise, ramp-down velocity shape is called a ___ profile.

(a) Physical acceleration limits mean speed changes take real time. (b) A trapezoidal velocity profile respects both velocity and acceleration limits.

4. In the pure pursuit steering strategy, what does the robot continuously steer toward?

Pure pursuit steers toward a point that advances along the path a fixed distance ahead, producing smoother motion than aiming at fixed waypoints.

5. Fill in the blank: in pure pursuit, the fixed distance ahead of the robot used to select the steering target is called the ___ distance.

Lookahead distance is the key tuning parameter of the pure pursuit steering strategy.

6. Answer both parts about feedback control for path tracking:

(a) What does path-tracking feedback control continuously measure?

(b) Fill in the blank: this feedback control depends directly on Unit 3's ___ to know the robot's actual current pose.

(a) Feedback control compares actual pose to the trajectory's intended pose and corrects the gap. (b) Without trustworthy localization, there is no way to measure the tracking error to correct.

7. Fill in the blank: the perpendicular deviation between a robot's actual position and its intended position on the trajectory is called ___.

Cross-track error is the standard term for this perpendicular deviation from the intended path.

8. Why do real systems often combine pure pursuit with explicit feedback correction, rather than using pure pursuit alone?

Pure pursuit's geometric steering doesn't directly respond to measured deviation the way proportional feedback correction does.

9. Why do A*-generated paths often contain sharp corners?

A*'s output reflects the discrete graph it searched — nothing in the search process favors smooth motion, only low path cost.

10. Why must a smoothed path still be checked against the original obstacles?

Smoothing reshapes geometry near corners, which risks cutting into an obstacle if not re-checked — a refinement on a validated path, not an independent safety guarantee.
