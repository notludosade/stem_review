Feedback Control for Path Tracking

Steering toward a lookahead point gets a robot roughly on track. Feedback control is what keeps measuring and correcting so 'roughly' becomes 'reliably.'

← Lesson 27 Lesson 29 → Contents Glossary →

This course's prerequisites already gave you the core idea of feedback control: measure the current state, compare it to the desired state, and command a correction proportional to the difference — the same idea behind a PID controller regulating a single joint or a single motor's speed. This lesson applies that same idea to the specific job of keeping a whole robot on its planned trajectory.

Definition Path-tracking feedback control continuously measures the difference between the robot's actual pose (from localization, Unit 3) and its intended pose on the trajectory at that same instant (from Lesson 26), then commands a correction sized to close that gap. This is Unit 3 and Unit 5 meeting directly: tracking a trajectory well is impossible without a trustworthy sense of where the robot currently actually is.
Why pure pursuit alone isn't the whole answer Lesson 27's pure pursuit steers based on the path's geometry — it doesn't explicitly measure "how far off-track am I right now" and correct proportionally to that specific error the way a PID-style feedback loop does. In practice, real systems often combine both: a geometric strategy like pure pursuit to decide roughly which way to steer, layered with feedback correction that responds specifically to measured tracking error — wheel slip, an imperfect lookahead choice, or accumulated small disturbances the geometric strategy alone wouldn't directly notice or correct for.
Example A robot's trajectory says it should be at (2.0, 1.0) m, 3 s into its run. Localization reports its actual position as (2.0, 1.15) m — 0.15 m off to one side, likely from a small amount of wheel slip on a turn. A feedback controller sees this 0.15 m cross-track error and commands a proportional steering correction back toward the trajectory, continuously, rather than letting that error persist or compound the way an uncorrected dead-reckoning estimate would (Lesson 15).

The result of combining timed trajectory generation, waypoint-following steering, and feedback correction is a robot that reliably executes the path Unit 4 planned — not perfectly, but self-correcting, in the same spirit as every control loop this course's prerequisites already introduced. One piece is still missing: making the underlying path itself easier to follow in the first place, which is where this unit closes.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does path-tracking feedback control continuously measure and act on?

Feedback control compares actual pose (from localization) to the trajectory's intended pose at that same moment, then commands a correction sized to the gap.

2. Why does path-tracking feedback control depend directly on Unit 3's localization?

Without a trustworthy pose estimate from localization, there's no way to measure the tracking error that feedback control is meant to correct.

3. Why do real systems often combine a geometric strategy like pure pursuit with explicit feedback correction, rather than relying on pure pursuit alone?

Pure pursuit's geometric steering doesn't directly respond to measured deviation the way proportional feedback correction does — combining both covers more failure modes.

4. Fill in the blank: the difference between a robot's actual position and its intended position on the trajectory, measured perpendicular to the path, is often called ___-track error.

Cross-track error is the standard term for this perpendicular deviation from the intended path.

5. Fill in the blank: a feedback controller commands a correction ___ to the size of the measured tracking error.

Proportional correction — sizing the response to the size of the error — is the core feedback-control idea this lesson applied to path tracking.
