Standing Waves and Resonance

Trap a wave between two fixed points and something new happens — it stops traveling and instead appears to oscillate in place, at only certain special frequencies.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Standing Waves A standing wave forms when two identical waves travel in opposite directions through the same medium (typically a wave and its own reflection) and interfere, producing fixed points of zero displacement (nodes) and fixed points of maximum displacement (antinodes) that don't move.
Harmonics on a String Fixed at Both Ends Only wavelengths satisfying λn = 2L/n (n = 1, 2, 3, ...) fit a string of length L with both ends fixed (nodes at both ends). Combined with v = fλ, this gives allowed frequencies fn = nv/(2L) — the harmonic series.
Why only certain frequencies produce a standing wave at all A standing wave requires the two fixed ends to both be nodes (since the string can't move where it's clamped) — but as a wave reflects back and forth, it only reinforces itself constructively (building a stable, unmoving pattern) at wavelengths where a whole number of half-wavelengths fits exactly between the two ends. Any other wavelength reflects out of phase with itself on each pass, canceling out through destructive interference rather than building up — this is exactly why a guitar string only rings at specific pitches (its harmonics) rather than an arbitrary frequency you might pluck it at.
Resonance Resonance occurs when a system is driven at one of its natural (harmonic) frequencies, causing the amplitude of oscillation to grow dramatically as energy is added in phase with the existing motion, cycle after cycle.
Example: finding the fundamental frequency A string of length 0.8 m has wave speed 200 m/s. The fundamental (n = 1): λ₁ = 2L/1 = 1.6 m. f₁ = v/λ₁ = 200/1.6 = 125 Hz.
Example: finding a higher harmonic Using the same string, find the third harmonic. f₃ = 3v/(2L) = 3(200)/(2×0.8) = 600/1.6 = 375 Hz — exactly three times the fundamental, as harmonics are always integer multiples of f₁.

1. Why does a string fixed at both ends only support specific standing-wave frequencies?

Only wavelengths fitting the boundary condition (nodes at both fixed ends) build up through constructive interference; all others cancel out.

2. At a node in a standing wave, the displacement of the medium is:

Nodes are fixed points of zero displacement; antinodes are points of maximum displacement.

3. A string of length 1 m has wave speed 100 m/s. What is its fundamental frequency?

λ1 = 2L = 2 m. f1 = v/λ1 = 100/2 = 50 Hz.

4. Fill in the blank: driving a system at one of its natural frequencies, causing amplitude to grow dramatically, is called ___.

Resonance occurs when energy is added in phase with the natural oscillation, cycle after cycle.
