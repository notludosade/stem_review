Electric Potential

"Voltage" is potential energy per unit charge — a property of a location in space, not of any particular charge sitting there.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Electric Potential V = U/q = kQ/r (unit: volt, V = J/C), the electric potential at a point due to a source charge Q — potential energy per unit charge, a property of the location itself, independent of whatever test charge might be placed there. The work needed to move a charge between two points is W = qΔV, or equivalently ΔU = qΔV.
Why potential (V) and field (E) are related but different quantities Field E is a vector describing force per unit charge; potential V is a scalar describing energy per unit charge — no direction, just a number at each point. This mirrors the field/potential-energy split from Lesson 2 and 4: just as E = F/q strips force down to "per charge," V = U/q strips potential energy down to "per charge." Potential is often easier to work with than field precisely because it's a scalar — you can add potentials from multiple sources with simple addition, no vector components required.
Common Usage: Voltage as a Difference In circuits (Unit 3), what matters is almost always the potential difference, ΔV, between two points — commonly just called "voltage." A 9-volt battery, for instance, maintains a 9 V potential difference between its terminals.
Example: potential from a point charge Find the electric potential 0.4 m from a point charge Q = +6×10⁻⁶ C. V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(6×10⁻⁶)/0.4 = 53,940/0.4 = 134,850 V.
Example: work to move a charge through a potential difference How much work is required to move a charge of +2×10⁻⁶ C from a point at 100 V to a point at 400 V? W = qΔV = (2×10⁻⁶)(400 − 100) = (2×10⁻⁶)(300) = 6×10⁻⁴ J — positive work is required, since a positive charge naturally moves toward lower potential, so moving it toward higher potential takes external work.

1. Electric potential differs from electric field in that potential is:

V = U/q is a scalar (just a number at each point), while E = F/q is a vector with both magnitude and direction.

2. What is a key practical advantage of working with potential (V) rather than field (E) when combining effects from multiple source charges?

Because V has no direction, combining multiple sources is straightforward scalar addition, unlike vector addition needed for E.

3. How much work is needed to move a +5×10⁻⁶ C charge from a point at 200 V to a point at 50 V?

W = qΔV = (5×10⁻⁶)(50 − 200) = (5×10⁻⁶)(−150) = −7.5×10⁻⁴ J — negative, meaning the field itself does positive work moving a positive charge toward lower potential, so no external work input is required.

4. Fill in the blank: potential energy per unit charge at a point in space, measured in volts, is called ___.

Electric potential (V = U/q) is a property of a location, independent of any particular test charge placed there.
