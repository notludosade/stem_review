Compton Scattering and de Broglie Wavelength

Photons carrying momentum like particles was the final proof light isn't purely a wave. Then de Broglie asked the reverse question: what if matter has wave properties too?

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Compton Scattering When a photon collides with a free electron, both energy and momentum are conserved exactly as in a particle collision — the scattered photon loses energy (and thus gains wavelength) by an amount depending on the scattering angle. This behavior only makes sense if photons carry real, particle-like momentum, p = h/λ (Lesson 2).
Why Compton scattering settled the photon-momentum question The photoelectric effect (Lesson 1) showed light exchanges energy in discrete amounts, but skeptics could still argue this was just an unusual absorption process rather than proof that photons carry true momentum. Compton scattering removed that doubt: the photon-electron collision obeys the same conservation-of-momentum bookkeeping used for two billiard balls colliding (Unit 1 mechanics territory), and the measured wavelength shift matches the prediction only if the photon is treated as a real particle with momentum p = h/λ — settling decisively that photons are not just energy packets, but momentum-carrying particles as well.
The de Broglie Wavelength Louis de Broglie proposed that if light (a wave) has particle-like momentum, perhaps matter (particles) has wave-like properties too: λ = h/p = h/(mv). Every moving object has an associated wavelength — though for everyday objects, this wavelength is so absurdly small it's completely unobservable.
Why you never notice your own de Broglie wavelength λ = h/(mv) has Planck's constant (≈6.6×10⁻³⁴ J·s) in the numerator — for any object with everyday mass and speed, the resulting wavelength is many orders of magnitude smaller than an atomic nucleus, utterly undetectable by any practical means. Only for extremely low-mass particles like electrons does mv become small enough that λ = h/(mv) grows to a measurable, physically relevant size — which is exactly why electron wave behavior (like diffraction through crystal lattices) is observable in the lab, while a thrown baseball's wave nature never is.
Example: de Broglie wavelength of an electron An electron (m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg) moves at 2×10⁶ m/s. λ = h/(mv) = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/((9.11×10⁻³¹)(2×10⁶)) = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/(1.82×10⁻²⁴) ≈ 3.64×10⁻¹⁰ m — comparable to atomic spacing, which is why electron diffraction through crystals is observable.
Example: de Broglie wavelength of a thrown ball A 0.2 kg ball moves at 10 m/s. λ = h/(mv) = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/(0.2×10) = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/2 ≈ 3.3×10⁻³⁴ m — vastly smaller than any atom, confirming why we never observe wave behavior in thrown balls.

1. Compton scattering is significant because it demonstrated that:

The measured wavelength shift in Compton scattering only matches predictions if photons are treated as momentum-carrying particles.

2. Why is the de Broglie wavelength of a thrown baseball never observable, while an electron's often is?

The much larger mv for a baseball drives its de Broglie wavelength far below any observable scale, unlike an electron's.

3. Find the de Broglie wavelength of a proton (m ≈ 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg) moving at 3×10⁵ m/s.

λ = h/(mv) = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/((1.67×10⁻²⁷)(3×10⁵)) = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/(5.01×10⁻²²) ≈ 1.3×10⁻¹² m.

4. Fill in the blank: the wavelength λ = h/(mv) associated with any moving mass is called the ___.

The de Broglie wavelength extends wave-particle duality from light to all matter.
