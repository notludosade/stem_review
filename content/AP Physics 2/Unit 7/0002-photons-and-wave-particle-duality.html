Photons and Wave-Particle Duality

Unit 6 proved light is a wave. Lesson 1 just showed light also behaves like discrete particles. Both are true — this lesson makes peace between them.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

The Photon A photon is a discrete packet (quantum) of light energy, carrying energy E = hf and momentum p = h/λ. Light is neither purely a wave nor purely a stream of particles — it exhibits both behaviors depending on the experiment.
Wave-Particle Duality Wave-particle duality is the principle that light (and, as Lesson 4 will show, matter too) displays wave-like behavior (interference, diffraction — Unit 6) in some experiments and particle-like behavior (discrete energy exchange, momentum — this unit) in others.
Why "wave" and "particle" aren't actually contradictory once you drop the classical picture Everyday intuition builds "wave" and "particle" from macroscopic experience — ripples on water versus billiard balls — and these two pictures do seem mutually exclusive at that scale. But light is neither of those familiar things; it's a quantum entity whose behavior only resembles one classical picture or the other depending on what you measure. Double-slit interference (Unit 6) probes light's wave-like accumulation of amplitude; the photoelectric effect (Lesson 1) probes its particle-like all-or-nothing energy exchange. Both descriptions are correct simultaneously — the contradiction exists only if you insist reality must match one of two limited, human-scale metaphors.
Example: photon momentum Find the momentum of a photon with wavelength 600 nm. p = h/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/(600×10⁻⁹) ≈ 1.10×10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s.
Example: comparing photon energy across the spectrum Compare a photon of red light (λ = 700 nm) to one of violet light (λ = 400 nm). Since E = hf = hc/λ, shorter wavelength means higher energy: violet's photon energy is 700/400 = 1.75 times greater than red's — consistent with violet light causing photoelectric ejection more easily than red light of the same intensity.

1. Wave-particle duality means that light:

Both wave and particle descriptions are simultaneously valid, each surfacing depending on what property is being measured.

2. Why do "wave" and "particle" seem contradictory, even though light is consistently both?

The apparent contradiction comes from forcing quantum behavior into two limited classical pictures, not from an actual inconsistency in nature.

3. Find the momentum of a photon with wavelength 500 nm.

p = h/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/(500×10⁻⁹) ≈ 1.33×10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s.

4. Fill in the blank: a discrete packet of light energy, carrying E = hf and momentum p = h/λ, is called a ___.

The photon is the particle-like quantum of light, existing alongside its wave description.
