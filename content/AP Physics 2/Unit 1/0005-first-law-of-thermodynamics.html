The First Law of Thermodynamics

Energy conservation, one more time — now tracking heat and work flowing into and out of a gas instead of a single object's motion.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

The First Law of Thermodynamics ΔU = Q − W, where ΔU is the change in a system's internal energy, Q is heat added to the system, and W is work done by the system on its surroundings (such as an expanding gas pushing a piston). This is energy conservation applied to a thermodynamic system.
Why this is the same idea as AP Physics 1's energy conservation, just relabeled This directly parallels AP Physics 1's conservation of mechanical energy: energy can move between forms and between a system and its surroundings, but the total is always accounted for. Here, a gas's internal energy (Lesson 3) changes exactly according to how much heat flows in and how much work the gas itself does pushing outward — nothing is created or destroyed, only transferred and converted, exactly as with kinetic and potential energy in AP Physics 1, Unit 3.
Special Thermodynamic Processes Isothermal (constant T, so ΔU = 0, meaning Q = W): heat in equals work out. Adiabatic (no heat exchange, Q = 0, so ΔU = −W): all internal-energy change comes from work alone. Isochoric (constant volume, so W = 0, meaning ΔU = Q): all heat added goes directly into internal energy, since no work is done without a volume change.
Why no volume change means no work done by a gas A gas does work by pushing a boundary (like a piston) outward through some distance — work requires a force acting through a displacement (AP Physics 1, Unit 3), and if the gas's volume never changes, that boundary never moves, so W = 0 regardless of how much pressure the gas exerts.
Example: applying the first law A gas absorbs 500 J of heat while doing 200 J of work expanding against a piston. ΔU = Q − W = 500 − 200 = 300 J — the gas's internal energy rises by 300 J, since not all the absorbed heat went into external work.
Example: an adiabatic compression A gas is compressed adiabatically (Q = 0), with 150 J of work done on it (so W, work done by the gas, is −150 J). ΔU = Q − W = 0 − (−150) = 150 J — the gas's internal energy rises entirely due to the work done on it, with no heat exchange at all. This is part of why a bicycle pump gets warm when compressing air quickly.

1. The first law of thermodynamics, ΔU = Q − W, is fundamentally an expression of:

The first law is energy conservation applied to a thermodynamic system, directly paralleling mechanical energy conservation from AP Physics 1.

2. Why does no work get done by a gas during an isochoric (constant-volume) process?

No volume change means no boundary displacement, so W = 0 regardless of pressure, and all added heat goes directly into internal energy.

3. A gas absorbs 800 J of heat and does 300 J of work expanding. What is the change in its internal energy?

ΔU = Q − W = 800 − 300 = 500 J.

4. Fill in the blank: a thermodynamic process with no heat exchange with the surroundings (Q = 0) is called ___.

In an adiabatic process, all internal energy change comes from work alone: ΔU = −W.
