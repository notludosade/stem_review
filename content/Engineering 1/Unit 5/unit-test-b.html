Energy & Systems Thinking — work, power, and conservation of energy, systems thinking and feedback loops, and efficiency and losses. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A crane holds a shipping container stationary in the air for several minutes using a large upward force. According to the physics definition of work, how much work does the crane do on the container during this time?

W = F × d requires actual displacement — force alone, without motion, transfers no energy by this definition.

2. Fill in the blank: total energy before and after any physical process is always exactly equal — a consequence of the law of ___ of energy.

Conservation of energy is one of the most rigorously tested principles in physics — every attempted perpetual motion machine has failed without exception.

3. A stagehand raises a 300 N prop 3 meters in 6 seconds; another raises an identical prop the same height in 2 seconds. Which comparison is correct?

Both lifts do W = F × d = 300 × 3 = 900 J of work; power is the rate of that transfer, so the faster lift needs more power for the identical work.

4. Fill in the blank: the energy of motion — what a moving car or a falling ball possesses — is called ___ energy.

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; a falling object's potential energy converts into kinetic energy as it speeds up.

5. A 60-watt light bulb is left on for 30 seconds. How much energy does it use?

Energy = P × t = 60 × 30 = 1,800 joules, directly from P = W / t.

6. A home HVAC system, treated as a system, takes in electricity and outside air and produces conditioned air plus waste heat. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the main benefit of reasoning about the HVAC system this way when troubleshooting an unusually high electric bill?

(b) Fill in the blank: a system takes in one or more ___, transforms them internally, and produces one or more outputs.

Systems thinking lets an engineer zoom to whatever level of detail a specific question needs, treating the whole HVAC unit as one box when that's the right level.

7. A room thermostat measures temperature and switches the heater on when it's too cold, off when it's too warm, keeping the room near a set target. Answer both parts:

(a) What type of feedback loop is this?

(b) Fill in the blank: unlike the thermostat's stabilizing loop, ___ feedback amplifies deviation rather than correcting it, and is generally something engineers actively avoid in control system design.

Negative feedback is self-correcting and stable, which is exactly why engineers design most control systems around it rather than positive feedback.

8. A pump takes in 500 W of electrical power and delivers 400 W of useful hydraulic power, the rest lost to heat and friction. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the pump's efficiency?

(b) Fill in the blank: efficiency is the ratio of useful output to total ___.

η = useful output / total input = 400 / 500 = 0.80, or 80%.

9. A well-designed electric motor is roughly 90% efficient. Answer both parts:

(a) What happens to the other 10% of the input energy, given that conservation of energy forbids it from simply vanishing?

(b) Fill in the blank: losses from winding resistance and bearing friction in a motor ultimately end up as ___, not destroyed energy.

Conservation of energy means none of it vanishes — "lost" energy is really energy converted into a form the system wasn't built to use, primarily heat.

10. A design team is confident that with sufficiently advanced future materials, they'll eventually eliminate all losses and reach 100% efficiency. Answer both parts:

(a) What does the second law of thermodynamics say about this expectation?

(b) Fill in the blank: every real efficiency improvement in engineering history has ___ losses toward, but never all the way to, the impossible 100% ceiling.

This is a hard physical limit from the second law of thermodynamics — real design work minimizes losses, but can never eliminate them entirely.
