Lenz's Law, Self-Inductance, and RL Circuits

Faraday's law says an EMF gets induced. It doesn't say which way the current flows — that direction is never arbitrary, and getting it backwards would mean building a machine that generates energy from nothing.

← Lesson 19 Lesson 21 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Lenz's Law An induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change in flux that produced it. If flux through a loop is increasing, the induced current creates its own magnetic field opposing that increase; if flux is decreasing, the induced current creates a field reinforcing it, fighting the decrease. This is exactly the minus sign in Faraday's law, EMF = −dΦ_B/dt, made concrete: a direction rule, not just an afterthought.
Why Lenz's Law Has to Be True Suppose it were backwards — suppose the induced current reinforced the change in flux instead of opposing it. A small initial increase in flux would induce a current that increases the flux further, which induces more current, which increases the flux even more — current spiraling upward forever with no energy input at all. That's a free-energy machine, and those don't exist. Lenz's law isn't a separate, independent law of nature bolted onto Faraday's law — it's required by conservation of energy. It's also why pushing a magnet toward a loop always feels like pushing against resistance: the induced current's own field pushes back on the magnet, and the mechanical work needed to overcome that push is exactly what becomes the electrical energy delivered to the circuit.
Definition — Self-Inductance A coil carrying a changing current creates its own changing flux through itself, and by Faraday's law, that induces an EMF back on the coil — opposing the current's own change, by Lenz's law. EMF = −L dI/dt, where L is the coil's self-inductance (unit: the henry, H = V·s/A), a geometric property of the coil itself, playing the same role for current that capacitance plays for voltage.
Worked Derivation — The RL Circuit A battery of EMF ε, resistor R, and inductor L in series, switch closed at t = 0. Kirchhoff's loop rule: ε − IR − L(dI/dt) = 0, a first-order differential equation in I(t) — structurally identical to Unit 5's RC circuit equation with I standing in for charge's role. Solving with I(0) = 0 gives I(t) = (ε/R)(1 − e^(−t/τ)), charging toward I_max = ε/R, with time constant τ = L/R. Remove the battery from an already-current-carrying LR loop instead, and the current decays as I(t) = I₀e^(−t/τ) — the same exponential decay shape RC discharge used.

Every RC result from Unit 5 has a direct RL mirror — swap "charge can't jump instantaneously" for "current can't jump instantaneously," and swap RC for L/R.
Worked Example A series RL circuit has L = 0.1 H, R = 20 Ω, and ε = 12 V. τ = L/R = 0.1/20 = 0.005 s = 5 ms. I_max = ε/R = 12/20 = 0.6 A. At t = τ, the current has reached the same universal 63.2% of its final value that RC charging reaches at one time constant: I(τ) ≈ (0.632)(0.6) ≈ 0.379 A.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A bar magnet's north pole approaches a wire loop head-on. By Lenz's law, the induced current in the loop:

Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change that caused it. An approaching north pole means increasing flux into the loop's face, so the induced current opposes that increase by presenting its own north pole toward the magnet — a repulsive force.

2. Fill in the blank: an RL circuit has L = 40 mH and R = 8 Ω. Its time constant τ is ___ ms.

τ = L/R = 0.040/8 = 0.005 s = 5 ms.

3. Suppose induced currents worked backwards — reinforcing, rather than opposing, the change in flux that caused them. What would this imply?

Reinforcement instead of opposition creates positive feedback — current growing without bound and without any energy source — which is exactly why Lenz's law is required by, not separate from, conservation of energy.

4. An inductor in a circuit opposes a sudden change in ___, the same role a capacitor plays opposing a sudden change in ___.

An inductor's EMF = −L dI/dt fights any sudden jump in current; a capacitor's charge q = CV can't jump instantaneously either, so it fights any sudden jump in voltage. The two components are mirror images of each other.
