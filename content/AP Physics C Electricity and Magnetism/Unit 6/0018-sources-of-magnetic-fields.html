Sources of Magnetic Fields

Moving charges don't just feel magnetic forces — they create magnetic fields too. The Biot-Savart law builds that field up from first principles; Ampère's law gets the same answer in one line whenever the geometry is symmetric enough to use it.

← Lesson 17 Unit 7, Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — The Biot-Savart Law A tiny current element I dl creates a magnetic field contribution at a point a displacement r away: dB = (μ₀/4π)(I dl × r̂)/r², where r̂ is the unit vector from the element toward the field point and μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A is the permeability of free space. Total B comes from integrating dB over the entire current path — the magnetic analog of building up E from dE = kdq/r² for a continuous charge distribution.
Result — Field of a Long Straight Wire Integrating the Biot-Savart law over an infinite straight wire carrying current I gives a clean result at perpendicular distance r from the wire: B = μ₀I/(2πr). The field lines form circles centered on the wire, direction set by the right-hand rule — thumb along the current, fingers curl in the direction B circulates. A wire carrying I = 10 A produces B = (4π×10⁻⁷)(10)/[2π(0.05)] = 4×10⁻⁵ T at r = 0.05 m — falling off as 1/r, not 1/r², since this is the field of an infinite line, not a point.
Why Ampère's Law Is the Magnetic Analog of Gauss's Law Ampère's law, ∮B·dl = μ₀I_enc, relates the circulation of B around a closed loop to the current enclosed by that loop — exactly parallel to how Gauss's law relates the flux of E through a closed surface to the charge enclosed. Just as Gauss's law becomes trivial algebra whenever symmetry lets you choose a surface where E is constant and always parallel (or perpendicular) to the surface, Ampère's law becomes trivial algebra whenever you can choose an "Amperian loop" where B is constant in magnitude and always parallel to dl — a circle around a straight wire, or a rectangle straddling the wall of a long solenoid, are the two classic choices.
Worked Example — Field Inside a Solenoid For an ideal long solenoid with n turns per unit length carrying current I, a rectangular Amperian loop with one side deep inside the solenoid and one side far outside gives B·(length) = μ₀I_enc = μ₀(n·length)(I), so B = μ₀nI — uniform inside, essentially zero outside. With n = 2000 turns/m and I = 3 A: B = (4π×10⁻⁷)(2000)(3) ≈ 7.54×10⁻³ T, a field over a hundred times stronger than the straight wire's field from the previous box, entirely because the solenoid's many loops stack their contributions instead of spreading out in circles.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A long straight wire carries current straight up out of the page. Looking down at the page from the direction the current is coming toward you, the magnetic field circles around the wire:

Right-hand rule: thumb points along the current (toward the viewer, out of the page), fingers curl counterclockwise as seen from that same viewpoint — that's the direction B circles.

2. Fill in the blank: a long straight wire carries I = 15 A. At r = 0.03 m, the field is B ≈ ___ ×10⁻⁴ T (enter just the coefficient).

B = μ₀I/(2πr) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(15)/[2π(0.03)] = (2×10⁻⁷)(15)/0.03 = 1.0×10⁻⁴ T.

3. Ampère's law, ∮B·dl = μ₀I_enc, is fastest to use for actually computing B when:

Just as with Gauss's law, Ampère's law is always true but only algebraically easy when symmetry pulls B out of the integral as a constant — a circular loop around a straight wire or a rectangle through a solenoid wall are the standard symmetric choices.

4. Fill in the blank: an ideal solenoid has n = 1750 turns/m carrying I = 4 A. The interior field is B ≈ ___ ×10⁻³ T (2 decimal places).

B = μ₀nI = (4π×10⁻⁷)(1750)(4) ≈ 8.80×10⁻³ T.
