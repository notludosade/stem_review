Magnetic Force on Currents and Torque on Loops

Zoom out from a single moving charge to a whole wire full of them, and F = qv × B scales up to F = IL × B. Bend that wire into a loop and the net force vanishes — but a torque survives, and that torque is the entire physical basis of a DC motor.

Definition — Force on a Current-Carrying Wire A straight wire segment of length L, carrying current I through a uniform field B, feels F = IL × B, with L pointing along the wire in the direction of conventional current. Magnitude: F = BIL sinθ, θ the angle between the wire and B. This follows directly from the single-charge law: a wire of cross-sectional area A and n charge carriers per volume, each drifting at v_d, contains nAL charges, each feeling qv_d × B — summing all of them gives a total force of (nAL)(qv_d × B) = (nqv_dA)(L̂ × B) = IL × B, since I = nqv_dA. Same right-hand rule as before: point fingers along the current, curl toward B, thumb gives F.
Definition — Torque on a Current Loop A planar loop of area A carrying current I sits in a uniform field B. The forces on opposite sides of the loop are equal and opposite, so the net force on any closed current loop in a uniform field is always exactly zero. But those opposing forces don't act along the same line, so they produce a net torque: τ = IA × B, magnitude τ = IAB sinθ, where θ is the angle between the loop's normal vector (direction via the right-hand rule, curling fingers with the current) and B. Defining the loop's magnetic moment μ = IA (a vector along the normal) compresses this to τ = μ × B — the same mathematical form as the electric dipole torque τ = p × E from earlier in this course.
Why This Is the Physical Basis of a DC Motor Torque τ = IAB sinθ is maximal when the loop's plane is parallel to B (its normal perpendicular to B, θ = 90°) and drops to zero when the loop's plane is perpendicular to B (its normal aligned with B, θ = 0°) — that zero-torque orientation is the loop's stable equilibrium, where it would simply stop if left alone. A real DC motor cheats this stopping point with a commutator: a mechanical switch that flips the current's direction every half-turn, right as the loop would otherwise settle into equilibrium, so instead of stopping it gets shoved past and keeps spinning in one direction continuously.
Worked Example — Force on a Wire, Torque on a Loop A straight wire segment L = 0.3 m carries I = 5 A perpendicular to B = 0.6 T: F = BIL = (0.6)(5)(0.3) = 0.9 N. Separately, a rectangular loop 0.1 m × 0.05 m (area A = 0.005 m²) carrying I = 2 A sits with its plane parallel to a field B = 0.8 T (maximum-torque orientation, θ = 90°): τ = IAB = (2)(0.005)(0.8) = 0.008 N·m = 8×10⁻³ N·m.

1. Current flows through a straight wire in the +x direction, in a field B pointing in the +z direction. Using F = IL × B, the force on the wire points:

x̂ × ẑ = −ŷ (since ẑ × x̂ = ŷ, reversing the order flips the sign), and current is positive, so F points in the −y direction.

2. Fill in the blank: a wire segment L = 0.4 m carries I = 6 A perpendicular to B = 0.5 T. The force on the wire is F = ___ N.

F = BIL sinθ = (0.5)(6)(0.4)(sin 90°) = 1.2 N.

3. The net force on a closed current loop of any shape, sitting in a uniform magnetic field, is:

In a uniform field, the forces on opposite current elements always cancel in pairs regardless of the loop's shape — the net force is zero, even though the net torque generally isn't.

4. A current loop's torque τ = IAB sinθ (θ measured from the loop's normal to B) is zero when:

sinθ = 0 when θ = 0°, i.e. when the normal vector is aligned with B — that's the loop's plane sitting perpendicular to B, its stable equilibrium orientation with no net torque.
