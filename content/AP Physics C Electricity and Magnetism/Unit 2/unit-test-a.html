1. A point charge of 2 μC creates a potential at a distance of 0.2 m. What is that potential? (k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²)

V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(2×10⁻⁶)/(0.2) = 17,980/0.2 = 89,900 ≈ 8.99×10⁴ V.

2. Two point charges, both +5 μC, sit 1.0 m apart. What is the potential at the point exactly midway between them (0.5 m from each)?

Potential is a scalar: V₁ = V₂ = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(5×10⁻⁶)/(0.5) = 89,900 V each, and they add directly (unlike the field, which does cancel here by symmetry): V_net = 89,900 + 89,900 = 179,800 ≈ 1.80×10⁵ V.

3. A ring of radius 0.3 m carries total charge 4 μC. What is the potential on its axis at x = 0.4 m from the center? (r = √(x²+R²) = 0.5 m)

V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(4×10⁻⁶)/(0.5) = 35,960/0.5 = 71,920 ≈ 7.19×10⁴ V, using r = √(x²+R²), not x or R alone.

4. Electric field lines and equipotential surfaces always intersect at:

Since V doesn't change along an equipotential surface, E can have no component along it — the two are always perpendicular.

5. Inside the material of an isolated conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field is:

Any leftover interior field would keep pushing free charge until it redistributed enough to cancel it — equilibrium means E = 0 throughout the conducting material.

6. A point charge of 1 μC sits at the origin. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the potential at r = 0.2 m is ___ V.

(b) If r were instead doubled (charge unchanged), the new potential would be:

(a) V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(1×10⁻⁶)/(0.2) = 8990/0.2 = 44,950 V. (b) V falls off as 1/r, one power of r slower than E's 1/r² — doubling r halves V, not quarters it.

7. Two point charges, both +6 μC, sit 0.8 m apart. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the potential at the point exactly midway between them (0.4 m from each) is ___ V.

(b) Compared to the electric field at that same midpoint, this potential:

(a) V₁ = V₂ = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(6×10⁻⁶)/(0.4) = 53,940/0.4 = 134,850 V; V_net = 134,850 + 134,850 = 269,700 V. (b) The field vectors from two equal charges point opposite ways at the midpoint and cancel to zero, but potential is scalar and both contributions add — a direct illustration of why V and E don't have to agree in behavior.

8. A ring of radius 0.3 m carries total charge 8 μC. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the potential on its axis at x = 0.4 m (r = √(x²+R²) = 0.5 m) is ___ V.

(b) For x much larger than R, V(x) approaches:

(a) V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(8×10⁻⁶)/(0.5) = 71,920/0.5 = 143,840 V. (b) When x ≫ R, √(x²+R²) ≈ x, so V(x) ≈ kQ/x — any localized charge looks like a point charge from far enough away.

9. An isolated conductor is in electrostatic equilibrium. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: one point on its surface is measured at V = 750 V. At the conductor's interior center, V = ___ V.

(b) Any excess charge on this conductor must reside entirely on its outer surface because:

(a) A conductor in equilibrium is one single equipotential object — every point, interior or surface, shares the same V = 750 V. (b) Since E = 0 on any Gaussian surface drawn just inside the conductor, Gauss's law forces Q_enc = 0 there, stranding all excess charge on the outer surface.

10. Moving a charge along an equipotential surface. Answer both parts:

(a) The work done moving a charge along an equipotential surface is:

(b) Fill in the blank: if E had a nonzero component along a surface, V would have to ___ as you moved along that surface — contradicting what "equipotential" means.

(a) W = qΔV = q(0) = 0 anywhere on a single equipotential surface, regardless of path. (b) A surface-parallel field component would mean V changes along the surface — but "equipotential" means V is constant there, so E can only point perpendicular to it.
