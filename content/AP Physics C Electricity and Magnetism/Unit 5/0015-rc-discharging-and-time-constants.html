RC Discharging and Time Constants

Disconnect the battery from a charged capacitor and let it push its own charge back out through a resistor. Same time constant, same exponential shape, opposite direction — charging and discharging are two faces of one framework.

← Lesson 14 Unit 6, Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Setup — The Loop Rule Around a Discharging Capacitor A capacitor, already charged to Q₀ = CV₀, is connected across a resistor R with no battery anywhere in the loop. With no EMF term, the loop rule reads Q/C − IR = 0, and now the current is charge leaving the capacitor: I = −dQ/dt. Substituting gives −R(dQ/dt) = Q/C, or dQ/dt = −Q/(RC) — the same kind of first-order differential equation as charging, but now proportional to −Q instead of to (Cε − Q).
Method — Solving the Discharge Equation Separating variables: dQ/Q = −dt/(RC). Integrating with the initial condition Q(0) = Q₀ gives Q(t) = Q₀e^(−t/RC), and dividing by C converts this to V(t) = V₀e^(−t/RC). Differentiating gives the discharge current I(t) = (V₀/R)e^(−t/RC) = I₀e^(−t/RC). Compare this side by side with charging: charging rises, V(t) = V_max(1 − e^(−t/τ)); discharging falls, V(t) = V₀e^(−t/τ) — same τ = RC in both, just one curve climbing toward V_max and the other decaying toward zero.
Why "Fully Charged" and "Fully Discharged" Are Both Approximations At t = τ, charging has reached 63.2% of V_max while discharging has fallen to 36.8% of V₀ — note these are complements of each other (0.632 + 0.368 = 1), which makes sense since both curves are built from the same e^(−t/τ) factor, just added to or subtracted from 1. Neither exponential ever mathematically reaches its limit in finite time, so engineers use the convention that a circuit is "practically" done after five time constants (5τ), by which point charging is over 99% complete and discharging has under 1% of V₀ left — close enough for any real measurement to call it finished.
Worked Example — Discharging Curve A capacitor charged to V₀ = 9 V discharges through R = 3000 Ω and C = 1000 μF = 1×10⁻³ F. Time constant: τ = RC = (3000)(1×10⁻³) = 3 s. At t = τ = 3 s: V = 9e⁻¹ ≈ 9(0.368) ≈ 3.31 V. At t = 2τ = 6 s: V = 9e⁻² ≈ 9(0.135) ≈ 1.22 V — already down to about 14% of the starting voltage after just two time constants.

1. A capacitor charged to V₀ discharges through a resistor R with no battery in the loop. Its voltage as a function of time is:

Solving dQ/dt = −Q/(RC) gives exponential decay, Q(t) = Q₀e^(−t/RC), and dividing by C gives V(t) = V₀e^(−t/RC) — the mirror image of the charging curve.

2. Fill in the blank: a capacitor charged to V₀ = 15 V discharges through a circuit with τ = 2 s. At t = 2 s (= τ), V ≈ ___ V (2 decimal places).

V(τ) = V₀e⁻¹ = 15(0.3679) ≈ 5.52 V.

3. At t = τ, a charging capacitor has reached 63.2% of V_max. At that same t = τ, an otherwise identical discharging capacitor has fallen to what percentage of its starting voltage V₀?

Charging's fraction is (1 − e^(−t/τ)) and discharging's fraction is e^(−t/τ) — complements of each other built from the same exponential, so at t = τ they're 63.2% and 36.8% respectively, summing to 100%.

4. A discharging RC circuit has its resistance R doubled, with C left unchanged. The time it takes to discharge to any given fraction of V₀ will:

V(t) = V₀e^(−t/RC) depends on t only through the ratio t/RC, so doubling R doubles RC, which doubles the time needed to reach any particular fraction of V₀ — the same scaling applies to charging's time constant too.
