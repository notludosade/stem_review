Special Factoring Patterns

Two shapes come up so often they're worth recognizing on sight, skipping the number-pair search entirely.

← Lesson 11 Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Both patterns below are really just Lesson 11's method with the search already done for you — but recognizing the shape on sight is much faster than running the search every time.

Difference of squares

Pattern a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). This applies whenever you see one perfect square minus another perfect square, with nothing else in between.

Try any value of a and b — the pattern holds for every value, not just the one shown.

Worked examples x² − 16 = x² − 4² = (x + 4)(x − 4) · 9x² − 25 = (3x)² − 5² = (3x + 5)(3x − 5) · 49 − y² = (7 + y)(7 − y)
Why there's no "middle term" version FOILing (a + b)(a − b) gives a² − ab + ab − b² — the two middle terms are exact opposites and cancel completely, leaving only a² − b². That cancellation is exactly why a sum of squares like a² + b² does not factor this way (or at all, over the real numbers): there's no sign flip anywhere to make a middle term disappear.

Perfect square trinomials

Pattern a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)² and a² − 2ab + b² = (a − b)². Recognize it by checking: are the first and last terms perfect squares, and is the middle term exactly twice the product of their roots?

Try any value of a and b — the pattern holds for every value, not just the one shown.

Try It — Verify a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)²

3

Worked examples x² + 6x + 9: is 6 twice √9 (= 3)? 2 × 3 = 6 ✓, so x² + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)². · x² − 10x + 25: 2 × 5 = 10 ✓, so it's (x − 5)². · 4x² + 20x + 25 = (2x)² + 2(2x)(5) + 5² = (2x + 5)².
Why checking "is it 2ab" matters x² + 5x + 9 has the same first and last perfect squares as x² + 6x + 9, but 5 ≠ 2×3 — so it is not a perfect square trinomial, and Lesson 11's general search (find two numbers multiplying to 9, adding to 5) is what applies instead. Not every trinomial with square first and last terms fits this pattern.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Factor: x² − 49

x² − 49 = x² − 7², a difference of squares: (x + 7)(x − 7).

2. Factor: x² + 12x + 36

36 = 6², and 12 = 2 × 6, so this is a perfect square trinomial: (x + 6)².

3. Why does x² + 25 NOT factor as a difference of squares?

The difference-of-squares pattern only works for a subtraction between two squares; a sum of two squares doesn't factor this way at all over the real numbers.

4. Fill in the blank: x² − 64 factors as ___.

64 = 8², so x² − 64 = (x + 8)(x − 8).

5. Fill in the blank: x² − 18x + 81 factors as ___.

81 = 9², and 18 = 2 × 9, so this is a perfect square trinomial: (x − 9)².
