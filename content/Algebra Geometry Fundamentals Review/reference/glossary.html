Unit 1 — Linear Equations & Inequalities

Linear equation

An equation where the variable appears only to the first power, graphing as a straight line and solved by applying the addition/subtraction and multiplication/division properties of equality.

No solution vs. all real numbers

Solving can reveal an equation is never true (the variable cancels, leaving a false statement — no solution) or always true (the variable cancels, leaving a true statement — true for all real numbers).

Sign-flip rule for inequalities

Multiplying or dividing both sides of an inequality by a negative number reverses the inequality's direction; adding, subtracting, or multiplying/dividing by a positive number does not.

Compound inequality

An inequality like −3 < x ≤ 5 combining two conditions that must both hold at once, graphed as a segment with an open or closed circle chosen independently at each end.

System of equations

A set of equations considered together, whose solution must satisfy every equation at once — for two linear equations, the single point where their graphed lines intersect.

Substitution vs. elimination

Substitution solves one equation for a variable and plugs that expression into the other; elimination adds or subtracts (scaled) equations so one variable's terms cancel — both always produce the same solution.

Unit 2 — Quadratic Equations

Quadratic equation / standard form

An equation ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0; if a were 0 the x² term would vanish and the equation would just be linear.

Parabola / vertex

The curved graph of a quadratic equation; its vertex is the parabola's highest or lowest point, depending on whether it opens downward or upward.

Zero-product property

If two factors multiply to zero, at least one of them must be zero — the rule that turns a factored quadratic like (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 into two simple linear equations.

Repeated root

A quadratic's single solution when it factors as the same linear factor twice, such as (x − 3)(x − 3) = 0, rather than two distinct factors.

Quadratic formula

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / (2a) — solves any quadratic ax² + bx + c = 0 directly from its coefficients, whether or not it factors over the integers.

Discriminant

The expression b² − 4ac under the quadratic formula's square root; positive means two real solutions, zero means one repeated solution, negative means no real solutions.

Unit 3 — Exponents & Polynomials

Laws of exponents

xᵐ·xⁿ = xᵐ⁺ⁿ (product), xᵐ/xⁿ = xᵐ⁻ⁿ (quotient), (xᵐ)ⁿ = xᵐⁿ (power of a power), x⁰ = 1, and x⁻ⁿ = 1/xⁿ — all following directly from what an exponent counts.

Polynomial / monomial

A polynomial is a sum of terms, each a coefficient times a variable raised to a whole-number power; a monomial is a single such term, like 3x.

Like terms

Terms with the identical variable part (same variable, same exponent) — only like terms can be combined by adding or subtracting their coefficients.

FOIL

A memory aid for multiplying two binomials — First, Outer, Inner, Last — naming the four term-by-term products needed; a special case of "multiply everything by everything."

Dividing a polynomial by a monomial

Splitting into one fraction per term, (A + B)/C = A/C + B/C, then simplifying each piece with the laws of exponents — every term must be divided, not just the first.

Unit 4 — Factoring

Greatest common factor (GCF)

The largest expression dividing every term of a polynomial evenly; factoring it out (ab + ac = a(b + c)) is the distributive property run in reverse, and the first check on any factoring problem.

Factoring trinomials (number-pair search)

To factor x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b; the factored form is (x + p)(x + q) — reversing the FOIL multiplication that produced the trinomial.

Difference of squares

a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b); applies only to a subtraction between two perfect squares, since the middle terms only cancel in that case, not for a sum of squares.

Perfect square trinomial

a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)² (or a² − 2ab + b² = (a − b)²); recognized by checking that the first and last terms are perfect squares and the middle term equals exactly 2ab.

Unit 5 — Geometry Fundamentals

Complementary / supplementary angles

Complementary angles sum to 90°; supplementary angles sum to 180° — neither requires the angles to be adjacent, only that their measures add to the right total.

Vertical angles

The pair of opposite angles formed when two lines cross; always equal.

Transversal / corresponding / alternate interior angles

A transversal crossing two parallel lines creates corresponding angles (same position at each intersection, equal) and alternate interior angles (opposite sides of the transversal, between the parallel lines, equal).

Congruent vs. similar

Congruent figures match in both shape and size; similar figures match in shape only, with corresponding sides scaled by a shared scale factor — congruence is similarity with scale factor 1.

Scale factor and dimension scaling

The single ratio applied to every corresponding side length between similar figures; side lengths scale by k, area scales by k², and volume scales by k³.

Perimeter, area, volume

Perimeter measures the 1D distance around a 2D shape's boundary; area measures the 2D space it covers; volume measures the 3D space a solid fills — each in the matching unit (linear, square, cubic).

Unit 6 — The Pythagorean Theorem & Right Triangles

Pythagorean Theorem

For a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c: a² + b² = c² — an area relationship between squares built on each side, true only when the 90° angle is present.

Converse of the Pythagorean Theorem

Given three side lengths a, b, c (c the longest), if a² + b² = c², the triangle is a right triangle — letting three numbers alone prove or disprove a right angle.

Acute vs. obtuse (from a² + b² vs. c²)

If a² + b² > c², the angle opposite c is less than 90° (acute); if a² + b² < c², that angle exceeds 90° (obtuse) — the right-triangle case sits exactly on the boundary.

Pythagorean triple

Three positive whole numbers (a, b, c) satisfying a² + b² = c², such as (3, 4, 5); scaling every term by the same positive number always produces another valid triple.

Distance formula

d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²] — the Pythagorean Theorem applied to the horizontal and vertical legs between two coordinate points, with d as the hypotenuse.
