Analog-to-Digital Conversion: Resolution and Quantization

A deeper look at the ADC used casually since Unit 1 — what "resolution" actually means, and what it costs you.

← Unit 3, Lesson 17 Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 1 and Unit 3 both used the ADC without asking exactly how it turns a smooth voltage into a number. This lesson answers that directly — and gives a name to the small, unavoidable error every ADC introduces.

Definition An ADC's resolution is the number of bits it uses to represent a voltage reading. An n-bit ADC divides its input voltage range into 2n discrete levels. A common 10-bit ADC has 210 = 1024 levels (readings 0 through 1023) — exactly the range used throughout Unit 1's examples.
step size = reference voltage / 2n
Example A 10-bit ADC with a 5 V reference has a step size of 5 / 1024 ≈ 4.88 mV. Any two input voltages closer together than that can't be told apart — they both round to the same raw count. A 12-bit ADC with the same 5 V reference has a much finer step size of 5 / 4096 ≈ 1.22 mV.

Quantization: the rounding you can’t avoid

Definition Quantization is the rounding that happens every time a continuous analog voltage gets forced into one of the ADC's finite discrete levels. The resulting small difference between the true voltage and the reported level is quantization error — at most half a step size, but never fully eliminated at any finite resolution.
Why resolution isn’t "always maximize it" Higher resolution isn't free — more-bits ADCs are typically slower to convert each sample and more expensive, and beyond a certain point, the finer resolution is wasted resolving noise the sensor itself can't actually distinguish. Choosing resolution means matching it to what the sensor and the application genuinely need, not simply picking the largest number available.

1. How many discrete levels does a 12-bit ADC have?

An n-bit ADC has 2^n levels; 2^12 = 4096.

2. What is quantization error?

Because the ADC can only report one of a finite set of levels, some rounding is unavoidable — that rounding gap is quantization error.

3. Why isn’t choosing the highest available ADC resolution always the right call?

Resolution should match real requirements — beyond a certain point, extra bits mostly resolve sensor noise rather than meaningful signal, while adding cost and conversion time.

4. Fill in the blank: the number of bits an ADC uses to represent a voltage reading is called its ___.

Resolution determines how many discrete levels — 2^n for n bits — the ADC can report.

5. Fill in the blank: the rounding that occurs whenever a continuous voltage is forced into one of an ADC’s finite discrete levels is called ___.

Quantization is the unavoidable rounding step at the heart of every analog-to-digital conversion.
