Digital-to-Analog Conversion (DAC) and PWM as a Practical Substitute

Most cheap microcontrollers can’t output a true analog voltage — here’s the clever workaround nearly every one uses instead.

← Lesson 18 Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 18 covered turning an analog voltage into a digital number. The reverse operation — digital-to-analog conversion — turns a digital number back into an analog voltage.

Definition A DAC (digital-to-analog converter) is hardware that converts a digital number directly into a corresponding continuous analog voltage. Unlike ADC pins, which are common on nearly every microcontroller, a true DAC output is much less common — many popular boards (a standard Arduino Uno, for instance) have no dedicated DAC pin at all.

The workaround: PWM instead of a true DAC

Rather than requiring dedicated DAC hardware, most microcontrollers fake an analog-ish output using PWM — rapidly switching a digital pin fully HIGH and fully LOW, so quickly that whatever is connected to it responds to the average voltage over time rather than the instantaneous on/off switching.

Why this actually works A DC motor's shaft has real physical inertia — it can't meaningfully react to individual on/off pulses hundreds or thousands of times per second, so it responds to the pulses' time-averaged effect instead, spinning as if it received a steady, lower voltage. The same is true of an LED combined with a human eye's limited flicker perception at high enough switching rates. PWM doesn't literally produce an in-between voltage — it produces a rapidly switching full-voltage signal that behaves like one, to anything too slow to notice the switching itself.
Example A 5 V pin PWM'd at 50% duty cycle (detailed fully in the next lesson) delivers an average voltage of 2.5 V to a motor — close enough to true 2.5 V analog output that the motor spins at roughly the same speed either way, even though the pin itself is never actually sitting at a steady 2.5 V.

For an application that truly needs a smooth, literal analog voltage — not just an "acts like one" average — PWM can be passed through a simple low-pass filter circuit (a resistor and capacitor) that smooths the rapid switching into an actual steady voltage. But for the overwhelming majority of mechatronics uses in this course — motor speed, LED brightness — the raw PWM signal itself, without any extra filtering, is exactly what's used.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why do many popular microcontroller boards lack a true DAC output pin?

ADC input is far more common than DAC output on inexpensive microcontrollers — PWM fills the gap for most practical purposes.

2. Why does a DC motor respond to a PWM signal as if it were receiving a steady, lower analog voltage?

The motor can’t meaningfully respond to each fast on/off transition individually, so it effectively "sees" only the average voltage over many cycles.

3. What circuit would you add to a PWM output if an application genuinely needed a smooth, literal steady analog voltage rather than just an "acts like one" average?

A resistor-capacitor low-pass filter smooths PWM’s rapid switching into an actual steady voltage — needed only when a true analog signal, not just an equivalent average effect, is required.

4. Fill in the blank: hardware that converts a digital number directly into a corresponding continuous analog voltage is called a ___.

DAC — digital-to-analog converter — is the direct-conversion hardware that PWM substitutes for on most cheap microcontrollers.

5. Fill in the blank: rapidly switching a digital pin fully on and off so that a connected device responds to its time-averaged effect is called ___.

PWM (pulse-width modulation) is the workaround technique — the full topic of the next lesson.
