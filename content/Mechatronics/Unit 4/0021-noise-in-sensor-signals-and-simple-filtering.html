Noise in Sensor Signals and Simple Filtering

A perfectly still, unchanging quantity almost never produces a perfectly still reading — here’s why, and the crudest fix.

← Lesson 20 Lesson 22 → Contents Glossary →

Read a sensor twice in a row, with nothing in the real world actually changing, and you'll usually get two slightly different numbers anyway. That's noise — and it's present in nearly every real measurement this course has covered.

Definition Noise is random, unwanted variation in a sensor's reading that doesn't reflect an actual change in the physical quantity being measured. It comes from several sources at once: electrical interference from nearby wiring, the quantization error from Lesson 18, mechanical vibration, and a sensor's own internal jitter (an ultrasonic sensor's echo-edge detection, for instance, is rarely identical between two otherwise-identical pings).
Example A thermistor sitting in a perfectly stable-temperature room might report 3.01 V, then 2.98 V, then 3.02 V on three back-to-back readings — the true temperature never changed, but the reported voltage bounces around slightly every time.
Why noise matters more here than it might seem A single noisy reading feeding directly into a decision can cause a system to react to noise as if it were a real change — flickering a threshold-based output on and off, or (as Unit 6 will show) badly confusing a control loop's derivative term, which is specifically sensitive to how fast a reading is changing.

The crudest fix: averaging multiple samples

The simplest defense against noise is also the cheapest to implement: instead of trusting one reading, take several readings in quick succession and average them together before using the result.

Example Three readings of 3.01 V, 2.98 V, and 3.02 V average to (3.01 + 2.98 + 3.02) / 3 ≈ 3.003 V — closer to the true, stable value than any single one of the three individual readings, because random noise on each individual sample tends to partly cancel out when combined.

This works, but it has an obvious cost: taking N samples before producing a reading takes N times as long as taking one — a real delay that has to be weighed against how much smoothing is actually needed. The next lesson develops a more efficient, ongoing version of this same averaging idea, one that doesn't require restarting from scratch on every single reading.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is sensor noise?

Noise is variation that doesn’t correspond to a real physical change — it comes from electrical interference, quantization, vibration, and sensor jitter, among other sources.

2. Why can noisy readings be especially disruptive to a control loop’s derivative term (previewed here, covered fully in Unit 6)?

A term sensitive to rate of change is especially vulnerable to noise, since noise itself is exactly rapid, meaningless variation.

3. What is the main cost of using simple multi-sample averaging to reduce noise?

Averaging trades speed for smoothness — more samples means a smoother result but a longer wait before that result is ready.

4. Fill in the blank: random, unwanted variation in a sensor reading that doesn’t reflect a real physical change is called ___.

Noise is the general term for this unwanted variation, arising from several combined sources.

5. Fill in the blank: the simplest, cheapest defense against noisy readings is to take multiple samples and combine them by ___.

Averaging multiple samples lets random noise on individual readings partly cancel out, at the cost of extra time per reading.
