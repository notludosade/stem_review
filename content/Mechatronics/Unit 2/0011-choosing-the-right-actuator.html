Choosing the Right Actuator

Three actuators, three very different jobs — closing out the unit with the decision framework that ties them together.

← Lesson 10 Unit 3, Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Units so far covered three actuators one at a time. Real designs require picking the right one for a given job — and the wrong choice usually isn't subtle. A stepper motor spinning a drone propeller, or a plain DC motor trying to hold a robot arm's joint at an exact angle, would both fail in obvious ways.

The comparison, side by side

DC motor Best for: continuous rotation where exact position doesn't matter — wheels, fans, propellers. Cheapest and simplest to drive. No native position awareness at all; needs an external sensor (like an encoder) bolted on if position ever does matter.
Servo motor Best for: holding a specific angle, usually within a limited range (often 0°–180°), with active resistance to being pushed off that angle. Built-in feedback loop, simple one-wire control signal, moderate cost. Not built for continuous full-speed rotation or heavy sustained load.
Stepper motor Best for: precise, repeatable positioning over many steps, especially multi-turn motion a servo's limited range can't cover. No feedback sensor needed under normal operation, but silently vulnerable to missed steps under stall. More complex driver hardware and software than a DC motor.

Working through real scenarios

Scenario 1: a wheeled rover’s drive wheels The wheels just need to spin, at variable speed, for extended periods, with no requirement to stop at an exact angle. A DC motor (with a driver from Lesson 8) is the natural, cheapest fit — a servo's limited rotation range rules it out entirely, and a stepper's precision is unnecessary overhead here.
Scenario 2: a robot arm’s elbow joint The joint needs to move to and firmly hold a specific angle, resisting gravity and the load in the gripper, within a limited range of motion. A servo is the natural fit — its built-in feedback loop actively holds position without any extra sensor or control logic on your end.
Scenario 3: a 3D printer’s X-axis carriage The carriage must move to precise, repeatable positions across many small moves, often far more than 180° of equivalent rotation once geared to a belt. A stepper motor is the standard choice — precise, repeatable, multi-turn motion, with no feedback sensor required as long as the motion stays within the motor's torque limits.
The underlying question to always ask Does this job need continuous rotation, a held angle within a limited range, or precise multi-step positioning? That single question, asked before any other design decision, eliminates two of the three actuator types in almost every real scenario.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A design needs a wheel to spin continuously at variable speed, with no requirement to stop at any particular angle. Which actuator fits best?

Continuous rotation with no position requirement is exactly a plain DC motor’s strength, and the cheapest, simplest option for the job.

2. A design needs a robot gripper joint to move to and firmly hold a specific angle within a 0°–180° range, resisting being pushed. Which actuator fits best?

A servo’s built-in feedback loop is designed exactly for holding a commanded angle against a resisting load, within its limited range of motion.

3. Why would a stepper motor be a poor choice for spinning a drone’s propeller?

A propeller has no meaningful "position" to hold — it just needs to spin fast and continuously, which plays to a DC (or brushless) motor’s strength, not a stepper’s step-counted precision.

4. Fill in the blank: before choosing an actuator, the first question to ask is whether the job needs continuous rotation, a held angle within a limited range, or precise multi-step ___.

That single question — continuous spin, held angle, or multi-step positioning — narrows the choice to one actuator type in almost every real scenario.

5. Fill in the blank: of the three actuators covered in this unit, the one that requires no external position sensor but is silently vulnerable to missed steps under stall is the ___ motor.

A stepper tracks position purely by counting commanded steps, which fails silently the moment a stall causes a missed step.
