Stepper Motors and Precise Rotation

A motor that moves in fixed, countable increments — precise position tracking without a position sensor.

← Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

A stepper motor takes a different approach to precision than a servo does. Instead of measuring its own position, it moves in fixed, known increments called steps, and the controller simply counts how many steps it has commanded to know where the shaft should be.

Definition A stepper motor rotates in discrete, fixed-angle increments by energizing its internal coils in a specific sequence, one step at a time. A common step size is 1.8° per step, meaning 200 steps make one full revolution.
Why no feedback sensor is needed — with a catch Because each step moves the shaft a known, fixed angle, a controller can track position purely by counting commanded steps — no potentiometer or encoder required, unlike a servo. The catch: this only works if every commanded step actually happens. If the motor is pushed past its available torque (a stall), it can miss steps silently, and the controller's step count then no longer matches the real shaft position, with no way to detect the mismatch on its own.

Unipolar vs. bipolar, and driving one

Stepper motors come in two winding styles — unipolar (simpler to drive, slightly less torque-efficient) and bipolar (needs an H-bridge-style driver per winding, more torque-efficient) — driven by dedicated stepper driver ICs (like the ULN2003 for small unipolar steppers, or the A4988/DRV8825 for bipolar ones) that handle the coil-energizing sequence so the microcontroller just sends step and direction pulses.

Microstepping, briefly Many modern stepper drivers support microstepping — energizing coils at intermediate current levels to divide each full step into smaller sub-steps, trading a bit of torque for smoother motion and finer resolution than the motor's native step angle alone would allow.

Stepper vs. servo vs. plain DC motor

Comparison A stepper gives precise, repeatable positioning over many steps without needing a feedback sensor, but has less holding torque at high speed and no built-in way to detect a missed step. A servo gives a similar precise-position result over a narrower angle range (often 0°–180°), with active feedback that resists being pushed off its commanded angle. A plain DC motor gives neither — it's the right choice only when continuous spinning, not a specific position, is the actual goal.

This is why a 3D printer's axes and a CNC machine's cutting head use stepper motors — thousands of precise, countable moves building up an exact final position — while a robot arm's joint more often uses a servo, and a rover's drive wheel almost always uses a plain DC motor.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A stepper motor with a 1.8° step angle is commanded to take 50 steps. How far does the shaft rotate?

50 steps × 1.8° per step = 90° of rotation.

2. Why is a stepper motor able to track its position without a feedback sensor, unlike a servo?

Position tracking relies entirely on the assumption that every commanded step actually occurs — a stall breaks that assumption silently.

3. What happens if a stepper motor stalls (is pushed past its available torque) mid-operation?

A stall can cause missed steps invisibly — the controller keeps counting as if every step succeeded, so its internal position estimate silently drifts from reality.

4. Fill in the blank: dividing each of a stepper motor’s full steps into smaller sub-steps by energizing coils at intermediate current levels is called ___.

Microstepping trades some torque for smoother motion and finer resolution than the motor’s native step angle provides alone.
