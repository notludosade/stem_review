DC Motors: How They Work

Sensors gather information — actuators are how a mechatronic system pushes back on the physical world, starting with the simplest motor.

← Unit 1, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 1 was entirely about gathering information. This unit flips the direction: an actuator is any component that converts an electrical signal into physical motion or force — the way a mechatronic system actually changes something about the world. The most common actuator by far is the DC motor.

Definition A DC motor converts direct-current electrical energy into continuous rotational motion. Current flows through a coil of wire (the rotor) sitting inside a magnetic field; that current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a force, which produces torque and spins the shaft.

A plain two-terminal DC motor has no sense of "position" — apply voltage, and it spins for as long as voltage is applied, in whichever direction the polarity dictates. Reverse the two wires, and it spins the other way. This simplicity is exactly what makes DC motors the default choice for anything that just needs to spin continuously, like a wheel or a fan.

The specs that actually matter

Key specifications Rated voltage is the voltage the motor is designed to run at. No-load speed (in RPM) is how fast it spins with nothing attached to resist it. Stall torque is the maximum torque it can produce right before it stops turning entirely under too much load. Real operation always sits somewhere between those two extremes: more load means less speed, right up until stall torque is reached and the motor stops turning (while still drawing significant current — an important, often-overlooked failure case).
Why speed control isn’t just "less voltage" A DC motor's speed is roughly proportional to the voltage across it, so slowing it down means reducing that effective voltage. Simply wiring a variable resistor in series wastes a lot of power as heat and doesn't scale well for anything beyond small motors. The real answer — switching the motor on and off very rapidly instead of holding a lower steady voltage — is Unit 4's PWM lesson, and it's foreshadowed constantly from here on.

Brushed vs. brushless, briefly

Everything above describes a brushed DC motor — physical contacts ("brushes") mechanically switch current direction inside the motor as it spins, which is what actually keeps it turning continuously rather than settling into one position. Brushless motors achieve the same current-switching electronically instead of mechanically, avoiding brush wear and running more efficiently, at the cost of needing a dedicated controller circuit to do the switching. Brushless motors show up again in Unit 8, where they're the standard choice for drones — for now, brushed motors are the simpler starting point, and everything in this unit applies to them directly.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What physically causes a DC motor’s shaft to spin?

A coil carrying current inside a magnetic field experiences a force; that force produces torque, which spins the rotor.

2. What happens to a DC motor as its mechanical load increases, up to the point of stalling?

Real motors trade speed for torque as load increases; at stall the motor has stopped turning but is still drawing current, which is why a stalled motor can overheat.

3. Why isn’t simply adding a variable resistor in series a good way to control a DC motor’s speed?

A series resistor burns off the "unwanted" power as heat rather than actually reducing what the motor consumes — PWM, covered in Unit 4, avoids this waste.

4. Fill in the blank: the maximum torque a DC motor produces right before it stops turning under too much load is called ___ torque.

Stall torque is the torque at zero speed — the motor is still trying to turn and still drawing current, just fully loaded down.

5. Fill in the blank: reversing the two wires on a simple DC motor reverses its direction of ___.

Swapping polarity reverses current direction through the coil, which reverses the force direction and therefore the direction of rotation.
