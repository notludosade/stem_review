Servo Motors and Position Control

A DC motor, a gearbox, and a built-in feedback loop, all packaged together — this is your first real closed-loop system.

← Lesson 8 Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

A plain DC motor only knows how to spin. A servo motor knows how to hold a specific angle — and it manages that using ideas this course won't formally name until Unit 5, packaged into one small, self-contained unit.

Definition A servo motor (in the hobbyist sense used throughout this course) is a small DC motor combined with a reduction gearbox, a position sensor (usually a potentiometer attached to the output shaft), and a built-in control circuit — all working together to drive the shaft to, and hold it at, a commanded angle.
Why this is worth pausing on A servo is a complete closed-loop control system in miniature: it measures its own actual position (via the internal potentiometer), compares that to the commanded position, and drives its motor to close the gap — continuously, on its own, without any further instruction from your microcontroller beyond "go to this angle." Unit 5 gives this exact pattern a name: feedback. You're seeing a working example of it here, three units early.

How the microcontroller tells it where to go

A hobby servo is commanded with a PWM signal repeated every 20 milliseconds, where the width of the HIGH pulse within that period — not its repetition rate — encodes the target angle. A pulse of about 1.0 ms commands roughly 0°, 1.5 ms commands the center (roughly 90°), and 2.0 ms commands roughly 180°, with everything in between mapped proportionally.

Example A robot arm's gripper needs to open to a specific, repeatable angle every time, then hold it firmly while carrying a light load. A servo is a natural fit — send it one command, and it drives to that angle and actively resists being pushed off it, all using its own internal feedback loop.

A variant worth knowing: continuous rotation servos

A modified version of the same hardware, called a continuous rotation servo, removes the internal angle limit and reinterprets the same pulse-width signal as a speed and direction command instead of a position command — 1.5 ms means stop, shorter pulses spin one way, longer pulses spin the other. It looks identical from the outside, uses the identical control signal, but behaves like a simple speed-controlled motor rather than a position-holding one — worth knowing so you don't assume every servo-shaped part holds an angle.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What internal component allows a servo motor to know its own current shaft angle?

The internal potentiometer turns with the output shaft, giving the servo’s control circuit a continuous reading of its actual current angle.

2. A servo receives a repeating pulse close to 1.5 ms wide. Roughly what angle is being commanded?

A roughly 1.5 ms pulse width commands the servo’s center position, close to 90°, with 1.0 ms and 2.0 ms mapping to the two extremes.

3. Why is a standard servo motor described as a "closed-loop system in miniature"?

The servo measures its own position via its internal potentiometer and self-corrects toward the commanded angle — exactly the feedback pattern Unit 5 formally defines.

4. Fill in the blank: unlike a standard servo, a continuous rotation servo interprets its pulse-width signal as a ___ and direction command instead of a position command.

Removing the internal angle limit turns the same hardware and signal into a speed controller rather than a position controller.

5. Fill in the blank: a standard hobby servo’s control pulse repeats roughly every ___ milliseconds.

The 20 ms repetition period is standard across most hobby servos, even though the pulse width within that period varies with the commanded angle.
