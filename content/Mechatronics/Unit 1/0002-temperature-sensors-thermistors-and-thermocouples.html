Temperature Sensors: Thermistors and Thermocouples

Two very different ways to turn heat into a voltage — one cheap and simple, one built for extremes.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Temperature is one of the most common quantities a mechatronic system measures — ovens, 3D printer hot ends, engine bays, greenhouses. Two sensor types cover the overwhelming majority of real designs: the thermistor for everyday ranges, and the thermocouple for extreme heat.

Thermistors: resistance that changes with temperature

Definition A thermistor is a resistor whose resistance changes predictably with temperature. The common type used in mechatronics is an NTC thermistor (negative temperature coefficient) — its resistance decreases as temperature rises.

A thermistor by itself just changes resistance — it doesn't produce a voltage a microcontroller can read directly. To turn that resistance change into a readable voltage, you pair it with a fixed resistor in a voltage divider: two resistors in series between the supply voltage and ground, with the microcontroller's ADC pin reading the voltage at the midpoint.

Why this circuit shows up everywhere This exact divider pattern — fixed resistor plus a variable resistive sensor — is how nearly every simple resistive sensor gets read, not just thermistors. You’ll see the identical circuit again for photoresistors in the next lesson, just with a different sensor in the bottom slot.

A thermistor's resistance-vs-temperature curve isn't a straight line, so getting an exact temperature back out requires a calibration step (covered in Lesson 6) — but over a modest working range, engineers often treat it as close enough to linear for a quick estimate.

Thermocouples: voltage from a junction of two metals

Definition A thermocouple is a junction of two dissimilar metal wires that generates a small voltage proportional to the temperature difference between that junction and a reference point — an effect called the Seebeck effect. No external power supply is needed to generate the signal itself.
Example A K-type thermocouple (nickel-chromium paired with nickel-aluminum) can measure from about −200°C up to roughly 1260°C — a range that would destroy most thermistors, which typically top out well under 300°C. This is why thermocouples show up in kilns, engine exhaust, and industrial furnaces, while thermistors handle everyday electronics and appliances.

The trade-off: a thermocouple's raw output is tiny (microvolts to low millivolts per degree), so it needs an amplifier circuit before a microcontroller can read it usefully — more supporting hardware than a thermistor's simple voltage divider. Thermistors also tend to respond faster and cost less, which is why they dominate in consumer and hobbyist mechatronics where extreme heat isn't a factor.

Choosing between them Building a 3D-printer hot-end sensor, a room-temperature monitor, or a simple thermostat? A thermistor is almost always the right, cheap choice. Measuring a furnace, an exhaust manifold, or anything glowing? That's thermocouple territory.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What happens to an NTC thermistor’s resistance as temperature rises?

NTC stands for negative temperature coefficient — resistance moves opposite to temperature, falling as it gets hotter.

2. Why can’t a microcontroller read a thermistor’s resistance directly?

ADC pins measure voltage. A voltage divider is what converts the thermistor’s changing resistance into a changing voltage the ADC can actually sample.

3. Why would an engineer choose a thermocouple over a thermistor for measuring a kiln’s temperature?

A kiln’s extreme heat would damage most thermistors; thermocouples are built for exactly this range, though they do need amplification circuitry the thermistor’s simple divider doesn’t.

4. Fill in the blank: pairing a thermistor with a fixed resistor between supply voltage and ground, and reading the voltage at their midpoint, is called a voltage ___.

A voltage divider is exactly this two-resistor arrangement, and it’s the standard way to read any simple resistive sensor.

5. Fill in the blank: the physical effect that lets a junction of two dissimilar metals generate a temperature-proportional voltage is called the ___ effect.

The Seebeck effect is the thermoelectric phenomenon a thermocouple relies on — no external power source needed to produce the signal.
