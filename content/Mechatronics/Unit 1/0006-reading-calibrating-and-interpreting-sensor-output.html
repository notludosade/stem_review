Reading, Calibrating, and Interpreting Sensor Output

A raw ADC number is not a temperature. Getting from one to the other is its own skill — and it closes out this unit.

← Lesson 5 Unit 2, Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Every analog sensor lesson so far has ended at the same place: a voltage, read by an ADC, that turns into a raw number. This lesson covers the last, easy-to-skip step — turning that raw number into a real-world measurement you can actually trust.

From raw count to voltage

An ADC doesn't report volts directly — it reports a count, scaled across its resolution. A 10-bit ADC (common on many entry-level microcontrollers) reports a value from 0 to 1023, where 0 corresponds to 0 V and 1023 corresponds to the reference voltage (often the supply voltage, e.g. 5 V).

voltage = (raw ADC count / 1023) × reference voltage
Example A 10-bit ADC with a 5 V reference reads a raw count of 614 from a thermistor divider. Voltage = (614 / 1023) × 5 ≈ 3.00 V. That's the physical voltage at the divider's midpoint — still not a temperature, just the first conversion step.

From voltage to a real-world unit

Calibration is the process of relating a sensor's electrical output to the physical quantity it's meant to measure. The simplest useful approach is a two-point calibration: take readings at two known reference conditions, then use those two points to build a straight-line mapping between everything in between.

Definition A two-point calibration records the sensor's output at two known, trusted reference values (for a thermistor: ice water at 0°C and boiling water at 100°C, for example) and uses linear interpolation between those two points to convert any other reading into an estimated physical value.
Example A thermistor divider reads 3.30 V in an ice bath (0°C) and 2.10 V in boiling water (100°C). That's a slope of (100 − 0) / (2.10 − 3.30) ≈ −83.3°C per volt. A field reading of 2.85 V then estimates to 0 + (2.85 − 3.30) × (−83.3) ≈ 37.5°C.
Why not just trust the datasheet formula? Manufacturing tolerances mean no two thermistors, photoresistors, or voltage dividers behave identically — a generic formula gets you in the right neighborhood, but a quick calibration against known reference points corrects for the specific resistor values and specific sensor unit actually on your breadboard.

One reading is rarely the truth

A single raw ADC sample is also noisy — read the same steady sensor twice in a row and you'll often get two slightly different numbers, from electrical interference, ADC quantization, or the sensor's own jitter. Averaging several samples before trusting a reading is a cheap first defense against this noise, and it's the idea Unit 4 develops into proper filtering techniques. For now, the takeaway is simple: a sensor's raw output is a starting point, not a finished measurement — it needs converting, calibrating, and often averaging before it's something you can build a decision on.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A 10-bit ADC with a 5 V reference reads a raw count of 512. What voltage does that correspond to?

(512 / 1023) × 5 ≈ 2.50 V — roughly the midpoint of the 0–5 V range, matching the midpoint of the 0–1023 count range.

2. What is the purpose of a two-point calibration?

Two-point calibration uses two trusted reference readings to interpolate a conversion from raw output to a physical unit like temperature — it doesn’t address noise, only the mapping itself.

3. Why might a generic manufacturer formula for a thermistor still give a slightly wrong temperature on a specific circuit?

Real components vary from unit to unit within their manufacturing tolerance, so a calibration against known reference points corrects for the specific parts actually in use.

4. Fill in the blank: converting a raw ADC count into a real-world physical unit using known reference readings is called ___.

Calibration is the general process of relating a sensor’s raw output to the physical quantity it’s meant to represent.

5. Fill in the blank: reading a steady, unchanging sensor multiple times and combining the results to reduce random variation is a simple first defense against sensor ___.

Noise causes repeated readings of the same true value to vary slightly — averaging multiple samples is the simplest way to reduce its effect.
