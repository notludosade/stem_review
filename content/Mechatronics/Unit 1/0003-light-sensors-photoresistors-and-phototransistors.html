Light Sensors: Photoresistors and Phototransistors

The same voltage-divider trick from temperature sensing, applied to light — plus a faster alternative when timing matters.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Just as a thermistor's resistance responds to heat, some components' electrical properties respond to light. The two you'll meet most often are the photoresistor, for slow ambient-light sensing, and the phototransistor, for fast, precise light detection.

Photoresistors (LDRs): cheap and simple

Definition A photoresistor (also called a light-dependent resistor, or LDR) is a resistor whose resistance decreases as light hitting it increases — more light means an easier path for current to flow.

An LDR is read exactly the way a thermistor is: dropped into a voltage divider with a fixed resistor, with the midpoint voltage fed to an ADC pin. Swap the thermistor from Lesson 2's circuit for a photoresistor, and you have a light sensor with no other changes needed — same circuit, different sensing element.

Why this matters LDRs are slow (tens to hundreds of milliseconds to respond to a light change) and their resistance-to-light relationship isn’t very precise or consistent between individual units. That's a fine trade for a night-light or a simple "is it dark yet" check, but it rules them out anywhere fast or exact light readings matter.

Phototransistors: faster and more precise

Definition A phototransistor is a transistor whose base current is triggered by light hitting an exposed junction, causing a much larger current to flow between its other two terminals. More light means more current flows.
Example An optical encoder disk (a spinning disk with alternating opaque and clear segments) needs a light sensor that can register thousands of on/off transitions per second as the disk spins past it. An LDR's sluggish response would blur those transitions together; a phototransistor can keep up.

Phototransistors also respond far more linearly and consistently to light intensity than LDRs do, which matters when you need a light reading you can actually trust across multiple units or over time — not just a rough "brighter or dimmer" signal.

Choosing between them Building a simple light-triggered night light, or a basic "is a box present" light-blocking check where speed doesn't matter? An LDR is cheap and good enough. Building an optical encoder, a fast break-beam counter, or anything reading an infrared remote signal? Reach for a phototransistor (or the closely related photodiode).

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. How does a photoresistor’s (LDR’s) resistance respond to increasing light?

More light means lower resistance for a photoresistor — the same inverse relationship an NTC thermistor has with heat.

2. Why is an LDR a poor choice for reading a fast-spinning optical encoder disk?

An LDR’s response time (tens to hundreds of milliseconds) is far too slow to resolve the thousands of transitions per second a spinning encoder disk produces.

3. What circuit does a photoresistor need in order to produce a readable voltage for a microcontroller’s ADC pin?

Like any simple resistive sensor, an LDR’s resistance change must be converted into a voltage change via a voltage divider before an ADC can read it.

4. Fill in the blank: a transistor whose base current is triggered by incoming light, producing a fast and precise light-dependent current, is called a ___.

A phototransistor trades the photoresistor’s simplicity for much faster, more linear response — needed for anything timing-sensitive.

5. Fill in the blank: another common name for a photoresistor is a light-dependent ___, abbreviated LDR.

Light-dependent resistor is the full name behind the LDR abbreviation — describing exactly what changes: its resistance, based on light.
