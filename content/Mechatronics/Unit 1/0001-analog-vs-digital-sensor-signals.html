Analog vs. Digital Sensor Signals

Every sensor speaks one of two languages to a microcontroller — a continuously varying voltage, or a plain on/off pulse.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Every sensor's job is to turn some physical quantity — temperature, light, distance, contact — into an electrical signal a microcontroller can read. That signal always arrives in one of two forms: analog or digital. Which form a sensor uses shapes everything about how you wire it, how you read it, and how much information you actually get back.

Definition An analog sensor outputs a voltage that varies continuously over a range — any value between its minimum and maximum is meaningful. A digital sensor outputs only a small set of discrete states, almost always just two: HIGH (roughly the supply voltage) or LOW (roughly 0 V).
Example A photoresistor's voltage climbs and falls smoothly as a room gets brighter or dimmer — every intermediate brightness gets a slightly different voltage. A limit switch has exactly two states: pressed or not pressed. There's no "half-pressed" reading worth keeping, so it outputs a clean digital HIGH or LOW instead.

This distinction determines what kind of microcontroller pin a sensor needs. An analog sensor must connect to one of the microcontroller's dedicated ADC (analog-to-digital converter) pins, which are typically limited to a handful per chip. A digital sensor can connect to almost any general-purpose pin, of which there are usually many more.

Why it matters Analog pins are a scarcer resource than digital ones on most microcontrollers. Knowing which sensors truly need continuous information (a light level, a temperature) versus which only need a yes/no state (a button, a switch) is the first design decision in any sensor layout — get it wrong and you can run out of analog pins on a board that still has digital pins to spare.

A wrinkle: digital signals can still carry analog information

The analog/digital split describes the voltage levels a sensor uses, not necessarily how much information it carries. Some sensors are digital in voltage but analog in meaning — they encode a continuous quantity into the timing of a digital pulse rather than into the pulse's height. An ultrasonic distance sensor is the clearest example: its output pin only ever reads HIGH or LOW, but the duration of that HIGH pulse is proportional to distance. You'll see exactly this trick in Lesson 4.

So the precise way to think about it: analog vs. digital describes how a sensor's pin behaves electrically. It doesn't, by itself, tell you how much real-world information is packed into that behavior.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the defining feature of an analog sensor’s output?

Analog sensors produce a continuously varying voltage — every intermediate value between the minimum and maximum is meaningful, unlike a digital sensor’s handful of discrete states.

2. Why does it matter, practically, whether a sensor is analog or digital?

A microcontroller has far fewer ADC-capable pins than general-purpose digital pins, so which type a sensor needs directly affects how you plan a pin layout.

3. An ultrasonic distance sensor’s echo pin only ever reads HIGH or LOW. Why is it still described as carrying analog information?

The voltage is strictly digital (HIGH/LOW), but the pulse’s timing encodes a continuous, analog quantity — distance. Electrical behavior and information content are two different things.

4. Fill in the blank: a microcontroller converts a continuously varying sensor voltage into a digital number using its ___ pins.

ADC stands for analog-to-digital converter — the specific pins on a microcontroller capable of reading a continuous voltage.

5. Fill in the blank: a limit switch, which only ever reports pressed or not pressed, is an example of a ___ sensor.

Two discrete states with nothing meaningful in between is exactly the definition of a digital signal.
