Limit Switches and Digital Input Sensing

The simplest sensor there is — and the one wiring detail that trips up almost everyone the first time.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

A limit switch is about as simple as a sensor gets: a mechanical switch that closes or opens when something physically presses against it, marking the end of a range of travel — a drawer fully closed, a robot arm reaching its rotation limit, a 3D-printer carriage hitting home position.

Definition A limit switch reports a single digital state: triggered or not triggered, based on physical contact. It has no in-between reading — unlike the analog sensors from earlier lessons, there's nothing to calibrate or scale, just a HIGH or a LOW.

Normally open vs. normally closed

Limit switches come wired one of two ways. A normally open (NO) switch's circuit is broken until something presses it, at which point the circuit closes. A normally closed (NC) switch is the opposite: circuit closed by default, opened when pressed.

Why NC is often the safer choice With an NC switch, a broken or disconnected wire looks identical to the switch being triggered — both result in an open circuit. That means a wiring fault fails into the "safe" or "stop" state rather than silently pretending nothing happened. This fail-safe property is exactly why NC switches are common on safety-critical limits, like an emergency stop or an over-travel cutoff, where a broken wire should trigger a stop, not hide the problem.

The floating pin problem

A raw switch by itself doesn't fully define a digital input's voltage in both of its states. When a normally-open switch isn't pressed, the input pin isn't connected to anything — it's floating, and a floating digital input can read as a random, noisy mix of HIGH and LOW picked up from nearby electrical interference, not a reliable LOW.

Definition A pull-up resistor connects an input pin to the supply voltage through a large resistor, so the pin reads HIGH by default and only reads LOW when something (like a closed switch) actively connects it to ground. A pull-down resistor does the reverse — LOW by default, HIGH only when actively connected to the supply. Either one guarantees a defined state instead of a floating one.
Example Most microcontrollers include a built-in pull-up resistor that can be enabled in software for any digital input pin, which is why so much example code for buttons and switches reads "pressed" as LOW rather than HIGH — the pin idles HIGH via the internal pull-up, and pressing the switch pulls it down to ground.

A quick note on bounce

Mechanical contacts don't switch cleanly — the metal contacts inside physically bounce for a few milliseconds before settling, which a microcontroller reading thousands of times per second can register as several rapid presses instead of one. This problem, called switch bounce, is usually fixed with a short software delay after the first detected change (ignoring further changes for a few milliseconds) or a small filtering circuit. You'll see this idea again once real input-reading code shows up in Unit 3.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What reading does a limit switch produce?

A limit switch is a purely digital sensor — contact or no contact, with no meaningful in-between reading to calibrate.

2. Why is a normally-closed (NC) switch often preferred for a safety-critical cutoff?

With NC wiring, both "triggered" and "wire broken" look like an open circuit — the system fails toward safety rather than silently failing to notice a broken connection.

3. Why does an unpressed, unconnected (floating) digital input pin give unreliable readings?

Without a pull-up or pull-down resistor forcing a defined default state, a floating pin has no firm connection to either rail and can pick up stray electrical noise.

4. Fill in the blank: a resistor that connects an input pin to the supply voltage so it reads HIGH by default, only reading LOW when actively pulled to ground, is called a ___ resistor.

A pull-up resistor guarantees a defined HIGH default state instead of leaving the pin floating.

5. Fill in the blank: the brief, rapid on/off flickering a mechanical switch produces as its contacts physically settle is called switch ___.

Switch bounce can register as multiple rapid presses unless it’s filtered out with a short delay or a filtering circuit.
