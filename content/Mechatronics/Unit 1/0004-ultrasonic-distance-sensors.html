Ultrasonic Distance Sensors

Measuring distance by timing an echo — and why the exact answer depends on the temperature lesson two lessons back.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Not every sensor measures its target's electrical properties. An ultrasonic distance sensor measures distance by sending out a sound pulse and timing how long it takes to bounce back — the same principle a bat or a submarine's sonar uses.

Definition An ultrasonic distance sensor emits a short burst of high-frequency sound (typically around 40 kHz, well above human hearing) from a transmitter, then measures the time until an echo returns to a receiver. That round-trip time, combined with the speed of sound, gives the distance to whatever reflected the pulse.
distance = (speed of sound × echo time) / 2

The division by 2 matters: the timed pulse travels to the object and back, so the one-way distance is half the round-trip distance the timing actually measures.

How a real module reports the measurement

A common module like the HC-SR04 has two pins relevant here: a trigger pin (a digital output from the microcontroller, held HIGH for about 10 microseconds to fire the ping) and an echo pin (a digital input to the microcontroller, which the sensor holds HIGH for a duration exactly equal to the round-trip travel time).

Callback to Lesson 1 This is precisely the "digital pin, analog meaning" case from Lesson 1. The echo pin only ever reads HIGH or LOW — strictly digital — but the length of time it stays HIGH is a continuous, analog quantity carrying the real measurement. Reading this sensor means timing a pulse, not just checking a voltage level.
Example Speed of sound in air is about 343 m/s at room temperature, which works out to roughly 0.0343 cm per microsecond. If the echo pin stays HIGH for 600 microseconds, the round-trip distance is 600 × 0.0343 ≈ 20.6 cm, so the one-way distance to the object is about 10.3 cm.

Why the temperature lesson matters here

The speed of sound isn't a fixed constant — it changes with air temperature, getting faster as air warms. A calculation using a fixed 343 m/s is only exactly correct at one particular temperature; on a much hotter or colder day, that assumption introduces real measurement error. High-precision applications correct for this by reading a temperature sensor (Lesson 2) alongside the ultrasonic sensor and adjusting the speed-of-sound constant accordingly — an early example of one sensor's reading improving another's accuracy.

Limitations worth knowing

Real-world caveats Ultrasonic sensors struggle with soft materials that absorb sound rather than reflecting it (foam, fabric), with surfaces angled away from the sensor (the echo bounces off to the side instead of back), and with a minimum detectable range (an object too close doesn't give the receiver time to separate the echo from the outgoing pulse). Multiple ultrasonic sensors operating near each other can also cross-talk, each picking up echoes meant for its neighbor.

Check your recall

1. An ultrasonic sensor’s echo pin stays HIGH for 800 microseconds. Using 0.0343 cm/microsecond for the speed of sound, what is the one-way distance to the object?

800 × 0.0343 ≈ 27.4 cm is the round-trip distance; dividing by 2 for the one-way distance gives about 13.7 cm.

2. Why must the round-trip travel time be divided by 2 to find the distance to an object?

The measured time covers the full there-and-back trip, so the one-way distance is exactly half of what a naive speed × time calculation would give.

3. Why does an ultrasonic sensor’s distance reading become less accurate on a much hotter or colder day than usual, if the speed-of-sound value used in the calculation is never updated?

Speed of sound rises with temperature; using a fixed value baked in at one temperature introduces error whenever the real air temperature differs from that assumption.

4. Fill in the blank: on a module like the HC-SR04, the pin the microcontroller briefly pulses HIGH to fire off the sound burst is called the ___ pin.

The trigger pin starts the measurement; the separate echo pin is what reports the result back.

5. Fill in the blank: because the echo pin’s HIGH duration — not its voltage level — carries the actual distance measurement, this is an example of a digital signal carrying ___ information.

The voltage itself is strictly digital (HIGH/LOW), but its timing encodes a continuous, analog quantity — exactly the nuance introduced in Lesson 1.
