Bang-Bang (On/Off) Control

The simplest closed-loop controller that actually works — full power or nothing at all, decided by the sign of the error.

← Lesson 24 Lesson 26 → Contents Glossary →

With feedback and error now defined, the simplest possible way to use them is almost embarrassingly direct: check whether the error is positive or negative, and turn the actuator fully on or fully off accordingly.

Definition Bang-bang control (also called on/off control) drives the actuator to one of exactly two states — fully on or fully off — based purely on whether the measured value is above or below the setpoint. There is no in-between output.
Example A home heater's thermostat: if the measured temperature is below the setpoint, the heater runs at full power; if at or above the setpoint, it shuts off completely. A refrigerator's compressor works the same way — full cooling or fully off, nothing in between.
Pseudocode 
loop():
    temp = readTemperature()
    if temp < SETPOINT:
        heaterOn()
    else:
        heaterOff()
Why this is genuinely useful, not just a toy example Bang-bang control needs no calibrated gains, no tuning, and almost no computation — just a comparison. For any actuator that's naturally an on/off device anyway (a heater relay, a compressor, a solenoid valve), it's often the simplest option that actually gets the job done, and it's exactly what many real household appliances use.

The problem lurking at the setpoint

Picture the measured temperature sitting almost exactly at the setpoint, with a small amount of sensor noise (Unit 4, Lesson 21) causing it to bounce back and forth across that single threshold. Every tiny noise-driven crossing flips the heater fully on, then fully off, then on again — rapidly, right at the boundary. This rapid switching, called chattering, wears out relays and actuators far faster than their rated switching life expects, and the next lesson fixes it directly.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What output states can a bang-bang controller produce?

Bang-bang control is strictly two-state — no in-between output exists, unlike proportional control (Unit 6).

2. What decides whether a bang-bang-controlled heater turns on or off?

Bang-bang control makes its decision purely from the sign of the error — above or below setpoint — with no regard for its size or rate of change.

3. Why does a bang-bang controller tend to switch rapidly on and off ("chatter") when the measured value sits very close to the setpoint?

With only one threshold to cross, even tiny noise-driven fluctuations near it trigger rapid, repeated switching — the chattering problem the next lesson addresses.

4. Fill in the blank: a control strategy that drives an actuator to only fully on or fully off, based on whether the measured value is above or below setpoint, is called ___ control.

Bang-bang (or on/off) control is the simplest closed-loop strategy, using only the sign of the error.

5. Fill in the blank: the rapid, repeated on/off switching a bang-bang controller produces when the reading hovers near the setpoint is called ___.

Chattering is the practical downside of a single sharp threshold — the next lesson introduces a fix.
