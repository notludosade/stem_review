Open-Loop vs. Closed-Loop Control

The single biggest design decision in any control system: does it check its own work?

← Unit 4, Lesson 22 Lesson 24 → Contents Glossary →

Every unit so far has quietly used both kinds of control without naming them. A stepper motor (Unit 2) commanded to move 200 steps just... moves 200 steps, with no way to confirm it actually got there. A servo motor (Unit 2) commanded to an angle actively measures its own position and corrects until it matches. Those are two fundamentally different control strategies, and this lesson finally names them.

Definition Open-loop control issues a command based purely on a predetermined plan, with no sensor checking whether the actual result matches what was intended. Closed-loop control measures the actual result with a sensor and continuously adjusts the command based on that measurement.
Example: open-loop A microwave running for a fixed 90 seconds doesn't measure whether the food is actually hot — it just runs the timer and stops. A stepper motor commanded to move 200 steps just executes those steps, with no check that it didn't stall partway through (Unit 2, Lesson 10).
Example: closed-loop A servo motor (Unit 2, Lesson 9) measures its actual shaft angle with an internal potentiometer and keeps adjusting until it matches the commanded angle. A home thermostat measures the actual room temperature and switches the heater based on that measurement, not a fixed timer.
Why anyone would choose open-loop at all Closed-loop control is generally more robust — it can correct for disturbances a fixed plan can't anticipate. But it costs more: a sensor, wiring, and more complex logic. Open-loop is simpler, cheaper, and perfectly adequate whenever the system's behavior is predictable enough that checking isn't worth the added cost — a stepper motor well within its torque limits genuinely doesn't need position feedback for most jobs.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the defining structural difference between open-loop and closed-loop control?

The presence (or absence) of a feedback path from sensor back to controller is the entire distinction between the two.

2. A microwave runs for a fixed 90-second timer regardless of the food’s actual temperature. What kind of control is this?

A fixed timer executes a plan with no check on the actual outcome — exactly open-loop control, even though it does eventually stop.

3. Why might an engineer deliberately choose open-loop control over closed-loop, even knowing closed-loop is generally more robust?

Closed-loop’s robustness comes at a real cost — when a system behaves predictably enough, that extra cost isn’t worth paying.

4. Fill in the blank: a control strategy that measures its actual output and feeds that measurement back into the controller is called ___-loop control.

Closed-loop control is defined by this feedback path — the controller checks and reacts to the real result, not just a predetermined plan.

5. Fill in the blank: a servo motor’s internal potentiometer, which measures its own shaft angle and enables it to correct toward a commanded position, is an example of a sensor providing ___.

Feedback is the general name for a measurement routed back into a controller — exactly what the servo’s potentiometer provides, and the subject of the next lesson.
