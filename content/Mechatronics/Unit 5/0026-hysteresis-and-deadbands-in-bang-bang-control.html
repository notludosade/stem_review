Hysteresis and Deadbands in Bang-Bang Control

One threshold causes chattering. Two thresholds, spaced apart, fix it — at a real cost.

← Lesson 25 Lesson 27 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 25 ended on chattering: a single on/off threshold, combined with ordinary sensor noise, causes rapid, wasteful switching right at the setpoint. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it — stop using just one threshold.

Definition Hysteresis (in this context, also called a deadband) replaces bang-bang control's single threshold with two: a lower threshold that turns the actuator ON, and a separate, higher threshold that turns it OFF. Once triggered, the actuator stays in that state until the reading crosses the opposite threshold — not just the original one.
Example A thermostat set to 70°F with a 2°F hysteresis band turns the heater ON when temperature drops to 69°F, and doesn't turn it OFF again until temperature climbs all the way to 71°F. A temperature bouncing between 69.5°F and 70.5°F due to noise no longer crosses either threshold at all — no chattering.

The trade-off: fewer switches, looser control

Why a wider band isn’t simply better A wider deadband means the actuator switches far less often, extending relay and switch lifespan and reducing wear — but it also lets the measured value drift further from the setpoint before any correction kicks in, giving looser, less precise overall control. A narrower band gives tighter control at the cost of more frequent switching. Choosing the band width means picking a point on this trade-off, based on how much switching wear the actuator can tolerate versus how precisely the setpoint actually needs holding.

Even with hysteresis, bang-bang control still never settles at one exact steady value — it always oscillates somewhere within the deadband. That's an inherent limit of the strategy itself, not a bug to be tuned away, and it's exactly what motivates the next lesson.

1. What does adding hysteresis to a bang-bang controller change?

Hysteresis uses two separated thresholds instead of one, so ordinary noise near the setpoint no longer causes rapid switching.

2. A thermostat has an ON threshold of 68°F and an OFF threshold of 72°F. If the heater just turned on at 68°F, at what temperature will it turn off?

Hysteresis means the heater, once triggered on, stays on until the opposite (higher) threshold is reached — not just any rise above the ON threshold.

3. What is the trade-off of choosing a wider hysteresis deadband?

Fewer switches versus tighter control is the fundamental trade-off — wider deadbands favor the former at the expense of the latter.
