Limitations of Bang-Bang Control and When You Need More

Closing out Unit 5 by being honest about what on/off control can never do — setting up Unit 6’s solution.

← Lesson 26 Unit 6, Lesson 28 → Contents Glossary →

Bang-bang control, even with hysteresis, is genuinely useful — but it has real, structural limits that no amount of tuning the deadband can fix. This lesson is honest about them, because they're exactly what motivates Unit 6.

It can never hold one exact value

An inherent limitation, not a bug Lesson 26 already showed this: bang-bang control always oscillates somewhere within its deadband — it structurally cannot settle at one precise, unchanging value the way a servo (Unit 2) holds a commanded angle. For applications like a home thermostat, that's a perfectly fine trade-off. For applications needing precise, steady control — holding an exact speed, an exact temperature — it isn't.

It struggles with slow-responding systems: overshoot

Bang-bang control assumes the actuator's effect on the system is close to immediate. Many real systems have real lag — thermal mass that takes time to heat up or cool down, mechanical inertia that takes time to respond. When there's meaningful lag, switching the actuator off exactly at the setpoint isn't enough — the system keeps changing for a while even after the actuator turns off, "coasting" past the target.

Example An oven's heating element switches off right when the oven reaches its target temperature — but the element itself, and the air around it, are still hot and still radiating heat into the oven cavity for a short time afterward. The oven's actual temperature keeps climbing briefly after the switch-off, overshooting the target before it starts to fall back.
Why this points directly at Unit 6 Overshoot happens because bang-bang control reacts only to where the system currently is, never to how fast it's changing — it has no way to "see the overshoot coming" and back off early. A control strategy that also accounts for the rate of change could, in principle, ease off before reaching the setpoint rather than right at it. That's exactly the job of the derivative term in Unit 6's PID controller.

It can’t give a smooth, proportional response

A cruise control that only had "full throttle" and "no throttle" available would lurch uncomfortably instead of gently correcting small speed deviations. Some actuators — a PWM-driven motor, a variable-position valve — are physically capable of smooth, in-between output, and throwing that capability away in favor of strict on/off control wastes what the actuator could actually do. This gap — a continuously variable actuator deserves a continuously variable command, not just on/off — is precisely where Unit 6 begins.

1. Why can bang-bang control never hold a controlled variable at one exact, unchanging value, even with hysteresis added?

Oscillation within the deadband is structural to bang-bang control itself — no amount of retuning the deadband removes it entirely.

2. Why does an oven’s temperature often keep rising for a short time after its heating element switches off?

Real systems with lag keep responding for a while after the actuator changes state, causing overshoot past the setpoint before settling back.

3. Why is bang-bang control a poor fit for a cruise control system, even though a car’s throttle is physically capable of smooth, in-between positions?

When an actuator can genuinely produce a smooth, continuously variable output, restricting it to strict on/off throws away real capability — motivating a proportional strategy instead.

4. Fill in the blank: a system’s tendency to keep responding for a while after an actuator changes state, due to thermal mass or mechanical inertia, is called ___.

Lag is what causes overshoot in bang-bang-controlled systems that don’t respond instantaneously to actuator changes.

5. Fill in the blank: a controller reacting only to the current error, with no way to anticipate an approaching overshoot from how fast the error is changing, is exactly the gap Unit 6’s ___ term is designed to fill.

The derivative term reacts to the rate of change of error, giving a controller exactly the "see it coming" capability bang-bang control lacks.
