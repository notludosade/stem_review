Writing Digital Output: Driving LEDs and Simple Loads

A GPIO pin can safely light an LED on its own — but almost everything heavier needs help.

← Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 14 introduced digital output as setting a pin HIGH or LOW. This lesson looks at what that output pin can actually drive safely, and what it can't.

The simple case: an LED

Setting a GPIO pin HIGH can directly light an LED — as long as a current-limiting resistor is placed in series with it. An LED is essentially a one-way, low-resistance path once it turns on; without a resistor to limit current, Ohm's law (V = IR, from Engineering 1) says the current would spike far past what either the LED or the GPIO pin can safely handle.

Example A 5 V output pin driving a red LED (roughly a 2 V forward drop) through a 220 Ω resistor: the resistor sees (5 − 2) = 3 V across it, giving a current of 3 / 220 ≈ 13.6 mA — comfortably within both the LED's and the GPIO pin's safe limits.

Everything heavier needs a driver

Why not just wire a motor or relay the same way A GPIO pin's current limit (tens of milliamps) is nowhere near enough for a motor, a solenoid, or most relays — exactly the same reasoning that motivated the H-bridge in Unit 2, Lesson 8. The general rule: a GPIO pin is only ever a control signal for anything beyond an LED-scale load, never the actual power source. A transistor, a relay, or a driver IC sits between the pin and the real load, letting a small control current switch a much larger one.
Example Turning a 12 V solenoid on and off from a 5 V microcontroller: the GPIO pin drives the base of a small transistor (or the control input of a relay), which then switches the solenoid's much larger current using its own separate 12 V power source — the same "small signal controls large power" pattern as the H-bridge, just for an on/off load instead of a reversible motor.

A quick preview: output isn’t only ever fully on or off

Every example above treats a digital output as strictly HIGH or LOW — fully on, fully off. Many GPIO pins can also be driven with PWM, switching rapidly between HIGH and LOW to produce an effective in-between brightness or speed. That's a deliberately deep topic of its own, covered fully in Unit 4 — this lesson just flags that "digital output" doesn't mean "only two possible effects on the real world."

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does an LED need a current-limiting resistor when driven from a GPIO pin?

An LED’s low forward resistance would otherwise let current climb dangerously high, following Ohm’s law — the resistor limits it to a safe value.

2. Why can’t a GPIO pin directly power a 12 V solenoid the way it can directly light an LED?

Just like the motor case from Unit 2, a solenoid needs more current (and often more voltage) than a GPIO pin provides — a driver component switches its own separate power source instead.

3. What general rule applies to driving any load heavier than a single LED from a GPIO pin?

The pattern repeats throughout this course: GPIO (or PWM) provides a small control signal, and dedicated hardware handles the real current for anything beyond an LED.

4. Fill in the blank: a resistor placed in series with an LED to keep its current within a safe range is called a ___ resistor.

A current-limiting resistor is exactly this safety component, sized using Ohm’s law to keep current within safe bounds.

5. Fill in the blank: rapidly switching a digital output pin between HIGH and LOW to produce an effective in-between brightness or speed is called ___, covered fully in Unit 4.

PWM (pulse-width modulation) is the technique that turns a strictly on/off digital pin into something that can approximate an in-between analog effect.
