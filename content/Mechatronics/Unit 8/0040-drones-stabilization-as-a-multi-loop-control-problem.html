Drones: Stabilization as a Multi-Loop Control Problem

A quadcopter is inherently unstable the instant it leaves the ground — staying level is a full-time, high-speed control job.

← Lesson 39 Lesson 41 → Contents Glossary →

Left alone with no active control, a quadcopter doesn't hover — it tips over. Every moment of stable flight is the direct, continuous output of several PID control loops (Unit 6), running many times per second.

The pieces, mapped onto this course Actuators: typically four brushless DC motors (Unit 2's brushed-motor concepts, in a more efficient electronically-commutated form), each driven by an ESC (electronic speed controller) — functionally a specialized motor driver (Unit 2, Lesson 8), converting a control signal into the precise current each motor needs. Sensor: an IMU (inertial measurement unit), combining an accelerometer and a gyroscope, measuring the craft's current tilt and rotation rate.
Definition Stabilization, in this context, means continuously adjusting each motor's relative speed so the craft's measured tilt (from the IMU) matches a commanded tilt (often "level," or a small deliberate tilt the pilot commands to move in a direction) — a direct application of closed-loop control (Unit 5) to a physically unstable system.

Why several loops, not just one

Roll, pitch, and yaw, each with their own loop A quadcopter's orientation has three independent rotational axes to control — roll (tilting side to side), pitch (tilting forward/back), and yaw (rotating around the vertical axis) — and each typically gets its own PID loop, computing its own correction, all running simultaneously. The four motors' individual speeds are the shared output all three loops adjust together: increasing the speed of motors on one side while decreasing the opposite side corrects roll, for instance, with pitch and yaw corrections layered on top of the same four motor commands.
Why this needs to run so fast A multi-rotor craft's instability isn't gentle — a small, uncorrected tilt compounds quickly into a much larger one, and then a crash, within a fraction of a second. This is Unit 5, Lesson 23's open-loop-vs-closed-loop stakes made physically real: an open-loop quadcopter (no active correction at all) is not a worse-performing drone, it's one that simply cannot fly. The PID loops here typically run hundreds of times per second, far faster than this course's slower thermal or positional examples ever needed to.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What sensor gives a drone’s flight controller its measurement of current tilt and rotation rate?

The IMU is the drone’s core feedback sensor — measuring tilt and rotation rate, the quantities the stabilization control loops react to.

2. What is an ESC (electronic speed controller), in terms of concepts from this course?

An ESC plays the driver-IC role from Unit 2, Lesson 8 — a small control signal governing a much larger current delivered to the motor.

3. Why is an open-loop (uncorrected) quadcopter not just a "worse-performing" drone, but one that effectively cannot fly at all?

This is the real-world stakes version of Unit 5’s open-loop-vs-closed-loop distinction — for an inherently unstable system, closed-loop control isn’t an enhancement, it’s the only way the system functions at all.

4. Fill in the blank: a quadcopter’s three independent rotational axes — tilting side to side, tilting forward/back, and rotating around the vertical axis — are called roll, pitch, and ___.

Roll, pitch, and yaw are the three axes a drone’s stabilization system controls, each typically with its own PID loop.

5. Fill in the blank: continuously adjusting motor speeds so a drone’s measured tilt matches a commanded tilt is an application of ___-loop control to a physically unstable system.

Closed-loop control (Unit 5) is exactly what stabilization is — continuous measurement and correction, applied here at high speed to an inherently unstable craft.
