Robotic Arms: Sensors, Actuators, and Control Together

Several closed loops running independently, each holding one joint, coordinated into one purposeful motion.

← Unit 7, Lesson 38 Lesson 40 → Contents Glossary →

A robotic arm looks complex, but it's built from a small number of repeating pieces this course has already covered — one per joint, plus a gripper at the end.

One joint, broken down Each joint is, structurally, a servo motor (Unit 2, Lesson 9) or a geared DC motor paired with its own position sensor. That position sensor is often an encoder rather than the simple potentiometer inside a hobby servo.
Definition An encoder is a position sensor that reports rotation, often as a stream of digital pulses counted by the microcontroller (an incremental encoder), or as an absolute angle read directly (an absolute encoder). Either way, it plays the same role Lesson 9's internal servo potentiometer did — measured position feeding back into a closed loop — just often with more precision than a simple potentiometer offers, which matters for an arm's larger, more heavily loaded joints.
Why each joint gets its own control loop Each joint independently measures its own actual angle and drives its own motor toward its own commanded angle — a full closed-loop system (Unit 5) running per joint, often using PID (Unit 6) for smooth, precise positioning against the joint's own load and gravity. A six-joint arm is, in this sense, six of this course's control loops running side by side, not one big combined calculation.

The end-effector: its own small subsystem

A gripper at the arm's end is frequently its own sensor-plus-actuator subsystem: an actuator (often a servo, for a simple open/close gripper) paired with its own sensor — sometimes a simple limit switch (Unit 1, Lesson 5) confirming the gripper has actually closed around an object, sometimes a more sophisticated force sensor. This is exactly Unit 7's integration pattern, nested one level down inside a larger system.

Coordination: the part beyond this course’s scope Making all the joints arrive at their target angles together, so the gripper traces a specific path through space rather than each joint just independently reaching its own target whenever it happens to get there, requires kinematics — the mathematics relating joint angles to the gripper's actual position in space. That's genuinely its own field of study, well beyond this course, but it's worth knowing it sits on top of, not instead of, the per-joint control loops covered here.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Structurally, what is each joint of a robotic arm most similar to, among the actuators covered in Unit 2?

Each joint is essentially a servo-like closed-loop position system, often built from a geared DC motor and its own dedicated position sensor.

2. What role does an encoder play in a robotic arm’s joint?

An encoder is a position-feedback sensor, functionally similar to the potentiometer inside a hobby servo, just often with finer precision for a joint under more load.

3. Why does the text describe a six-joint robotic arm as "six of this course’s control loops running side by side," rather than one single combined calculation?

Each joint runs its own independent sense-decide-act loop, following exactly the same pattern as any single-joint system from Unit 7, just replicated per joint.

4. Fill in the blank: a position sensor that reports rotation, often as a stream of digital pulses or a directly readable absolute angle, is called an ___.

An encoder is the standard position-feedback sensor for a robotic joint, playing the same feedback role a servo’s internal potentiometer plays at smaller scale.

5. Fill in the blank: the mathematics relating a robotic arm’s joint angles to its gripper’s actual position in space, needed to coordinate all joints toward one purposeful motion, is called ___.

Kinematics is the field that sits on top of the per-joint control loops covered in this lesson, coordinating them into deliberate, path-following motion.
