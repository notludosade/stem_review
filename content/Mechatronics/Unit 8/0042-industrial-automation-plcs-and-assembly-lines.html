Industrial Automation: PLCs and Assembly Lines

The same sense-decide-act loop from Unit 3, running at industrial scale, reliability, and safety requirements.

← Lesson 41 Lesson 43 → Contents Glossary →

Step back from a single robot or a single fan, and the same core ideas from this entire course scale up to run an entire factory floor — with reliability requirements this course's earlier hobbyist-scale examples never had to meet.

Definition A PLC (programmable logic controller) is an industrial-grade cousin of the microcontroller (Unit 3) — ruggedized for harsh factory environments (dust, vibration, electrical noise, wide temperature swings) and built for extremely high reliability, since a fault on a production line can be enormously costly or dangerous. Rather than the setup/loop C-like code from Unit 3, PLCs are traditionally programmed in ladder logic, a different-looking notation originally designed to resemble the relay wiring diagrams it replaced.
Different language, same underlying loop Ladder logic looks visually unfamiliar next to this course's pseudocode, but it's solving the identical problem: read sensor inputs, apply decision logic, drive actuator outputs, continuously — exactly Unit 3, Lesson 17's read-decide-write pattern, just expressed in a notation built for factory technicians to trace and troubleshoot visually rather than read as text.

A conveyor line, mapped onto this course’s vocabulary

Example A part travels down a conveyor belt. A photoelectric sensor (conceptually similar to Unit 1's phototransistor-based sensing) detects when it reaches a specific station. That detection triggers an actuator — a pneumatic piston, or a robotic arm (Lesson 39) — to perform an operation on the part. Often, the next step in the sequence won't begin until a sensor confirms the previous step actually completed successfully — a safety interlock, structurally identical to Unit 7, Lesson 37's priority-override pattern, just chained across several stations instead of within one subsystem.
What’s genuinely different at this scale The core loop hasn't changed at all from Unit 3 — what's changed is the consequence of a bug or a hardware fault, and the engineering discipline that follows from it: extensive safety interlocks, redundant sensors, and hardware built to survive far harsher, more continuous duty than a hobbyist project ever needs to.
