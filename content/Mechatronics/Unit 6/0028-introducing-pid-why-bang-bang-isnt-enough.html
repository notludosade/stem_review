Introducing PID: Why Bang-Bang Isn’t Enough

Three terms, three different jobs, combined into one continuously variable output — the most widely used control algorithm in the world.

← Unit 5, Lesson 27 Lesson 29 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 5 ended with a clear list of complaints about bang-bang control: it can't hold an exact value, it overshoots on any system with real lag, and it wastes actuators capable of smooth, proportional output. PID control is the standard answer to all three complaints at once.

Definition PID control computes a continuously variable actuator output from the error signal (Unit 5, Lesson 24), combining three separate terms: Proportional (reacts to the current error), Integral (reacts to accumulated past error), and Derivative (reacts to how fast the error is changing).
output = Kpe + Ki∫e dt + Kd(de/dt)

Here, e is the error (setpoint − measured value), and Kp, Ki, and Kd are tunable constants called gains that control how strongly each term contributes to the final output. Setting any gain to zero removes that term from the controller entirely — a "PD controller" is just PID with Ki = 0, for instance.

Each term’s job, at a glance

P — proportional Reacts to the error right now. Bigger error, bigger corrective push. Covered in Lesson 29.
I — integral Reacts to how long and how much error has accumulated over time. Fixes the small, lingering steady-state error that pure P control leaves behind. Covered in Lesson 30.
D — derivative Reacts to how fast the error is changing right now. Anticipates and dampens overshoot — exactly the "see it coming" capability Unit 5 identified as missing from bang-bang control. Covered in Lesson 31.
Why PID is worth learning specifically PID isn't just one option among many equally common alternatives — it's estimated to be in use in the vast majority of industrial control loops worldwide, from factory temperature control to the drone and automotive systems Unit 8 covers. Learning it well is learning the actual default tool of the field, not a simplified teaching example.

The next three lessons take each term in turn, building intuition for what specifically it fixes and what happens when its gain is set too high or too low. Lesson 32 then puts all three back together, and Lesson 33 covers the practical craft of choosing the three gains for a real system.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What do the letters P, I, and D stand for in PID control?

PID stands for Proportional, Integral, and Derivative — the three terms combined into the controller’s output.

2. In the PID formula, what role do Kp, Ki, and Kd play?

Kp, Ki, and Kd are the tunable weights on the proportional, integral, and derivative terms — adjusting them is exactly what Lesson 33’s tuning process does.

3. What happens to a PID controller if Ki is set to exactly zero?

Setting any gain to zero simply removes that term’s contribution from the output — PID with Ki = 0 behaves as a PD controller.

4. Fill in the blank: the PID term that reacts to how much error has accumulated over time, fixing lingering steady-state error, is the ___ term.

The integral term is specifically what Lesson 30 shows fixes the small, persistent offset a pure proportional controller leaves behind.

5. Fill in the blank: the PID term that reacts to how fast the error is currently changing, helping dampen overshoot, is the ___ term.

The derivative term is what gives PID the "anticipation" capability that bang-bang control (Unit 5) structurally lacks.
