Basic PID Tuning Intuition

Closing out Unit 6 with the practical part textbooks often skip: how do you actually pick Kp, Ki, and Kd?

← Lesson 32 Unit 7, Lesson 34 → Contents Glossary →

Every lesson so far assumed the three gains were already chosen. In practice, choosing them — tuning — is its own skill, usually done by observing a real system's response and adjusting from there rather than computing exact values from a formula.

Definition PID tuning is the process of adjusting Kp, Ki, and Kd so that a system responds quickly, without excessive oscillation or overshoot, and settles precisely at the setpoint. There's no universal "correct" set of values — the right gains depend on the specific system being controlled.

A practical order to tune in

Step 1: Start with P alone (Ki = Kd = 0) Increase Kp gradually from zero until the system responds reasonably quickly, accepting that some overshoot or mild oscillation is normal at this stage — that's expected, not yet a problem to solve.
Step 2: Add I to eliminate steady-state error Increase Ki from zero, slowly, just enough to eliminate the lingering offset from Lesson 29. Too much, too fast risks the windup and overshoot problems from Lesson 30 — small, incremental increases are safer than one large jump.
Step 3: Add D to damp remaining oscillation Increase Kd from zero to reduce overshoot and settle the system more smoothly, watching for the noise-driven jitter from Lesson 31 — if the output becomes erratic rather than smoother, Kd is too high (or the sensor signal needs filtering first, per Unit 4).

A symptom-to-fix reference table

Reading the system’s behavior back into a tuning decision Responds too slowly, never quite reaching setpoint in reasonable time → increase Kp. Oscillates or overshoots significantly → decrease Kp, or increase Kd to add damping. Settles near, but not exactly at, the setpoint → increase Ki (a classic steady-state-error symptom). Output is jittery or erratic → decrease Kd, or apply sensor filtering (Unit 4) before tuning D any further.

This is intentionally qualitative, not a precise mathematical procedure — real, rigorous tuning methods (like Ziegler-Nichols) exist and go well beyond this course's scope, but this symptom-driven intuition is exactly how most working engineers actually start, long before reaching for a more formal method.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the recommended order for introducing PID’s three gains during tuning?

Tuning P first, then I, then D lets you observe and understand each term’s specific effect before layering on the next.

2. A system consistently settles just short of its setpoint, never quite reaching it. Which gain should most directly address this?

Settling near, but not exactly at, the setpoint is the classic steady-state-error symptom that increasing Ki is meant to fix.

3. A system’s output becomes jittery and erratic after Kd is increased. What are two reasonable responses?

Jittery output from a raised Kd usually means the derivative term is reacting to noise — reducing Kd or filtering the signal first (Unit 4) are the standard responses.

4. Fill in the blank: the process of adjusting Kp, Ki, and Kd so a system responds well for its specific application is called PID ___.

Tuning is the practical, largely empirical process of finding workable gains for a specific real system.

5. Fill in the blank: a system that responds too slowly to reach its setpoint in reasonable time most directly suggests increasing ___.

A sluggish response is the classic symptom addressed by increasing the proportional gain first.
