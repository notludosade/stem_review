Derivative Control (D): Damping and Anticipation

The only PID term that reacts to speed, not position — and the one most easily fooled by noise.

← Lesson 30 Lesson 32 → Contents Glossary →

P reacts to how large the error is right now. I reacts to how much error has piled up over time. The derivative term reacts to something neither of the others considers at all: how fast the error is changing, right now.

outputD = Kd × (de/dt)
Definition The derivative term computes the rate of change of the error — how quickly the error is growing or shrinking — and scales that rate by Kd. A large rate of change (error closing fast) produces a large derivative output; a small or zero rate of change produces little or none.
Why this is exactly the "seeing it coming" capability Unit 5 identified as missing Recall the oven overshoot example (Unit 5, Lesson 27): a bang-bang controller reacts only to current position, never to how fast that position is approaching the target, so it can't ease off early. A derivative term does exactly that — as the error shrinks rapidly (the system racing toward the setpoint), the derivative term produces a negative contribution that pulls output down in advance, damping the approach before overshoot actually happens, rather than only reacting after the fact.
Example A system's error is dropping quickly — from 10 to 2 units over a short time — meaning it's approaching setpoint fast. The derivative term detects that fast negative rate of change and contributes a damping (output-reducing) effect, easing off the throttle before the error would otherwise reach zero and overshoot past it, the way pure P control alone might.

The real downside: extreme sensitivity to noise

Why D is the riskiest term to tune aggressively A rate of change is computed from the difference between consecutive readings — and Unit 4, Lesson 21 already established that consecutive readings of an unchanging quantity can still differ slightly due to noise. To a derivative term, that noise-driven difference looks exactly like a real, fast rate of change, and it reacts to it just as strongly as it would to a genuine one. A large Kd on a noisy, unfiltered signal can make a controller's output jumpy and erratic — reacting to noise, not to reality.

This is precisely why Unit 4's filtering lessons weren't a detour — a moving average or EMA filter (Unit 4, Lessons 21–22), applied to the sensor reading before it ever reaches a derivative term, is often what makes a derivative term usable at all rather than a source of jitter.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does the derivative term in a PID controller react to?

The derivative term is specifically about rate of change — how quickly the error is growing or shrinking — unlike P (current size) or I (accumulated history).

2. How does the derivative term help reduce overshoot?

By reacting to a fast-shrinking error in advance, the derivative term can pull output down before overshoot actually happens — the anticipation P and I alone can’t provide.

3. Why is a derivative term especially vulnerable to sensor noise, more so than the proportional or integral terms?

Because it’s built from a difference between readings, the derivative term can’t distinguish a real fast change from noise that happens to look like one — which is why filtering the signal first (Unit 4) matters so much for D specifically.

4. Fill in the blank: the derivative term’s output is proportional to Kd multiplied by the ___ of the error.

Rate of change (de/dt) is exactly what the derivative term measures and reacts to.

5. Fill in the blank: applying a moving average or EMA filter (Unit 4) to a noisy sensor reading before it reaches a derivative term is often what makes that term usable, rather than a source of output ___.

Filtering the raw signal first prevents noise from being misread as a fast, genuine rate of change, avoiding jittery, erratic derivative output.
