Proportional Control (P)

Bigger error, bigger push — the simplest of the three PID terms, and the one that reveals PID’s first real weakness.

← Lesson 28 Lesson 30 → Contents Glossary →

The proportional term is PID's simplest piece: the corrective output scales directly with how large the current error is.

outputP = Kp × error
Example A proportional-controlled heater with Kp = 10 (output in % PWM duty per °F of error): at 5°F below setpoint, output = 10 × 5 = 50% duty. At 1°F below setpoint, output = 10 × 1 = 10% duty. As the temperature closes in on the setpoint, the heater eases off smoothly instead of staying pinned at full power until it hits a bang-bang threshold.

Choosing Kp: too small, too large, and in between

The Kp trade-off Too small a Kp produces a sluggish response — the system barely pushes back even with a sizable error, taking a long time to approach the setpoint. Too large a Kp produces an aggressive, overreacting response — combined with any real system lag (Unit 5, Lesson 27), a large correction can overshoot the setpoint, then overcorrect back the other way, oscillating rather than settling. Finding a reasonable middle ground is the first step of the tuning process in Lesson 33.

The weakness pure P control never fixes on its own

Consider cruise control climbing a hill with only proportional control. As the car slows below the setpoint speed, error grows, and P control increases throttle proportionally — good. But the moment the car's speed stabilizes at some value slightly below the setpoint (with just enough error to keep supplying the extra throttle needed to fight gravity on the incline), the system settles there and stays there. It needs some nonzero, persistent error to justify a persistent throttle correction against the hill's constant pull — so it accepts a small permanent gap instead of ever fully closing it.

Definition Steady-state error is the small, persistent gap between setpoint and actual value that remains even after a system has settled and stopped changing. Pure proportional control very often leaves one behind, because it needs a nonzero error to produce any nonzero corrective output at all.

This isn't a tuning mistake — it's a structural limitation of proportional control by itself, on any system that needs a sustained corrective push just to hold steady (like fighting gravity on a hill, or fighting ongoing heat loss to a cold room). Fixing it is exactly the integral term's job, next.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A proportional controller has Kp = 4. At an error of 6 units, what is the proportional output?

output_P = Kp × error = 4 × 6 = 24.

2. What typically happens when Kp is set too large for a system with real lag?

An overly aggressive Kp combined with real lag tends to produce overshoot and oscillation rather than a smooth, quick settle.

3. Why does a cruise control system using only proportional control often settle at a speed slightly below the setpoint on an uphill climb?

Sustaining a corrective output against a constant disturbance (gravity) requires a persistent error under pure P control — exactly the steady-state error problem.

4. Fill in the blank: the small, persistent gap between setpoint and actual value that remains after a system has settled, common under pure proportional control, is called ___ error.

Steady-state error is the structural weakness of proportional-only control that the integral term (next lesson) is designed to eliminate.

5. Fill in the blank: in the proportional control formula, output equals Kp multiplied by the ___.

Proportional output is simply the gain Kp scaled by the current error — nothing more.
