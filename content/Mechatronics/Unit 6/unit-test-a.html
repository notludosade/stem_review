1. Fill in the blank: PID stands for Proportional, Integral, and ___.

PID combines proportional, integral, and derivative terms into one continuously variable output.

2. What role do Kp, Ki, and Kd play in a PID controller?

Kp, Ki, and Kd are the tunable weights on the proportional, integral, and derivative terms.

3. Fill in the blank: with Kp = 5 and an error of 3 units, the proportional output is ___.

output_P = Kp × error = 5 × 3 = 15.

4. Why does a cruise control system using only proportional control often settle at a speed slightly below setpoint on an uphill climb?

Sustaining a corrective output against a constant disturbance requires a persistent error under pure P control — steady-state error.

5. What does the integral term in a PID controller accumulate?

The integral term sums error continuously over time, giving the controller a memory of persistent error.

6. Fill in the blank: clamping the integral sum to a maximum reasonable value so it can’t inflate without bound during actuator saturation is a common form of anti-___ protection.

Anti-windup protection is the standard fix for the overshoot integral windup would otherwise cause.

7. What does the derivative term in a PID controller react to?

The derivative term is specifically about rate of change, unlike P (current size) or I (accumulated history).

8. Why is a derivative term especially vulnerable to sensor noise?

The derivative term can’t distinguish real fast changes from noise, since both look like a large rate of change.

9. Fill in the blank: with error = 2, integral sum = 5, rate of change of error = 1, Kp = 3, Ki = 2, Kd = 1, the total PID output is ___.

(3×2) + (2×5) + (1×1) = 6 + 10 + 1 = 17.

10. A system consistently settles just short of its setpoint, never quite reaching it. Which gain should most directly address this?

Settling near, but not exactly at, the setpoint is the classic steady-state-error symptom that increasing Ki addresses.
