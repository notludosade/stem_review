Agile Frameworks: Scrum and Kanban

The two dominant concrete implementations of agile values — sprints and backlogs versus a continuous flow of work.

← Unit 6, Lesson 19 Lesson 21 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 2 named waterfall — executing all SDLC phases once, in strict order — as one specific process, and promised its main alternative would come later. This lesson covers agile, the dominant alternative in modern software teams, and its two most common concrete implementations: Scrum and Kanban.

Agile as values, not a single process

Why "agile" alone doesn't tell you exactly what to do The Agile Manifesto lays out a set of values — favoring working software over exhaustive documentation, responding to change over rigidly following an upfront plan, frequent collaboration over a fixed contract negotiated once. These values, on their own, don't specify a concrete day-to-day process. Scrum and Kanban are two different, concrete frameworks that each try to embody those same values in a specific, practical way.

Scrum: fixed-length sprints and defined roles

The sprint Scrum organizes work into fixed-length iterations called sprints (commonly one or two weeks). At the start, the team commits to a specific set of work from the backlog (the full, prioritized list of everything the product might need); at the end, they review what shipped and reflect on the process itself before starting the next sprint.
Defined roles and ceremonies Scrum defines specific roles (a Product Owner who prioritizes the backlog, a Scrum Master who facilitates the process) and recurring meetings: a daily short status sync, sprint planning, a sprint review demoing what shipped, and a retrospective reflecting on how the process itself went.

Kanban: continuous flow, no fixed iterations

A visual board with work-in-progress limits Kanban has no fixed-length sprints — work flows continuously through columns on a board (commonly "To Do," "In Progress," "Done"), and new work is pulled in as capacity frees up, rather than committed to in a fixed batch upfront. A key mechanic is a work-in-progress (WIP) limit: capping how many items can be "In Progress" at once, which forces the team to finish existing work before starting new work rather than spreading itself across too much at once.

Choosing between them

Why the right choice depends on the kind of work Scrum's fixed sprints suit work that can be planned in reasonably sized, predictable chunks — typical feature development. Kanban's continuous flow suits work that arrives unpredictably and needs to be handled roughly in the order it appears — support tickets, production incidents, ongoing maintenance. Many real teams also blend elements of both rather than adopting either framework exactly as specified — the frameworks exist to serve the underlying agile values, not the reverse.
Example A team building a new product feature over the next month plans it out in two-week sprints, since the work is knowable and roughly predictable. The same company's on-call support team uses a Kanban board instead, since incoming bug reports and customer issues can't be scheduled into a fixed two-week sprint — they arrive whenever they arrive, and need to flow through the board continuously.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the relationship between agile and Scrum/Kanban?

Agile is the values layer; Scrum and Kanban are specific, concrete implementations of those values.

2. What does a Kanban work-in-progress (WIP) limit actually do?

A WIP limit is specifically about limiting concurrent in-progress work, not about deadlines or team size.

3. Why might a support team handling unpredictable incoming issues prefer Kanban over Scrum?

Kanban's lack of fixed iterations suits work that can't be predicted and batched into a sprint in advance.

4. Fill in the blank: in Scrum, a ___ is a fixed-length iteration, commonly one or two weeks, during which the team commits to a specific set of backlog work.

Sprint is Scrum's core unit of time-boxed, committed work.

5. Fill in the blank: ___ uses a visual board and work-in-progress limits to manage a continuous flow of work, with no fixed-length iterations.

Kanban is the continuous-flow framework, distinct from Scrum's fixed sprints.
