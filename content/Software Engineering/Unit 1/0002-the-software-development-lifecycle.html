The Software Development Lifecycle

Requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance — what each phase is actually for.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 argued that software engineering is about more than making code work once. The software development lifecycle (SDLC) is the standard name for the sequence of phases a piece of software actually passes through, from an idea to a system someone depends on. Every team's process is some variation on these phases — the names and order can shift, but the work each phase does is remarkably constant.

The six phases

1. Requirements Figuring out what the software actually needs to do, and for whom, before writing any code. Covered in depth in the next lesson — it's the phase where the most expensive mistakes are made, because a wrong requirement caught early costs a conversation, and the same mistake caught late costs a rewrite.
2. Design Deciding the shape of the solution — what the major components are, how they talk to each other, what data structures and architecture to use — before writing the implementation. Unit 4 (design patterns) and Unit 5 (architecture) both live here.
3. Implementation Actually writing the code. This is the phase CP1 and CP2 focused on almost entirely — and it's usually the smallest slice of a system's total lifetime cost, even though it's the most visible one.
4. Testing Verifying the software actually does what the requirements said, and that it didn't break anything that used to work. Unit 3 is dedicated entirely to this phase.
5. Deployment Getting the software into the hands of the people who will actually use it — releasing it to production, to an app store, or to a device. Modern teams often do this continuously, in small increments, rather than as one large release.
6. Maintenance Fixing bugs, patching security issues, and adapting the system as requirements inevitably change, for as long as the software stays in use — which is usually most of its total lifetime, and where most engineering hours across a career actually get spent.

Why "maintenance" is the phase that matters most

Why this reframes what a "software project" even means Students often picture a software project as ending once it ships — the implementation and testing phases feel like the climax. In industry, deployment is closer to the midpoint: the maintenance phase that follows commonly lasts for years and typically consumes more total engineering effort than every earlier phase combined. A system that was easy to write but is painful to maintain is, by the numbers, a worse engineering outcome than one that took longer to write but stays easy to change.

The phases aren't strictly linear

Why real teams loop back constantly The six phases are useful as a checklist of work that has to happen, but almost no real team executes them once, in strict order, start to finish — that rigid version is a specific process called waterfall, covered in Unit 7 alongside its main alternative, agile. In practice, testing reveals a requirement was misunderstood, sending the team back to phase one; maintenance surfaces a new requirement, restarting the whole cycle at a smaller scale. The phases describe what kinds of work exist, not a one-way pipeline.
Example A team ships a feature (deployment), then during maintenance a user reports that a specific input crashes the app. Fixing it means re-entering testing (writing a test that reproduces the crash), implementation (the actual fix), and sometimes even requirements (was the crashing input supposed to be allowed at all?) — all inside what looks, from the outside, like a single small "maintenance" task.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which SDLC phase typically consumes the most total engineering effort over a system's life?

Deployment is closer to a system's midpoint than its end — maintenance is usually where most engineering hours actually go.

2. Why are the six SDLC phases not a strict one-way pipeline in real teams?

A bug found in testing or maintenance can loop a team back through implementation or even requirements, even after deployment has already happened.

3. Which phase did Computer Programming 1 and 2 focus on almost entirely?

Writing correct code is the implementation phase — usually the smallest slice of a system's total lifetime cost despite being the most visible.

4. Fill in the blank: executing all six SDLC phases once, in strict order, start to finish, is a specific process called ___, covered in Unit 7.

Waterfall is the rigid, strictly-linear version of the SDLC; most real teams deviate from it in some way.

5. Fill in the blank: the ___ phase verifies that software does what the requirements said and didn't break anything that used to work, and is the subject of this course's entire Unit 3.

Testing is the verification phase, distinct from implementation (writing the code) and requirements (deciding what it should do).
