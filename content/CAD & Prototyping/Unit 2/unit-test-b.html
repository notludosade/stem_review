Unit 2 Test — Version B

Assemblies and Constraints — mates, degrees of freedom in an assembly, and design intent in the feature tree. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 80% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A folding tripod leg must hold open at exactly 60° relative to the tripod's body. Which mate type fits this?

An angle mate fixes a specific angular relationship between two parts — exactly what a leg held open at a fixed angle needs.

2. Fill in the blank: a collection of separate part files combined with constraints defining how each part can move relative to the others is called an assembly, and those constraints are called ___.

Mates are the assembly-level constraints that fix or limit relative position and motion between parts.

3. A part starts at 6 DOF. A coincident mate (−3 DOF), a distance mate (−1 DOF), and an angle mate (−1 DOF) are applied, with no overlap between them. How many DOF remain?

6 − 3 − 1 − 1 = 1 DOF remains.

4. Fill in the blank: of a free rigid body's 6 total degrees of freedom, 3 are rotational and 3 are ___.

3 translational (x, y, z) + 3 rotational (about x, y, z) = 6 total.

5. A designer edits a base extrude's height. A boss feature was built on top of that extrude's top face. What happens to the boss?

The boss's sketch plane is the base extrude's top face — moving that face moves everything built from it.

6. A shelf bracket's back plate must sit perfectly flush against a wall panel — no gap, and no tilt in any direction. Answer both parts:

(a) Which single mate achieves flush, no-gap, no-tilt contact between the two flat faces?

(b) Fill in the blank: this planar face-to-face mate removes ___ degrees of freedom.

(a) Coincident forces flush, flat contact with no gap and no tilt. (b) It removes 3 DOF: 1 translation perpendicular to the face + 2 tilt rotations.

7. A designer applies mates totaling 7 DOF worth of removal to a single 6-DOF part. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this indicate?

(b) Fill in the blank: since the part started with only ___ total degrees of freedom, removing 7 is not actually possible without redundancy or conflict.

(a) You can't remove more freedom than exists — 7 DOF worth of mates against a 6-DOF budget signals redundancy or conflict. (b) The starting budget is 6 DOF.

8. A label-engraving sketch is anchored to a fillet's rounded edge instead of the part's original flat face. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the practical risk of this choice?

(b) Fill in the blank: preserving design intent generally means referencing ___ geometry — original faces or named planes — rather than geometry produced by features likely to be revised.

(a) A reference to a cosmetic feature's geometry creates a fragile dependency likely to break later. (b) Preferring stable geometry over incidental geometry is a core design-intent practice.

9. A part has 0 DOF remaining. A new mate is added that would force the part into a different position than the one its existing mates already established. Answer both parts:

(a) What is this new mate called?

(b) Fill in the blank: if that new mate had instead agreed with the position already established, it would be called ___ instead.

(a) A mate that disagrees with an already-removed freedom is conflicting. (b) A mate that agrees with it instead is redundant — harmless, but unnecessary.

10. A designer plans a bracket's feature tree: base extrude, boss feature, mounting holes, then fillets and chamfers last. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does building the rough shape first and cosmetic details last generally preserve design intent better than the reverse order?

(b) Fill in the blank: a feature that depends on an earlier feature's geometry is called that earlier feature's ___.

(a) Placing frequently-revised cosmetic features last limits how much later work is at risk when they're tuned. (b) The dependent feature is the child.
