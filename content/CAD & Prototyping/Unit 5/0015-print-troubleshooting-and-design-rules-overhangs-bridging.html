Print Troubleshooting and Design Rules — Overhang Angle and Bridging Distance

Most print failures trace back to a design decision, not a printer malfunction — and most of them are avoidable before the print ever starts.

← Lesson 14 Unit 6, Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 5 closes with the failures every FDM print eventually runs into, and the design rules — decided in CAD, before slicing — that avoid most of them.

Common failure modes

Warping Corners or edges lift off the bed mid-print, usually from uneven cooling and shrinkage as the plastic solidifies. Worse for materials that shrink more as they cool (ABS is notably more prone to this than PLA).
Stringing Thin wisps of plastic left between separate features, from filament oozing out of the nozzle during travel moves between them.
Layer shifting Layers suddenly offset partway through a print — almost always a mechanical issue (a belt skipping, an obstruction), not a design issue.
Poor bed adhesion The first layer fails to stick reliably, risking the whole print detaching partway through.

Maximum unsupported overhang angle

Definition The overhang angle, as used in this lesson, is measured from horizontal — how far a surface leans away from vertical, toward flat. A common rule of thumb across FDM printers and slicers: surfaces within about 45° of vertical (equivalently, steeper than 45° from horizontal) typically self-support and print cleanly without added support material. Surfaces shallower than that — closer to flat, more overhanging — generally need support to print reliably.

This 45° figure is a rule of thumb, not a hard physical law — it varies somewhat by printer, cooling, and material — but it's a reliable enough starting point that most slicers default to flagging overhangs beyond it automatically.

Bridging

Definition Bridging is printing a horizontal span between two already-supported points with nothing underneath at all — the extreme case of an overhang, at 0° from horizontal. It relies on the extruded filament, under slight tension, cooling fast enough to hold its shape before it can sag. Every printer and material combination has a practical maximum reliable bridging distance — commonly cited as roughly 5–10mm for a well-tuned PLA setup with good part cooling, though this is genuinely dependent on material, fan setup, and speed, and shouldn't be treated as a fixed universal number.

Designing around these limits instead of fighting them

Redesign beats slicer tricks Chamfering a downward-facing corner to bring it back under the 45° threshold, reorienting the whole part so a problematic overhang becomes a flat bottom face instead (Lesson 13), or breaking one long unsupported span into several shorter bridges with small internal gussets — all of these are CAD-level design decisions that eliminate a problem outright, rather than papering over it with more support material and post-print cleanup. This is the same design-for-manufacturability logic from Lesson 11, applied specifically to FDM's layer-by-layer constraints.
Why catching this in CAD is worth it A failed print costs filament, machine time, and — if it's discovered late — a missed deadline. A design decision made once in CAD, before the first print attempt, is free by comparison. The same logic from Lesson 11's DFM discussion applies again here: it's far cheaper to fix a problem in the model than to discover it in a failed print.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Using this lesson's convention (angle measured from horizontal), roughly what overhang angle is the common threshold for printing cleanly without support?

Surfaces steeper than about 45° from horizontal (within 45° of vertical) commonly self-support; shallower than that usually needs added support material.

2. What makes bridging different from a typical steep overhang?

Bridging is the 0°-from-horizontal extreme, with no support directly underneath — it depends entirely on the filament's cooling speed to avoid sagging across the gap.

3. Why is warping more commonly associated with ABS than with PLA?

Greater shrinkage during cooling means more internal stress pulling at corners and edges — a material property difference, not a printer setting.

4. Fill in the blank: printing a horizontal span between two supported points with nothing underneath at all is called ___.

Bridging relies on the filament cooling fast enough to hold its own shape across the unsupported gap.

5. Fill in the blank: rather than relying on more support material and cleanup, the more reliable fix for a problematic overhang is usually changing the part's ___ (or its geometry) at the CAD stage.

Reorienting the part, or redesigning the geometry itself (like adding a chamfer), removes the problem outright rather than working around it after the fact.
