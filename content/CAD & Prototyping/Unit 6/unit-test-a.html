1. What is kerf?

Kerf is real material loss along the cut path, typically 0.1–0.3mm, centered on the drawn line.

2. Fill in the blank: kerf = 0.1mm, nominal joint width = 10.0mm. The tab should be drawn at ___ mm for a snug post-cut fit.

Draw tab = nominal + kerf = 10.0 + 0.1 = 10.1mm, so the actual cut tab lands on the true 10.0mm nominal.

3. A designer wants to burn a grayscale photo into a wood panel's surface without cutting through it. Which laser operation is this?

Raster engraving scans line by line, varying power, to render a grayscale image — vector operations follow drawn paths instead.

4. Fill in the blank: a flexible joint cut directly into a rigid sheet as a dense pattern of short parallel slits is called a ___.

A living hinge distributes bending strain across many small flexible strips instead of one rigid piece.

5. A design requires a true compound-curved surface, like a smoothly contoured phone cradle. Which process can actually produce it?

A compound curve is a hard geometric requirement only an additive, fully 3D process like FDM can satisfy — laser cutting is fundamentally limited to flat stock.

6. A tab and slot are both drawn at the same nominal width, with kerf = 0.12mm and no compensation applied. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the resulting slop is ___ mm.

(b) Why does this slop always favor the same direction (tab narrower, slot wider) rather than varying randomly?

(a) Uncompensated slop = 2 × kerf = 2 × 0.12 = 0.24mm. (b) Kerf removal is geometrically predictable, not a random tolerance — which is exactly why it can be compensated for in the file.

7. A finger joint is designed for 3mm plywood, but the actual stock delivered is 4mm plywood. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the most likely consequence of cutting the original file on the thicker stock?

(b) Fill in the blank: an interlocking joint's dimensions have to match the sheet's actual ___, not just look correct in the 2D file.

(a) Finger joints are only as deep as the material is thick — a mismatch prevents proper interlocking. (b) The real stock thickness has to match the file's joint geometry.

8. A flat panel design needs to be produced in a batch of 8 identical parts. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does laser cutting typically have a decisive time advantage over FDM printing for this specific scenario?

(b) Fill in the blank: arranging multiple copies of a flat part efficiently onto one sheet before cutting is called ___ the parts.

(a) Nesting lets an entire batch of flat parts cut in roughly the same time as a single part. (b) This arrangement process is called nesting.

9. A finger-jointed box is fully kerf-compensated for a dead-tight fit, but is difficult to assemble by hand. Answer both parts:

(a) What is a common way to make assembly easier without abandoning kerf compensation entirely?

(b) Fill in the blank: this trade-off echoes Unit 3's fit types directly — trading tightness for ease of assembly is the same trade-off as choosing a ___ fit over an interference fit.

(a) A small deliberate extra gap on top of full kerf compensation trades tightness for assembly ease. (b) This mirrors choosing a clearance fit when free movement or easy assembly matters more than a dead-tight fit.

10. A flat, batch-of-several bracket needs a snug slot and has no compound curves anywhere in its geometry. Answer both parts:

(a) Which process does this design favor, and why?

(b) Fill in the blank: hitting the slot's required tolerance on the laser-cut version depends directly on applying ___.

(a) With no compound curves required, laser cutting's batch and tolerance advantages make it the favored choice for this design. (b) Kerf compensation is what makes the slot land on its intended nominal size.
