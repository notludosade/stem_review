Laser Cutting and Finishing — the 2D workflow, kerf, joinery, and choosing between 3D printing and laser cutting. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 80% or higher to clear this unit.

1. Why does a laser-cut part always come out slightly smaller than its drawn vector outline?

Kerf is real material removed along the cut path — half is lost from the kept piece's edge.

2. Fill in the blank: kerf = 0.25mm, nominal joint width = 5.0mm. The slot should be drawn at ___ mm for a snug post-cut fit.

Draw slot = nominal − kerf = 5.0 − 0.25 = 4.75mm, so the actual cut slot opens up to the true 5.0mm nominal.

3. A designer wants to cut a box's side panels completely out of a sheet of plywood. Which laser operation is this?

Vector cutting follows a drawn path at full power to cut all the way through — exactly what's needed to free a shape from the sheet.

4. Fill in the blank: interlocking rectangular protrusions and matching slots cut into two panels' edges, letting them lock together at a right angle without fasteners, are called ___.

Finger joints (or tabs) are the classic interlocking joint used in laser-cut boxes and enclosures.

5. A design is fundamentally flat, needs a batch of several identical parts, and requires no compound curves anywhere. Which factor most strongly favors laser cutting here?

Laser cutting's batch-nesting advantage is decisive specifically when the design's geometry doesn't force the choice toward 3D printing in the first place.

6. A tab and slot are both drawn at the same nominal width, with kerf = 0.2mm and no compensation applied. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the resulting slop is ___ mm.

(b) Which direction does this slop go — is the tab too big or too small relative to nominal, and the slot too big or too small?

(a) Uncompensated slop = 2 × kerf = 2 × 0.2 = 0.4mm. (b) The tab loses material on both edges (smaller); the slot's opening gains material on both edges (larger) — always in this direction.

7. A living hinge pattern is designed and tested successfully on 3mm acrylic. Answer both parts:

(a) If the same file is cut on a much thicker piece of acrylic without any redesign, what is the most likely result?

(b) Fill in the blank: material ___ directly affects how much a given living-hinge slit pattern can flex before cracking.

(a) A living hinge pattern's flexibility is tuned to a specific material thickness — thicker stock is stiffer and more prone to cracking at the same pattern. (b) Thickness is a direct factor in the hinge's real-world behavior.

8. A flat panel design needs to be produced in a batch of 12 identical parts. Answer both parts:

(a) Roughly how does laser cutting's total job time scale with batch size for a flat, nestable design, compared to FDM printing?

(b) Fill in the blank: an FDM printer without a farm of multiple machines produces a batch ___, one part after another.

(a) Nesting lets laser cutting handle a batch in close to one job's time; FDM's added time accumulates per part without multiple machines running in parallel. (b) A single FDM printer works through a batch sequentially.

9. A finger-jointed enclosure is compensated for full kerf plus an extra 0.08mm of clearance on each mating pair. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the trade-off being made here?

(b) Fill in the blank: a dead-tight, fully kerf-compensated joint with zero extra clearance behaves most like an ___ fit from Unit 3.

(a) Extra clearance trades tightness for assembly ease, at the cost of a small amount of rattle. (b) A dead-tight fit with essentially no play behaves like an interference (or very tight transition) fit.

10. A redesign adds a compound-curved cradle to an otherwise flat bracket. Answer both parts:

(a) What happens to a laser-cutting recommendation for this design, once the compound curve is added?

(b) Fill in the blank: a compound curve is a hard ___ constraint that no amount of favorable cost or time math can substitute for.

(a) A hard geometric requirement overrides any cost/time advantage laser cutting might otherwise have. (b) This is a geometric constraint, not a cost trade-off.
