Joinery for Laser-Cut Parts — Finger Joints, Tabs, Living Hinges, and Kerf Compensation

Draw a tab and its slot at the exact same nominal width, and the kerf makes them fit sloppily by twice its width — every single time, in the same direction. The fix is arithmetic, not luck.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 16 established that kerf shrinks a cut piece relative to its drawn outline. This lesson works out exactly what that does to an interlocking joint, and how to compensate for it so the physical parts actually fit the way the file intended.

Common laser-cut joints

Finger joints / tabs Interlocking rectangular protrusions (fingers or tabs) cut into the edges of two mating panels, alternating with matching slots, so the panels lock together at a right angle without fasteners — though glue is often added for a permanent joint. Common in laser-cut boxes and enclosures.
Living hinge A flexible joint cut directly into an otherwise rigid sheet — commonly plywood or acrylic — using a dense pattern of short parallel slits. The material bends along that line by distributing bending strain across many small flexible strips instead of concentrating it in one rigid piece, letting a rigid material fold like a hinge with no separate hardware.

Working out the kerf compensation math

Consider a tab designed at nominal width w, meant to plug into a slot also drawn at that same nominal width w. The laser removes kerf, centered on every cut line, on both the tab's outline and the slot's outline:

What actually happens, uncompensated Cutting the tab's outline removes kerf/2 from each side of the tab, so the actual tab width = w − kerf (material lost on both edges). Cutting the slot removes kerf/2 from each side of the slot's opening too — but for a slot, that loss makes the opening bigger, so the actual slot width = w + kerf. Uncompensated, drawing both at the same nominal w leaves a total mismatch of (w + kerf) − (w − kerf) = 2 × kerf of slop — the joint comes out sloppy by exactly twice the kerf, no matter what w was.
uncompensated slop = 2 × kerf
The compensation To make the finished, post-cut parts land on the true nominal width w: draw the tab slightly oversized by the kerf, and the slot slightly undersized by the kerf.
Draw tab width = w + kerf → actual cut tab = (w + kerf) − kerf = w. ✓
Draw slot width = w − kerf → actual cut slot = (w − kerf) + kerf = w. ✓
Worked Example — kerf = 0.15mm, nominal joint width = 6.00mm Draw the tab at 6.00 + 0.15 = 6.15mm in the file → actual cut tab = 6.15 − 0.15 = 6.00mm. Draw the slot at 6.00 − 0.15 = 5.85mm in the file → actual cut slot = 5.85 + 0.15 = 6.00mm. Both land exactly on the intended 6.00mm, for a snug fit. Left uncompensated at 6.00mm for both, the real result would have been a 5.85mm tab in a 6.15mm slot — 0.30mm of slop, exactly 2 × kerf as the general formula predicts.
Snug isn't always the goal A dead-tight, fully kerf-compensated fit can be hard to assemble by hand — this echoes Lesson 8's clearance-vs-interference choice directly. Designers of finger-jointed boxes sometimes intentionally add a small amount of extra clearance on top of full kerf compensation (commonly 0.05–0.1mm) for an easier push-fit rather than a true press-fit, trading a little rattle for assembly that doesn't require a mallet.

1. A tab and its slot are both drawn at the same nominal width, with no kerf compensation applied. What actually happens after cutting?

The tab loses material on both edges (narrower); the slot's opening gains on both edges (wider) — a combined 2×kerf mismatch.

2. Kerf = 0.20mm, nominal joint width = 8.00mm. What should the tab and slot be drawn at, respectively, for a snug post-cut fit?

Draw tab = w + kerf = 8.00 + 0.20 = 8.20mm; draw slot = w − kerf = 8.00 − 0.20 = 7.80mm — both cut down to the true 8.00mm nominal.

3. Why might a designer add a little extra clearance on top of full kerf compensation for a finger-jointed box?

This mirrors the clearance-vs-interference choice from Lesson 8 — a small deliberate extra gap trades tightness for ease of assembly.

4. Fill in the blank: a flexible joint cut into rigid sheet material as a dense pattern of short parallel slits, letting it bend without hardware, is called a ___.

A living hinge distributes bending strain across many small flexible strips instead of concentrating it in one rigid piece.

5. Fill in the blank: drawing a tab slightly oversized and a slot slightly undersized by the kerf width, so both land on the true nominal size after cutting, is called kerf ___.

Kerf compensation corrects for the beam's real material removal so the physical part matches the file's intended nominal dimensions.
