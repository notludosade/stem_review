Material Properties for Design — Strength, Stiffness, and Density

A CAD model is geometry-agnostic about material until you choose one — and that choice changes everything about whether the design actually works.

← Unit 3, Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Everything so far — sketches, features, assemblies, tolerances — applies regardless of what a part is made of. Unit 4 turns to material choice itself, starting with the three properties that come up in almost every design decision.

Three properties, three different questions

Definition Yield strength is the stress at which a material begins to permanently (plastically) deform — it answers "how much load before this part is damaged?" Stiffness (measured by the modulus of elasticity, E) is resistance to elastic deformation under load — it answers "how much does this part flex under load, before any damage happens at all?" Density is mass per unit volume — it answers "how heavy is this part for its size?"

These are genuinely independent properties. A material can be stiff but weak and brittle (glass barely flexes, but shatters at low stress). A material can be strong but flexible (spring steel is both strong and deliberately springy, designed to flex elastically over a large range without yielding). Picking a material by only one of these three numbers is a common and avoidable mistake.

Why density often decides the comparison, not strength alone When weight matters — aerospace parts, drones, anything worn on a body — raw strength or stiffness isn't the right comparison at all; specific strength (strength ÷ density) and specific stiffness (E ÷ density) usually are. From the table: aluminum 6061 has roughly 4.6× the yield strength of PLA (276 vs. 60 MPa), but only about 2.2× PLA's density — so aluminum still wins decisively on specific strength for a weight-limited design, but the margin is smaller than the raw strength numbers alone suggest. Plywood, meanwhile, has a lower yield strength than acrylic (about 60 vs. 70 MPa), but its much lower density (0.68 vs. 1.18 g/cm³) gives it a higher specific strength overall — exactly the kind of reversal raw strength numbers alone would hide.
Example — Choosing Among Three 3D-Printing Plastics A bracket needs to survive being dropped occasionally. PLA is the stiffest of the three and easiest to print cleanly, but the most brittle — it has the least give before cracking under a sudden impact. ABS is tougher and more heat-resistant, but tends to warp more during printing and often needs an enclosed printer. Nylon is the toughest and most flexible of the three, but absorbs moisture from the air (weakening it over time if stored improperly) and is generally the hardest of the three to print well. There is no universally "best" material here — the right choice depends on which property (stiffness, toughness, heat resistance, printability) the specific application actually needs most.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a material's modulus of elasticity (E) actually describe?

Modulus of elasticity measures stiffness — resistance to elastic (temporary, recoverable) deformation — which is a separate question from strength.

2. Why can a material be described as "stiff but weak"?

Stiffness and strength answer different questions — how much it flexes versus how much load it takes to damage it — and a material can rank very differently on each.

3. Why is "specific strength" (strength ÷ density) often more useful than raw strength for a weight-limited design?

Two materials can have very different densities alongside similar strength — specific strength normalizes for that, which matters directly whenever weight is a hard constraint.

4. Fill in the blank: the stress at which a material begins to permanently deform is called its ___.

Yield strength marks the boundary between elastic (recoverable) and plastic (permanent) deformation.

5. Fill in the blank: mass per unit volume, the property that matters most whenever a design is weight-limited, is called ___.

Density is independent of both strength and stiffness, which is exactly why specific strength and specific stiffness need to be computed separately.
