Fit Types — Clearance, Transition, and Interference Fits

Two toleranced dimensions meeting each other don't just have a size — they have a relationship, and that relationship determines whether the assembly slides, wobbles, or has to be pressed together.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 7 treated tolerance as a property of one dimension at a time. In practice, tolerance almost always matters most where two parts meet — a shaft in a hole, a pin in a bracket. The relationship between those two toleranced dimensions is called a fit, and it comes in three distinct flavors.

Three fit types

Definition A fit describes the relationship between two mating toleranced dimensions — classically a shaft and the hole it goes into.
Clearance fit The hole's smallest allowed size is still larger than the shaft's largest allowed size — every combination of parts within spec results in a gap. Used wherever parts must rotate or slide freely, like a shaft spinning in a bearing or a hinge pin that has to swing.
Interference fit The hole's largest allowed size is still smaller than the shaft's smallest allowed size — the shaft is always larger than the hole. Assembly requires force: pressing the parts together, heating the hole to expand it, or cooling the shaft to shrink it. The resulting friction grip holds the parts together with no fasteners or adhesive needed. Used for things like a bearing race pressed into a housing.
Transition fit The hole's and shaft's tolerance ranges overlap, so depending on exactly where each individual part lands within its own spec, the result could be a slight clearance or a slight interference. Used where precise location matters (more than a clearance fit would guarantee) but the parts still need to be separable by hand or light force — a dowel pin locating two plates is a classic case.
Worked Example — Checking Three Shafts Against One Hole A hole is toleranced 12.00 +0.02−0.00mm, so it ranges from 12.00mm to 12.02mm. Three candidate shafts:
Shaft X = 11.95 ± 0.01mm (11.94–11.96mm) — always smaller than the hole's smallest size (12.00mm) → clearance fit, guaranteed gap between 0.04mm and 0.08mm.
Shaft Y = 12.04 ± 0.01mm (12.03–12.05mm) — always larger than the hole's largest size (12.02mm) → interference fit, guaranteed interference between 0.01mm and 0.05mm.
Shaft Z = 12.01 ± 0.01mm (12.00–12.02mm) — this range overlaps the hole's 12.00–12.02mm range entirely → transition fit: individual assembled pairs could land anywhere from a slight clearance to a slight interference, depending on exactly where each part measures within spec.
Why picking the wrong fit type breaks a design even when every part is "in tolerance" Every one of the shafts above could be manufactured perfectly within its own stated tolerance and still be the wrong choice. A bearing shaft toleranced for interference when the application actually needed a clearance fit will seize — the bearing can't spin. A locating dowel pin toleranced for clearance when the application needed a transition or interference fit will wobble, and fail to locate the parts precisely. Choosing the fit type is choosing the part's function, not just its size.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A hole ranges from 20.00mm to 20.03mm. A shaft ranges from 19.90mm to 19.96mm. What type of fit is this?

Since the shaft's largest possible size (19.96mm) is still less than the hole's smallest possible size (20.00mm), every valid pair has a gap — a clearance fit.

2. Which fit type is appropriate for a bearing race that must be pressed into a housing and held by friction alone, with no fasteners?

An interference fit's guaranteed friction grip is exactly what holds a pressed-in bearing race in place without any separate fastener.

3. Why is a transition fit sometimes preferred over a clearance fit for a locating dowel pin?

A clearance fit guarantees a gap, which is exactly what a locating pin can't afford — a transition fit keeps the location precise while remaining removable.

4. Fill in the blank: a fit where the shaft is always larger than the hole, requiring force or a temperature change to assemble, is called an ___ fit.

Interference fits require force to assemble — pressing, heating the hole, or cooling the shaft — and hold together by friction alone.

5. Fill in the blank: a fit where the hole's and shaft's tolerance ranges overlap, so an individual assembled pair could land as either a slight clearance or a slight interference, is called a ___ fit.

Transition fits sit in between — overlapping ranges mean the outcome depends on where each specific part actually measures.
