GD&T Basics — Flatness, Perpendicularity, and Position Tolerancing

A ± dimension only controls size. GD&T is the symbolic language for controlling shape, orientation, and location too — and it can do it more efficiently than stacking more ± dimensions ever could.

← Lesson 8 Unit 4, Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

Lessons 7 and 8 controlled size with ± dimensions and fits. But a hole can be exactly the right diameter and still be in the wrong place, or a surface can be exactly the right size and still not be flat. GD&T is the toolset built specifically to control those other kinds of variation — introduced here at a conceptual, introductory level.

What GD&T is

Definition Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing (GD&T) is a standardized symbolic language (formalized in the U.S. as ASME Y14.5) for specifying allowable variation in a part's form, orientation, and location — control that a plain ± linear or angular dimension can't express precisely.
Definition A datum is a theoretically exact reference — a plane, axis, or point — established from a real feature on the part, that other tolerances are measured from. Datums give GD&T tolerances a fixed, unambiguous origin, the same way a coordinate system needs an origin before "x = 5" means anything.

Three GD&T controls, from simplest to most useful

Flatness (a form tolerance) Controls how much a single surface may deviate from a perfect plane — regardless of its size or orientation relative to anything else. The tolerance zone is the space between two parallel planes, separated by the stated flatness value; the entire real surface must lie between them.
Perpendicularity (an orientation tolerance) Controls how much a surface or axis may deviate from exactly 90° relative to a specified datum. Unlike a plain ± angular dimension, it defines a genuine tolerance zone (two parallel planes for a surface, or a cylinder for an axis) rather than just an angle range measured from one edge.
Position (a location tolerance) Controls how far a feature's actual center — most often a hole's axis — may deviate from its theoretically exact ("basic") location, measured relative to datums. Almost always specified as a diameter: a cylindrical tolerance zone the feature's axis has to fall within, no matter which direction it drifts.
Why a round tolerance zone beats two ± dimensions Locating a hole with separate ± x and ± y dimensions instead of true position tolerancing creates a square tolerance zone, not a round one — and a square zone is quietly inconsistent about how much error it allows depending on direction. Take a hole located with ±0.10mm in both x and y: straight along either axis, the allowed deviation is 0.10mm, but toward a corner (the diagonal), the allowed deviation grows to √(0.10² + 0.10²) ≈ 0.141mm — about 41% more than the axis-aligned case, purely because the zone is square. A position tolerance of Ø0.20mm instead defines a genuinely round zone with a uniform 0.10mm radius allowance in every direction — no hidden extra slack in the corners, and no unnecessarily tight restriction along the axes either. That's the actual engineering justification for true position tolerancing, not just tradition.
Why this matters beyond the math GD&T lets a designer state precisely what functionally matters — "this hole's axis must fall within a Ø0.20mm cylinder of its true position" — instead of over-specifying with ± dimensions that either restrict directions that don't actually matter to the part's function, or leave an inconsistent gap in a direction that does.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a datum provide in GD&T?

Datums give GD&T tolerances a fixed, unambiguous origin, established from a real feature on the actual part.

2. A surface is toleranced with flatness 0.05mm. What does this control?

Flatness is a pure form tolerance — it says nothing about orientation or location, only how close the surface itself comes to a perfect plane.

3. A hole is located with ±0.08mm in both x and y instead of a true position tolerance. Along the diagonal direction, roughly how much more deviation does this allow compared to straight along either axis?

A square ± x/± y zone's diagonal reach is √(0.08² + 0.08²) ≈ 0.113mm versus 0.08mm along an axis — about 41% larger, the same ratio as the lesson's worked example.

4. Fill in the blank: the orientation tolerance that controls how close a surface or axis comes to exactly 90° relative to a datum is called ___.

Perpendicularity defines a real tolerance zone for how close to 90° the feature actually is — not just a single angle measurement.

5. Fill in the blank: because a position tolerance is usually specified as a diameter, its tolerance zone for a hole's axis is shaped like a ___.

A diameter-based position tolerance defines a cylindrical tolerance zone — the hole's actual axis must fall somewhere inside that cylinder.
