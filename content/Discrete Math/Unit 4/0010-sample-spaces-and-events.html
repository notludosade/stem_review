Sample Spaces and Events

Every counting tool from Unit 3 was secretly building toward this: probability, for a finite sample space, is nothing more than counting favorable outcomes over total outcomes — sets and combinatorics doing double duty as the entire foundation.

1. Sample spaces and events

Definition — Sample Space and Event The sample space S (sometimes written Ω) is the set of every possible outcome of an experiment. An event E is simply a subset of the sample space, E ⊆ S — some collection of outcomes you care about. "Rolling a die" has sample space S = {1,2,3,4,5,6}; the event "rolling an even number" is E = {2,4,6} ⊆ S.

2. Probability of an event

Definition — Probability (Equally Likely Outcomes) When every outcome in a finite sample space S is equally likely, the probability of event E is P(E) = |E| / |S| — the count of favorable outcomes divided by the count of total outcomes. Both counts are exactly what Unit 3's tools compute.
Worked example Draw one card from a standard 52-card deck. S has |S| = 52 outcomes. Let E = "the card is a face card" (jack, queen, king, in any of 4 suits): |E| = 12. P(E) = 12/52 = 3/13.

3. The axioms of probability

The Three Axioms (1) 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1 for every event E — a probability is never negative and never exceeds certainty. (2) P(S) = 1 — something in the sample space always happens. (3) If E₁ and E₂ are mutually exclusive (E₁ ∩ E₂ = ∅), then P(E₁ ∪ E₂) = P(E₁) + P(E₂). Axiom 3 is Lesson 7's addition principle again, restated for probability instead of raw counts — the same mutual-exclusivity condition, for the same reason: overlapping events would double-count.
Why this lesson has almost no new ideas That's deliberate. A sample space is a set (Unit 2). An event is a subset (Unit 2). Computing P(E) = |E|/|S| for a non-trivial event is exactly Unit 3's counting toolkit — permutations when order matters, combinations when it doesn't. Probability, at this level, is the first place in the course where every earlier unit gets used in the same calculation at once.

1. Rolling two dice and summing them, which is the sample space?

The sample space needs to track every possible outcome of the actual experiment (rolling two distinguishable dice), which is 36 equally-likely ordered pairs — the sum is a derived event, not the sample space itself, and treating sums as equally likely (they aren't) is a common error this distinction prevents.

2. Drawing 1 card from a 52-card deck, what is P(the card is a king)?

There are 4 kings among 52 cards: P = 4/52 = 1/13.

3. Why does Axiom 3 (additivity) require E₁ and E₂ to be mutually exclusive?

This is exactly the addition principle's mutual-exclusivity requirement from Lesson 7 — an outcome in E₁ ∩ E₂ would otherwise be added into the total twice.

4. Fill in the blank: by Axiom 2, P(S) = ___.

The sample space contains every possible outcome, so something in it is guaranteed to happen — probability 1, certainty.

5. Fill in the blank: formally, any subset of the sample space is called an ___.

An event is defined as E ⊆ S — any collection of outcomes from the sample space, including S itself and ∅.
