How Engineering Interviews Are Structured

The typical stages a candidate moves through, and what each one is actually testing for.

← Unit 1, Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

A resume and portfolio (Unit 1) get you an interview. This unit covers what happens once you're actually in the process. This lesson maps out the typical stages, since knowing what each stage is testing changes how you should prepare for it.

Stage 1: the recruiter screen

What it tests A short call, usually with a recruiter rather than an engineer, confirming basic fit: are your qualifications, availability, location requirements, and compensation expectations roughly aligned with the role? This stage rarely tests deep technical skill — its purpose is to avoid wasting a technical interviewer's time on a mismatch that could have been caught in five minutes.

Stage 2: the technical screen

What it tests A conversation (sometimes with a live problem) with an actual engineer, usually 30-60 minutes, checking whether your technical background is real and roughly matches what the role needs. This is the first stage where being unable to explain your own portfolio project in technical depth becomes a serious problem.

Stage 3: the technical/design interview(s)

What it tests One or more deeper sessions — a design or whiteboard problem (Lesson 5), sometimes a take-home project — testing how you actually reason through an open-ended technical problem, not just whether you know facts. This is usually the highest-weighted stage in the final hiring decision.

Stage 4: the behavioral interview

What it tests Questions about past experiences ("tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate") assessing how you actually work with others under real conditions — collaboration, communication, handling failure — covered in depth in Lesson 6.

Stage 5: the onsite / final round

What it tests Often a combination of the above stages compressed into one longer day, sometimes including a meeting with a potential manager or team members, checking both technical fit and whether you'd work well within this specific team.

Why understanding the stage changes your preparation

Why this matters Preparing the same way for every stage wastes effort. Rehearsing STAR-method behavioral stories (Lesson 6) does little good in a recruiter screen focused on logistics, and drilling whiteboard problems does little good in a behavioral round focused on how you handle conflict. Identifying which stage you're walking into — and asking the recruiter directly if it's unclear — lets you prepare for what's actually being tested.

Not every process follows this exact order

Smaller companies sometimes compress several of these stages into one or two conversations; some processes skip a dedicated behavioral round entirely. The stages above are the common pattern, not a universal rule — but the underlying idea, that different conversations test different things, holds regardless of how a specific company structures its process.
