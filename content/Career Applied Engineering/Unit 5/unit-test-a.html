1. A civil engineering firm is designing a permanent highway bridge whose span and load requirements are fixed by the physical site. Which process best fits, and why?

Waterfall fits well when requirements are fixed by physical, safety-critical, or regulatory constraints known in advance, since costly mid-project changes are avoided by planning fully first.

2. A software team is building a new mobile app feature where user needs will only become clear once people try an early version. Which process fits, and why?

Agile fits when requirements are genuinely uncertain up front; short, adaptive cycles let a team correct course based on real feedback instead of a fixed guess.

3. A calendar invite simply says "Weekly Sync" with no further detail. What is the most likely consequence, based on why most meetings fail?

A meeting without a clear, stated purpose fails at the moment it's scheduled — attendees show up unsure what it's even for.

4. An engineer evaluating vendor proposals has a sibling who works at one of the vendors under consideration. What is the actual ethical obligation here?

The obligation is disclosure, not avoidance — conflicts are often unavoidable, but staying silently involved without disclosing one is the actual violation.

5. Fill in the blank: pressure to cut a corner rarely arrives as an explicit unsafe instruction — it usually shows up disguised as ordinary ___ pressure, which is exactly what makes it easy to go along with.

Because it feels like a scheduling conversation rather than an obvious ethical violation, this kind of pressure is the hardest to recognize and resist.

6. A firm is deciding how to run a new project. Answer both parts about how that choice should actually be made:

(a) What is the real deciding factor between agile and waterfall, according to the lesson?

(b) Fill in the blank: many real projects mix the two approaches — for example, waterfall for a fixed physical structure alongside agile for its accompanying software — an approach called a ___.

(a) Neither approach is universally better; how knowable the requirements are up front is the deciding question. (b) Mixing both within one project, matched to what each piece of work actually needs, is a hybrid approach.

7. A manager is planning a meeting to decide between two vendor proposals. Answer both parts about running it well:

(a) Besides a clear purpose, what else does the meeting need to actually work?

(b) Fill in the blank: without a brief ___ record of what was decided, a meeting's outcome exists only in attendees' memory, which fades and diverges within days.

(a) Purpose, the right people, and a written outcome are the three things every meeting needs. (b) A written record is what keeps the decision from dissolving into differing memories.

8. A team member is writing a weekly status update. Answer both parts about how to structure it well:

(a) What three sections cover almost every situation in a status update?

(b) Fill in the blank: of those three sections, ___ items should appear near the top, since that's the one status a reader can actually act on.

(a) Shipped, in progress, and blocked cover nearly every situation a reader needs to know about. (b) Burying blocked items at the bottom delays exactly the help a status update is meant to enable.

9. An engineer discovers a safety-critical test result was altered before a report went out. Answer both parts about the right sequence of action:

(a) What should the engineer do first?

(b) Fill in the blank: escalating to an outside regulator or the press is a real option, but it's typically appropriate only as a ___ resort, after internal channels have been tried and failed.

(a) Internal channels should be given a genuine chance first. (b) External reporting is a last resort, not a first response, since escalating too early can be unnecessary or foreclose better internal options.

10. A team skips a test "just this once" to hit a deadline, assuming it would have passed anyway. Answer both parts about handling this well:

(a) What is the recommended response, based on the lesson's guidance on pressure from inside your own team?

(b) Fill in the blank: recording the concern in ___ at the moment it's raised is most valuable exactly when it's most tempting to skip, since it's the only record that survives if the situation is later disputed.

(a) Naming the real risk explicitly turns an implicit shortcut into an explicit decision. (b) A written record protects both the person raising the concern and anyone who acts on it later.
