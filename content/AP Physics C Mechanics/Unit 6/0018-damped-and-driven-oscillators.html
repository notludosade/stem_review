Damped and Driven Oscillators

Real oscillators lose energy to friction — the same drag force from Unit 2 — and can regain it dramatically if pushed at just the right rhythm. This lesson stays qualitative: the point is recognizing the three regimes, not solving the ODE by hand.

← Lesson 17 Unit 7, Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

The Damped Oscillator Equation Adding a drag force −b(dx/dt) (Unit 2, Lesson 5's linear drag) to the SHM equation gives m d²x/dt² + b dx/dt + kx = 0. Its behavior falls into three qualitative regimes, depending on how strong the damping b is relative to m and k:
Three Damping Regimes Underdamped (weak damping): oscillation continues, but its amplitude decays, x(t) ≈ Ae−γtcos(ω′t + φ) — a cosine wrapped in a shrinking exponential envelope. Critically damped: the system returns to equilibrium in the least time possible, with no oscillation at all. Overdamped (strong damping): the system crawls back to equilibrium slowly, also without oscillating.
Why this is Unit 2's drag force, not a new force b dx/dt is exactly Lesson 5's linear drag, −bv, now added into an SHM system instead of free fall. Every real spring or pendulum experiences some drag from air resistance or internal friction, which is why undamped SHM (Lesson 16) is always an idealization — a real oscillator's amplitude always shrinks eventually, converting mechanical energy into heat.
Driven Oscillators and Resonance Adding a periodic driving force F₀cos(ωdt) to a damped oscillator produces a steady-state oscillation whose amplitude depends strongly on how close the driving frequency ωd is to the system's natural frequency. Resonance — a dramatic buildup in amplitude — occurs when ωd is at or near that natural frequency; without damping to cap it, resonant amplitude would grow without bound.

1. Which damping regime returns to equilibrium fastest, without any oscillation?

Critical damping is the boundary case that returns to equilibrium in minimum time while still avoiding any oscillation — overdamped systems are slower, and underdamped systems overshoot and oscillate.

2. Resonance — a dramatic buildup in oscillation amplitude — occurs when:

Driving a system near its own natural frequency lets energy be added efficiently on nearly every cycle, building amplitude dramatically — strong damping actually suppresses resonance rather than causing it.

3. In an underdamped oscillator, x(t) ≈ Ae−γtcos(ω′t + φ). What role does the e−γt factor play?

e^(−γt) decreases toward zero as t grows, multiplying the cosine's amplitude down cycle by cycle — the oscillation's envelope, tracing where the peaks and troughs sit.

4. Fill in the blank: a real oscillator's amplitude eventually shrinks because energy is lost to ___, converting mechanical energy to heat.

Damping (drag/friction) is the non-conservative force responsible for the decaying amplitude in any real, undriven oscillator.
