Reverse-Engineering Behavior. 10 questions covering every lesson in this unit. Score 80% or higher to clear this unit.

1. Why is a compiled game executable called a "black box"?

"Black box" describes something understood only through outside observation, not internal inspection.

2. Fill in the blank: a tool that attempts to reconstruct a higher-level approximation of a program's source from its compiled form is a ___.

Ghidra and IDA are two well-known decompiler/disassembler tools.

3. What is a crash dump?

A crash dump captures program state at failure, distinct from an ongoing log file written during normal play.

4. Fill in the blank: diagnosing a problem by making one small, deliberate change at a time and observing the result is called ___ debugging.

Trial-and-error debugging needs no source code or special tools — just patience and one change at a time.

5. What specifically triggers a DMCA Section 1201 concern, as opposed to ordinary reverse-engineering?

The trigger is specifically defeating a protection measure, not reverse-engineering in general.

6. Two different reverse-engineering approaches exist. Answer both parts:

(a) What does a disassembler produce, as distinct from a decompiler?

(b) Does using either tool require real, specialized skill?

Disassemblers and decompilers differ in output level, but both require genuine specialized skill.

7. A memory scanner flags an address that dropped by 10 right after the player took damage. Answer both parts:

(a) Does this prove that address is definitely the player's health value?

(b) What is the general process reverse-engineering compiled behavior actually resembles?

Reverse-engineering behavior is closer to detective work — hypothesis and testing — than to reading documentation.

8. A modder's game crashes with a vertex-count error in the log. Answer both parts:

(a) Does diagnosing this specific error require a decompiler?

(b) Where might a modder look next if the log alone weren't enough to solve it?

Log files and community knowledge solve most problems long before any deep reverse-engineering tool is needed.

9. A modder reverse-engineers an undocumented save-file format to build a compatible save editor. Answer both parts:

(a) Does this generally require defeating a protection measure like DRM or anti-cheat?

(b) What kind of case is this closest to?

Building a compatible tool from an undocumented format, without defeating a protection measure, is a textbook interoperability scenario.

10. Two modders both use a memory scanner on the same game. Answer both parts:

(a) One builds a quality-of-life mod with no anti-cheat defeated. The other uses it to cheat in ranked online matches. Do these carry the same legal risk?

(b) What earlier lesson's boundary does this scenario most directly echo?

Identical tools can land on opposite sides of the legal line depending on purpose — the same principle Unit 1 introduced, now applied to reverse-engineering specifically.
