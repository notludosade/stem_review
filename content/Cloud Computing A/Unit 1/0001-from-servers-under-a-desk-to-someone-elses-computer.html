From Servers Under a Desk to Someone Else's Computer

You already know how to write programs. This course is about where those programs actually run once someone besides you needs to use them — and why almost nobody buys their own hardware to do it anymore.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Every program you wrote in Computer Programming 1 ran on one machine — yours. The moment a program needs to be reachable by other people, at any hour, from anywhere, that stops being enough. Someone needs a machine that stays on. This lesson is about the two ways to get one: buy it yourself, or rent time on someone else's — and why the industry overwhelmingly settled on the second option over the last fifteen years.

The old way: on-premises infrastructure

Definition On-premises ("on-prem") infrastructure means an organization buys, owns, and physically operates its own servers — usually racked in a server closet or a company-owned data center. The organization is responsible for every layer: buying the hardware, wiring the network, keeping the power and cooling running, patching the operating systems, and replacing failed disks at 2 a.m.
Worked example A mid-size online retailer expects its site to handle 2,000 visitors an hour on a normal day, but 40,000 an hour during its Black Friday sale. Running on-prem, it has to buy enough servers to survive Black Friday — meaning for the other 364 days of the year, most of that hardware sits mostly idle, still costing money in power, cooling, floor space, and staff time to maintain. Buy too little, and the site crashes during the one week that matters most for revenue. Buy enough, and you've paid full price for capacity you use one day a year.
Why this is a genuinely hard problem, not just an inconvenience The retailer's dilemma is called capacity planning, and on-prem infrastructure makes it brutal: hardware has to be ordered, shipped, and installed weeks or months before it's needed, based on a guess about future demand. Guess low and you fail during your busiest moment. Guess high and you've sunk real money into machines depreciating in a closet. There's no way to "return" a rack of servers the week after Black Friday.

What "the cloud" actually is

Cloud computing solves the capacity-planning problem by changing who owns the hardware — and how finely it can be adjusted.

Definition Cloud computing means renting computing resources — processing power, storage, networking — from a provider's massive, shared data centers over the internet, paying only for what you actually use, and adjusting how much you use in minutes instead of months. The three big general-purpose providers are Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud Platform (GCP), though dozens of smaller providers exist for specific niches.

The retailer from the example above, running in the cloud, requests more server capacity a few days before Black Friday, pays for that extra capacity while it's actually needed, and releases it right after — paying close to nothing for that capacity the rest of the year. The provider can offer this because it is doing the same thing for thousands of other customers simultaneously, on hardware so large in aggregate that any one customer's spike is a rounding error against its total capacity.

CapEx vs. OpEx: the accounting shift underneath the technical one

Definition Capital expenditure (CapEx) is a large upfront purchase of a long-lived asset — buying a server outright. Operating expenditure (OpEx) is an ongoing, recurring cost tied to actual usage — a monthly cloud bill. On-prem infrastructure is overwhelmingly CapEx: big purchases, paid up front, before you know exactly how much you'll need. Cloud computing is overwhelmingly OpEx: smaller, continuous payments that scale with what you're actually consuming right now.
Why teams stopped running their own servers It isn't that on-prem hardware disappeared — banks, hospitals, and governments still run plenty of it, often for the regulatory and control reasons Lesson 3 covers. But for most organizations, the cloud shifts the hardest part of capacity planning — guessing demand months in advance and eating the cost of guessing wrong — onto a provider who has both the statistical scale to smooth out any one customer's spikes and the balance sheet to absorb the risk of owning the hardware. A startup with no idea whether it will have 10 users or 10 million next year can start on the cloud for a few dollars a month and grow into more capacity exactly as fast as its users show up, instead of guessing right the first time or going bankrupt buying servers nobody uses.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the core capacity-planning problem that on-premises infrastructure creates?

The core issue is the mismatch between a long hardware lead time and unpredictable future demand — not a technical limitation of the hardware itself.

2. Which best defines cloud computing as used in this course?

Cloud computing is about the rental, metering, and elasticity model for infrastructure — not about any specific language or "the web" generally.

3. Why can a cloud provider absorb one customer's Black Friday traffic spike without that customer having to own extra hardware?

Pooling demand across many customers with different peaks is exactly what lets a provider offer elastic capacity that no single customer could justify owning outright.

4. Fill in the blank: an ongoing, recurring cost that scales with actual usage, like a monthly cloud bill, is called ___.

OpEx is recurring and usage-based, in contrast to the large upfront purchase of CapEx.

5. Fill in the blank: infrastructure that an organization buys, owns, and physically operates itself, such as servers in a company-owned closet or data center, is called ___ infrastructure.

On-premises is the term for infrastructure an organization owns and runs itself, as opposed to renting it from a cloud provider.
