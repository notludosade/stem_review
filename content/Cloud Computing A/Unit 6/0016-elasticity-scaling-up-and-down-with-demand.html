Elasticity — Scaling Up and Down with Demand

Lesson 1 opened this whole course with a retailer stuck buying enough hardware for Black Friday and eating the cost the other 364 days. This lesson is the actual, mechanical answer to that problem.

← Unit 5, Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

Every piece of this course so far — VMs, load balancers, managed databases — can be sized once and left alone. Real traffic doesn't hold still, though: a retail site might see ten times more visitors at 8 p.m. than at 4 a.m., every single day. Sizing a fleet for the 8 p.m. peak wastes money at 4 a.m.; sizing it for the 4 a.m. quiet crashes at 8 p.m. Elasticity is the mechanism that makes picking one fixed size unnecessary.

What elasticity actually is

Definition Elasticity is a cloud environment's ability to automatically add or remove compute capacity in near-real time, in response to actual demand, with no human manually provisioning anything. The mechanism behind it is usually an auto scaling group: a defined pool of identical servers (built from Lesson 4's instance types) with a minimum size, a maximum size, and a scaling policy — a rule like "add another server whenever average CPU usage across the fleet exceeds 70%; remove one whenever it drops below 30%."

Worked example: a daily traffic curve A news site's auto scaling group is configured with a minimum of 2 servers and a maximum of 12. Overnight, with almost no readers, it settles at its 2-server minimum, paying only for that baseline capacity. As readers wake up and traffic climbs through the morning, the scaling policy adds servers automatically, one at a time, staying just ahead of demand. During a rare viral-story spike, it climbs toward its 12-server maximum. Once the spike passes, it scales back down over the following hour — no engineer touched a single setting during any of it.

Horizontal vs. vertical scaling

Definition Horizontal scaling (scaling out) means adding more servers of the same size, exactly what the auto scaling group above does — and it pairs naturally with Lesson 11's load balancer, which simply routes traffic across however many servers currently exist. Vertical scaling (scaling up) means making one existing server bigger — more vCPU, more memory. Vertical scaling has a hard ceiling (the biggest instance type a provider offers) and usually requires a brief restart; horizontal scaling has no such ceiling and can add capacity without interrupting servers already running. Most elastic cloud architectures lean on horizontal scaling for exactly these reasons.
Why a maximum matters as much as a minimum It's tempting to think of the maximum as pure caution, but it's doing two jobs at once. First, it's a hard cost ceiling — Lesson 17 covers how directly server count translates into your bill, and an auto scaling group with no maximum has, in effect, no spending limit. Second, it protects against runaway scaling triggered by a bug: a broken scaling policy or a misbehaving piece of code that keeps requesting more capacity in a loop has nowhere to go past the configured maximum. The minimum, in turn, guarantees a baseline of servers is always running, so there's no gap where a sudden burst of traffic arrives to find zero capacity waiting.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is elasticity, in the cloud infrastructure sense?

Elasticity is specifically about automatic, demand-driven capacity changes, not a fixed size or a discount.

2. Why does horizontal scaling pair naturally with a load balancer?

Adding or removing identical servers is exactly what a load balancer is built to route across seamlessly.

3. Why does an auto scaling group's maximum setting matter, beyond just being cautious?

The maximum is both a spending limit and a safeguard against a scaling policy or bug that keeps requesting more capacity indefinitely.

4. Fill in the blank: a defined pool of identical servers with a minimum size, maximum size, and a scaling policy is called an ___.

An auto scaling group is the mechanism that implements elasticity automatically.

5. Fill in the blank: making one existing server bigger — more vCPU, more memory — rather than adding more servers, is called ___ scaling.

Vertical scaling grows one machine's size, unlike horizontal scaling's approach of adding more machines.
