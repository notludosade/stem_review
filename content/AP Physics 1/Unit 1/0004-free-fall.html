Free Fall

Lesson 3's equations, with one variable pinned down for good: a = −g, always, for anything falling near Earth's surface with no air resistance.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Free fall is motion under gravity alone, with air resistance ignored. Near Earth's surface, every object in free fall — regardless of mass — has the same constant downward acceleration, g ≈ 9.8 m/s².

Why mass doesn't matter A feather and a hammer, dropped in a vacuum, hit the ground at the same time. This surprises intuition built from everyday experience, where air resistance slows light objects far more than heavy ones — but that's air resistance's effect, not gravity's. Strip air resistance away (or make the object dense enough that it's negligible) and every falling object accelerates identically at g.

Free fall problems are Lesson 3's equations with a locked in as −g (if up is chosen positive) or +g (if down is chosen positive) — the physics content of "free fall" is entirely in that one substitution.

Example: dropped, not thrown A stone is dropped (v0 = 0) from a cliff and hits the ground 3 s later. Taking down as positive: Δy = v0t + ½gt² = 0 + ½(9.8)(3)² = 44.1 m. The cliff is 44.1 m tall — found with no information about the stone's mass, because mass never entered the calculation.
Example: thrown straight up A ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. Taking up as positive (so a = −9.8 m/s²), find the time to reach its highest point, where v = 0. Using v = v0 + at: 0 = 15 + (−9.8)t → t = 1.53 s. By symmetry, the ball takes exactly as long to fall back down as it took to rise — free fall's motion is time-symmetric about the peak.

At the peak, acceleration is not zero

A common misconception: at the very top of the throw, since velocity is momentarily zero, students assume acceleration must be zero too. It isn't. Acceleration is g downward at every instant of free fall, including exactly at the peak — that constant downward acceleration is precisely what causes velocity to be zero at that one instant, on its way from positive to negative.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A bowling ball and a tennis ball are dropped simultaneously from the same height in a vacuum. Which hits the ground first?

With no air resistance, free-fall acceleration is g for every object regardless of mass, so both fall identically and land simultaneously.

2. At the exact peak of a ball's upward throw, its acceleration is:

Acceleration in free fall is constant throughout the entire flight, including the peak — it's velocity, not acceleration, that happens to be zero there.

3. A ball thrown straight up takes 2 s to reach its peak. Ignoring air resistance, how long does the entire round trip (up and back to the starting height) take?

Free-fall motion is time-symmetric about the peak: time up equals time down, so the round trip is 2 × 2 s = 4 s.

4. Fill in the blank: near Earth's surface, every object in free fall (no air resistance) has the same downward acceleration, approximately ___ m/s².

g ≈ 9.8 m/s² is the free-fall acceleration constant, identical for every mass in the absence of air resistance.
