Position, Displacement, Velocity, and Speed

Four words physics uses much more precisely than everyday speech does — and the difference matters the moment an object doubles back on itself.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Kinematics is the description of motion — not yet asking why something moves (that's Unit 2's job), just carefully describing how it moves. That description starts with a coordinate: a number line laid down along whatever path the object travels, with a zero point and a positive direction you choose.

Position An object's position, written x, is its location on that number line at a given instant. Position is only meaningful relative to a chosen origin and positive direction — "x = 5 m" means nothing without also knowing where x = 0 is and which way is positive.

Everyday language treats "distance traveled" and "how far from start" as the same thing. Physics splits them apart on purpose:

Displacement vs. distance Displacement (Δx) is the change in position: Δx = xf − xi, a straight-line difference that can be positive, negative, or zero. Distance is the total length of path actually covered, always positive, and never decreases. Walk 5 m east then 5 m back west: displacement is 0 m (you're back where you started), but distance is 10 m (your legs did work covering that path).

The same split happens one derivative up, between velocity and speed:

average velocity = Δx / Δt  average speed = (total distance) / Δt

Velocity inherits displacement's sign — it's Δx per unit time, so it can be negative (motion in the negative direction) as well as positive. Speed is always the magnitude of how fast ground is covered, with no direction attached. A car that drives 100 m east then 100 m west in 20 s has an average velocity of 0 m/s but an average speed of 10 m/s.

Average vs. instantaneous

Everything above is an average over some time interval Δt — it tells you nothing about what happened in between. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one specific instant: what the average velocity approaches as Δt shrinks toward zero, centered on that instant. A speedometer reads instantaneous speed; a road trip's "average speed" for the whole drive is the distance-over-total-time version above, and the two are rarely equal at any single moment.

Example A runner starts at x = 0 m, reaches x = 100 m at t = 12 s, then jogs back to x = 40 m at t = 20 s. Average velocity for the whole 20 s: Δx/Δt = (40 − 0)/20 = 2 m/s. Average speed: total distance (100 + 60 = 160 m) / 20 s = 8 m/s. The two numbers describe genuinely different things about the same 20 seconds.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A hiker walks 3 km north, then 3 km south, back to the trailhead. What is the hiker's displacement?

Displacement only compares final position to initial position. Ending exactly where you started always means zero displacement, no matter how much distance was covered getting there.

2. Which quantity can be negative?

Velocity inherits displacement's sign, so motion in the negative direction gives negative velocity. Distance and speed are magnitudes, and magnitudes are never negative.

3. A car's speedometer reading at one specific moment is an example of:

A speedometer reports what's happening right now, at one instant — the instantaneous rate, not an average taken over some longer interval.

4. Fill in the blank: the change in position, xf − xi, is called ___.

Displacement is defined exactly as this signed difference between final and initial position.

5. Fill in the blank: the total length of path actually traveled, which never decreases and is never negative, is called ___.

Distance only accumulates — it tracks path length covered, with no notion of direction to cancel anything out.
