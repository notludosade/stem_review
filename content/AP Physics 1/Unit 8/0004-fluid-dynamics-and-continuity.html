Fluid Dynamics and Continuity

Squeeze a hose and the water speeds up — a direct consequence of a fluid having nowhere else to go.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

The Continuity Equation For an incompressible fluid flowing through a pipe with no leaks, A1v1 = A2v2 — the product of cross-sectional area and flow speed (called the volume flow rate) stays constant along the pipe. Where the pipe narrows, the fluid must speed up; where it widens, the fluid slows down.
Why area and speed trade off exactly this way In a steady flow with no fluid created, destroyed, or squeezed into storage anywhere along the pipe, the volume of fluid passing any cross-section per second must be the same everywhere — otherwise fluid would be piling up or vanishing somewhere in between, which an incompressible fluid can't do. Volume flow rate is exactly Av (area times speed, since a slab of fluid of length vΔt and cross-section A has volume AvΔt passing per time Δt), so Av must be the same constant at every point along the pipe.
Example: water speeding up through a nozzle A hose with cross-sectional area 0.001 m² carries water at 2 m/s. It narrows to a nozzle with area 0.0002 m². Using continuity: (0.001)(2) = (0.0002)v2 ⟹ v2 = 0.002/0.0002 = 10 m/s — the water exits 5 times faster than it entered, exactly matching the 5-fold reduction in area.
Why covering part of a hose's opening with your thumb makes the water shoot farther Partially blocking the opening with a thumb reduces the effective cross-sectional area the water can exit through, without changing the volume flow rate supplied by the hose behind it — so by continuity, the exit speed must increase to compensate, launching the water stream farther, exactly as in the nozzle example above.
Example: blood flow through a narrowed artery An artery of normal cross-sectional area 0.0003 m² carries blood at 0.3 m/s. A narrowing (stenosis) reduces the area to 0.0001 m² at one point. By continuity: (0.0003)(0.3) = (0.0001)v2 ⟹ v2 = 0.9 m/s — blood must speed up threefold through the narrowed section, a physical effect used in medical imaging to detect arterial narrowing by measuring local flow speed.

1. The continuity equation A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ for an incompressible fluid follows from:

Continuity comes from volume conservation for an incompressible fluid in steady flow, not directly from momentum or energy conservation.

2. Water flows at 4 m/s through a pipe of area 0.008 m², entering a section of area 0.002 m². What is the speed in the narrower section?

(0.008)(4) = (0.002)v₂ ⟹ v₂ = 0.032/0.002 = 16 m/s.

3. Why does partially blocking a hose's opening with your thumb make the water shoot out faster?

By continuity, a smaller exit area forces a higher exit speed to maintain the same volume flow rate.

4. Fill in the blank: the equation A₁v₁ = A₂v₂, expressing constant volume flow rate along a pipe, is called the ___ equation.

The continuity equation ensures volume flow rate stays constant for an incompressible fluid in steady flow.
