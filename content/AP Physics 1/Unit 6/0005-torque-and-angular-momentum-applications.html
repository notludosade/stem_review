Torque, Angular Momentum, and Applications

Closing the loop between torque and angular momentum — and putting conservation to work on real spinning systems.

Torque as the Rate of Change of Angular Momentum τnet = ΔL/Δt — net torque equals the rate at which angular momentum changes, the rotational counterpart to Fnet = Δp/Δt (Unit 4, Lesson 1). This is really the same statement as the angular impulse-momentum theorem from Lesson 3 (τnetΔt = ΔL), just divided through by Δt and viewed as a rate rather than a one-time change.
Why this single relationship underlies both Lessons 3 and 4 Everything in this unit's back half comes from one equation. When τnet = 0, ΔL/Δt = 0, meaning L never changes — this is exactly Lesson 4's conservation law. When τnet ≠ 0, L changes at a rate set by the torque — this is Lesson 3's angular impulse-momentum theorem in its instantaneous form. Conservation isn't a separate rule; it's the special case of this equation where torque happens to be zero.
Application: Kepler's second law from angular momentum conservation A planet orbiting the Sun feels only a gravitational force always pointed directly at the Sun (through the pivot), so it exerts zero torque about that point (τ = rF sin θ with θ = 0° — Unit 5, Lesson 2). With τnet = 0, the planet's angular momentum L = mrv sin θ about the Sun is conserved throughout its elliptical orbit — this is precisely Kepler's second law: a planet moves faster when closer to the Sun (small r) and slower when farther away (large r), since L stays fixed while r changes.
Application: a diver's full sequence, revisited Off the board, a diver has some fixed L set by their initial push (any small board torque acts only during launch). Once airborne, no external torque acts (gravity acts through the center of mass, contributing zero torque about it — Unit 5, Lesson 2), so L is conserved throughout the dive exactly as in Lesson 4: tucking shrinks I and spins up rotation for the somersaults, extending before entry grows I back and slows the spin for a clean entry — a single conserved L, reshaped by changing I at will.
Why gravity alone can't add or remove spin once airborne Gravity acts as if concentrated at the center of mass (Unit 4, Lesson 5), and torque is measured relative to that same point in the diver's rotation — so gravity's own moment arm about the center of mass is zero, contributing no torque about that axis. This is exactly why a diver's total angular momentum, once launched, is locked in for the rest of the dive: the only way to change ω is to change I, not to fight physics with more spin from nowhere.

Unit 6 in summary

This unit built up the rotational analogs of Unit 3's energy concepts and Unit 4's momentum concepts: rotational kinetic energy ½Iω² (Lesson 1), its role in rolling-object races down a ramp (Lesson 2), angular momentum L = Iω (Lesson 3), its conservation when no external torque acts (Lesson 4), and the single rate relationship τnet = ΔL/Δt tying torque and angular momentum together exactly as force and linear momentum are tied (Lesson 5). Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. The relationship τ_net = ΔL/Δt is the rotational counterpart to which linear relationship?

Just as net force is the rate of change of linear momentum, net torque is the rate of change of angular momentum.

2. Why does a planet's angular momentum about the Sun stay conserved throughout its orbit?

Torque requires a force component perpendicular to the position vector; a force always aimed straight at the pivot (θ = 0°) contributes zero torque, so L is conserved.

3. Once a diver has left the board, why can't gravity change their total angular momentum during the dive?

With zero moment arm about the center of mass, gravity's torque about that axis is zero, so L is conserved for the rest of the dive regardless of tucking or extending.

4. Fill in the blank: a planet moving faster when close to the Sun and slower when far away, as a direct consequence of conserved angular momentum, is known as ___.

Kepler's second law is a direct consequence of angular momentum conservation, since gravity exerts zero torque about the Sun.
