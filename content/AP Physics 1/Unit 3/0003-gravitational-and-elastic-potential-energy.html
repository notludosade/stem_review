Gravitational and Elastic Potential Energy

Energy doesn't only live in motion — it can be stored in position, waiting to be released.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Potential energy is energy associated with an object's position or configuration, stored by a conservative force — one where the work it does depends only on starting and ending position, never on the path taken in between. Gravity and the spring force are both conservative; friction is not (Lesson 5).

Gravitational Potential Energy Near Earth's surface, PEgrav = mgh, where h is height measured from some chosen reference level. Only changes in height matter — the reference level (h = 0) can be placed anywhere convenient, since it's ΔPE that appears in every physical calculation, never PE's absolute value.
Why the reference level doesn't matter Choosing the floor, the tabletop, or sea level as h = 0 changes every object's PE number, but never changes any physically measurable result (like a final speed), because those results only ever depend on the difference in PE between two configurations. This is analogous to choosing an origin for position in Unit 1 — position values are arbitrary, displacement is not.
Elastic (Spring) Potential Energy A spring stretched or compressed by a distance x from its natural length obeys Hooke's law: the spring force is F = −kx, where k is the spring constant (N/m) and the negative sign shows the force always opposes the displacement, pulling back toward equilibrium. The energy stored is PEelastic = ½kx².
Why elastic PE has a ½ and gravitational PE doesn't Gravity near Earth's surface is (to a very good approximation) constant, so the force doing work stays the same value mg throughout a fall of height h, giving simply mgh. A spring's force changes as it stretches (F = −kx grows with x), so the work done compressing it from 0 to x is the area under a triangular force-vs-distance graph — ½ × base × height = ½ × x × kx = ½kx² — the same "area under the graph" logic from Unit 1, Lesson 2's v-t graphs.
Example: gravitational PE A 4 kg book is lifted from the floor to a shelf 1.5 m high. Taking the floor as h = 0: ΔPE = mgh = 4(9.8)(1.5) = 58.8 J of gravitational potential energy is gained.
Example: elastic PE A spring with k = 250 N/m is compressed 0.2 m from its natural length. PEelastic = ½(250)(0.2)² = 5 J.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. If you choose the tabletop instead of the floor as your h = 0 reference level, which of these changes?

The reference level shifts every PE value by a constant amount, but any physically meaningful result depends only on ΔPE, which is unaffected by this choice.

2. A spring is compressed twice as far from its natural length as before (same spring constant k). Compared to before, its stored elastic potential energy is:

PE_elastic = ½kx² depends on x squared, so doubling the compression distance quadruples the stored energy.

3. Why does gravitational PE = mgh have no factor of ½, while elastic PE = ½kx² does?

Work equals the area under a force-vs-distance graph; gravity's constant force gives a rectangular area (mgh), while a spring's linearly increasing force gives a triangular area (½kx²).

4. Fill in the blank: a force whose work depends only on starting and ending position, never on the path taken, is called a ___ force.

Conservative forces (gravity, spring force) are exactly the ones for which a potential energy function can be defined.
