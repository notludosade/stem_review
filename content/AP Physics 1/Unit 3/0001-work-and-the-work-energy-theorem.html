Work Done by a Force

Physics gives "work" a stricter meaning than everyday speech: no displacement, no work — no matter how hard you push or how long you hold something up.

← Unit 2, Lesson 7 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Work, defined The work done by a constant force F on an object as it moves through a displacement d is: W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector. Work is a scalar (no direction of its own), measured in joules (J), where 1 J = 1 N·m.
Why holding something up does zero work Standing still holding a heavy box feels exhausting, but physics says you've done zero work on it — because the box's displacement is zero (d = 0), and W = Fd cos θ is zero whenever d is zero, no matter how large F is. The fatigue is real (muscles do internal biochemical work), but no work is done on the box, since the box never moves.

The cos θ term matters just as much as F and d themselves: only the component of force along the direction of motion contributes to work. A force entirely perpendicular to displacement (θ = 90°) does exactly zero work, regardless of its magnitude.

Example: carrying a box horizontally Carrying a 10 kg box at constant height across a room, the force you exert is vertical (supporting its weight) while the displacement is horizontal — θ = 90°, so cos 90° = 0. You do zero work on the box during the horizontal walk, even though you're clearly exerting force the whole time.
Example: pulling a sled at an angle A sled is pulled by a rope at 30° above horizontal with 50 N of tension, moving the sled 20 m horizontally. W = Fd cos θ = 50(20)cos 30° = 866 J. Only the horizontal component of the tension (50 cos 30°) contributes; the vertical component does zero work since the sled doesn't move vertically.

Positive, negative, and zero work

The sign of cos θ carries real physical meaning. When θ < 90° (force has a component in the direction of motion), work is positive — energy is being added to the object's motion. When θ > 90° (force opposes motion, at least partly), work is negative — energy is being removed. Kinetic friction, for instance, always does negative work on a moving object, since it always points opposite to the direction of motion.

Why negative work is not a contradiction "Negative work" sounds like a paradox, but it simply means the force is taking energy away from the object's motion rather than adding to it — exactly what friction does when it slows a sliding block, or what gravity does to a ball thrown straight up (gravity points down, displacement points up during the rise, so θ = 180° and work is negative).

Net work

When several forces act on an object, the net work can be found either by summing the individual works done by each force separately, or equivalently by first finding the net force (Unit 2, Lesson 2) and computing Wnet = Fnetd cos θ directly. Both routes give the same number — this equivalence is exactly what Lesson 2's work-energy theorem is built on.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A waiter carries a tray at constant height across a room at constant velocity. How much work does the waiter's upward supporting force do on the tray?

The supporting force is vertical while displacement is horizontal, so θ = 90° and cos θ = 0 — zero work, regardless of the force's magnitude.

2. A block slides across a rough floor and slows down due to kinetic friction. The work done by friction on the block is:

Kinetic friction always points opposite to the direction of motion, so θ = 180°, cos θ = −1, and the work done is negative.

3. A rope pulls a crate at 40° above horizontal with 100 N of tension, moving it 5 m horizontally. The work done by the tension is closest to:

W = Fd cos θ = 100(5)cos 40° = 500(0.766) ≈ 383 J.

4. Fill in the blank: work is measured in units called ___, equal to one newton-meter.

The joule (J) is the SI unit of work and energy, defined as 1 N·m.
