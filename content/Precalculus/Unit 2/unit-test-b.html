1. A degree-5 polynomial has a positive leading coefficient. What is its end behavior?

Odd degree sends the two ends in opposite directions; a positive leading coefficient gives the standard pattern — falling on the left, rising on the right, the same shape as x³.

2. Fill in the blank: For a power function f(x) = xⁿ with n even, the two ends of the graph point in the ___ direction.

Even powers send both ends the same way — for example x² rises on both the left and the right.

3. For f(x) = (x − 2)(x + 5)⁴, answer both parts:

(a) What happens at x = −5?

(b) Fill in the blank: What is the degree of f? ___

(a) (x + 5) appears four times — even multiplicity — so the graph only touches the axis at x = −5 and bounces back without crossing. (b) Adding up the exponents on the factors, 1 + 4 = 5, gives the polynomial's degree.

4. Answer both parts about turning points:

(a) A polynomial's graph has 4 turning points. What is the smallest possible degree it could have?

(b) What is the maximum possible number of turning points for a degree-7 polynomial?

(a) A degree-n polynomial can have at most n − 1 turning points, so 4 turning points require a degree of at least 5. (b) Running that rule forward instead, a degree-7 polynomial allows at most 7 − 1 = 6 turning points.

5. Fill in the blank: dividing f(x) by (x − r) and getting a remainder of 0 tells you that r is a ___ of f.

Zero remainder means (x − r) divides evenly — that's the Factor Theorem, equivalent to saying r is a zero of f.

6. A degree-4 polynomial with real coefficients has zeros 2, −3, and 1 + 2i. Answer both parts:

(a) What must its fourth zero be?

(b) Fill in the blank: How many of these 4 zeros are real numbers? ___

(a) Complex zeros of a real-coefficient polynomial always come in conjugate pairs, so 1 + 2i forces 1 − 2i to be a zero too — that accounts for all 4 zeros the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees. (b) Only 2 and −3 are real; the conjugate pair 1 + 2i and 1 − 2i is non-real, so exactly 2 of the 4 zeros are real.

7. For f(x) = (x + 4)(x − 1) / [(x + 4)(x + 7)], answer both parts:

(a) What happens at x = −7?

(b) What happens at x = −4?

(a) (x + 7) is zero at x = −7 but doesn't cancel with anything in the numerator, so the graph grows without bound there — a true vertical asymptote, not a hole. (b) (x + 4) cancels between the numerator and denominator, so the function is undefined at x = −4 but doesn't blow up there — a removable hole instead.

8. What is the domain of f(x) = (x − 2) / (x² − 25)?

x² − 25 equals 0 at x = 5 and x = −5, so those are the two values excluded from the domain.

9. For f(x) = (x + 7) / (x² + 2), answer both parts:

(a) What is the horizontal asymptote?

(b) Where are the vertical asymptotes?

(a) The numerator's degree is smaller than the denominator's, so the horizontal asymptote is always y = 0 in that case. (b) Setting x² + 2 = 0 gives x² = −2, which has no real solution, so the denominator is never zero and the graph has no vertical asymptotes at all.

10. Fill in the blank: To find the equation of a slant asymptote, divide the numerator by the denominator and keep only the ___.

The remainder term always shrinks toward 0 as x grows without bound, leaving just the quotient as the line the graph approaches.
