Rates of Change & Behavior of Graphs

How fast a function is changing, and where it's going up, down, or turning around.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

So far you've evaluated functions and read domain/range off a graph. Now: how do you describe how a function changes — not just what it outputs? That question is the entire on-ramp to Calculus, so it's worth building real intuition for it now rather than just memorizing a formula.

1. Average rate of change

Pick any two points on a graph. The average rate of change between them is just the slope of the straight line connecting them — how much y changed, divided by how much x changed.

Definition For a function f, the average rate of change from x = a to x = b is:
[ f(b) − f(a) ] / ( b − a )

The line connecting the two points is called a secant line. Drag either point below and watch the secant line's slope update live.

Try it — Drag Either Point

—

This is f(x) = x² − 3x. Try dragging the two points to opposite sides of the dip — notice the average rate of change can come out negative, positive, or even zero.

Notice the secant line's slope changes depending on which two points you pick — average rate of change is a property of an interval, not a single point. That's different from "the slope," which you may be used to only meaning one fixed number for a whole line.

2. Increasing, decreasing, and turning points

Zoom into a single point instead of two. As you drag left to right, is the graph's height going up or down right there?

Try it — Drag Along the Curve

—

Drag slowly across the middle. There's exactly one spot where the label flips — that's not a coincidence.

The spot where it flips is called a local minimum — a "turning point" where the function stops decreasing and starts increasing. (If a graph turns the other way, from increasing to decreasing, that peak is a local maximum. Together these are called extrema.) A graph's behavior — the language of increasing, decreasing, and turning points — is usually more useful for understanding a function's shape than any single output value.

Why it matters for Calculus Shrink the gap between the two secant-line points in Widget 1 down toward zero, and the secant line settles onto the graph at a single point — that limiting line is called the tangent line, and its slope is the instantaneous rate of change. That's the derivative. Everything you just did by hand with two draggable points, Calculus does with a limit.

Height dropping as x increases is exactly what "decreasing" means — the same thing you watched the readout confirm in Widget 2.

3. At a local minimum, the function switches from ___ to increasing.

A local minimum is the bottom of a dip: the graph was falling, bottoms out, then starts rising.
