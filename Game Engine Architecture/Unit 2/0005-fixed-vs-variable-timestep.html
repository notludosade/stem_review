Fixed vs. Variable Timestep

Lesson 4's update(delta_time) took "how much time to advance the world by" as a given. Deciding what that number actually is turns out to be one of the most consequential choices in an engine's whole architecture.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

A frame doesn't take exactly the same amount of time every time. One frame might take 16 milliseconds, the next 22, the next 15 — depending on what's on screen, what the CPU is doing elsewhere, and the hardware it's running on. The question this lesson answers: should update() be told the real, wobbly duration of the frame that just happened, or a fixed number it can always count on?

Two strategies, two very different guarantees

Definition A variable timestep passes update() the actual, measured duration of the previous frame — it's always accurate to real elapsed time, but never exactly the same number twice. A fixed timestep always advances the simulation by the same constant amount (say, exactly 1/60th of a second) regardless of how long the frame actually took, running update() zero, one, or multiple times per rendered frame to keep pace with real time.

Worked example A ball is moving at a steady 100 pixels/second. Under variable timestep, a 250ms hitch (maybe fifteen normal frames' worth of time) makes update() advance the ball 25 pixels in one single jump — visibly teleporting, and worse, a fast-moving object could tunnel straight through a thin wall it should have collided with. Under fixed timestep, that same 250ms of real time still gets simulated in a burst of fifteen small 1/60-second steps, so the ball still ends up 25 pixels further along — but as fifteen small, physically consistent steps a collision system can actually catch, not one impossible leap.
Why fixed timestep is the default choice for physics Physics math (Unit 5 covers this directly) assumes small, roughly-equal time steps — a spring, a collision response, or gravity integrated over one giant 250ms leap can produce wildly different, sometimes unstable results compared to the same real duration simulated in fifteen small steps. A fixed timestep makes the simulation's behavior reproducible and independent of frame rate: the same inputs produce the same physics outcome on a fast gaming PC and a slower laptop alike, which variable timestep cannot promise.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a fixed timestep guarantee that variable timestep does not?

Fixed timestep guarantees a constant, small simulation step, decoupled from real frame duration.

2. What can go wrong under variable timestep when a single frame takes an unusually long time (a "hitch")?

A single huge timestep can move an object past a thin collider entirely in one leap.

3. Why is fixed timestep generally preferred for physics simulation specifically?

Small, consistent steps are what keep physics math numerically stable and reproducible.

4. Fill in the blank: always advancing the simulation by the same constant time step, running update() multiple times if needed to keep pace with real time, is called a ___ timestep.

Fixed timestep is defined by its constant, unchanging step size.

5. Fill in the blank: passing update() the actual, measured duration of the previous frame — accurate but never the same number twice — is called a ___ timestep.

Variable timestep passes whatever real duration actually elapsed, frame by frame.
