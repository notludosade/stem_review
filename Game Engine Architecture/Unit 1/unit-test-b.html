1. A racing game and a puzzle game are built on the same engine despite having almost nothing in common at the rules level. Why does that work?

Shared systems-level needs, not shared rules, is what a general-purpose engine serves.

2. Fill in the blank: code that encodes a rule specific to one title — like what "damage" means or what happens when the player dies — belongs to the ___, not the engine.

Rule-specific logic is exactly what marks code as game code rather than engine code.

3. What lets a studio swap in a different physics engine without touching its rendering code?

Clean subsystem separation is exactly what enables swapping one without disturbing another.

4. Fill in the blank: the reusable, general-purpose software layer providing rendering, physics, audio, input, and asset systems is called a game ___.

This is the term defined at the start of Lesson 1.

5. Why do serious engines avoid hard-coding a concept like "health" directly into their core?

The engine's job is to stay genre-agnostic, leaving meaning like "health" entirely to game code.

6. A three-person team builds a small 3D platformer on an existing engine instead of from scratch. Answer both parts:

(a) Without an engine, "drawing a textured triangle" alone would cost the team...

(b) Fill in the blank: an engine doesn't make their game good — it makes it possible to spend limited time on the small fraction that's actually ___ to their game.

An engine buys back time that would otherwise go to re-solving already-solved problems.

7. A team hard-codes "if enemy touches player, subtract 10 health" straight into the engine's core collision system. Answer both parts:

(a) In the moment, this shortcut is...

(b) The cost shows up later as...

The cost of blurring the engine/game line is deferred, architectural, and paid later.

8. In a "player fires a weapon" sequence, several subsystems act in order. Answer both parts:

(a) Which subsystem draws the bullet and any muzzle flash on screen?

(b) Which subsystem plays the gunshot sound?

Rendering draws the visual result; audio plays the corresponding sound.

9. In the 1990s, most studios wrote a new engine for nearly every game. Answer both parts:

(a) That was largely because...

(b) Today, building a full custom engine for most teams is usually...

The calculus flipped once general-purpose engines matured enough to fit most teams' needs.

10. Answer both parts about why engines separate subsystems:

(a) Keeping rendering, physics, and audio as separate subsystems mainly enables...

(b) Fill in the blank: this mirrors the ___ and cohesion principle from Software Engineering, applied to performance-critical systems.

Coupling and cohesion explain exactly why subsystem separation pays off long-term.
