Streaming and Level of Detail

Lesson 17 established a hard memory ceiling. This lesson is about the two techniques that let a game world feel enormous — hundreds of square kilometers, thousands of objects — without ever actually needing all of it in memory at once.

← Lesson 17 Unit 7, Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

A player only ever sees a small slice of an open world at once — nearby, and roughly what's on the horizon. Everything far outside that doesn't need full detail loaded in memory, or sometimes any detail at all yet. Two related ideas exploit exactly that.

Load what's near, simplify what's far

Definition Streaming loads and unloads assets in the background, based on the player's position, so only the area actually near the player (plus a margin) is ever fully resident in memory — Lesson 17's eviction, driven automatically by distance instead of a fixed rule. Level of detail (LOD) keeps multiple versions of the same model, at decreasing triangle counts, and swaps in a simpler version as an object gets farther from the camera — since a distant object covers only a few screen pixels anyway, its extra detail would be invisible, wasted rendering work (tying back to Unit 4's draw-call cost).

Worked example A mountain village has 40 detailed houses. Standing in the village square, the nearest houses render at LOD0 — full detail, thousands of triangles each, close enough that a player could see individual roof shingles. A house at the far edge of the village renders at LOD2 — a few hundred triangles, indistinguishable at that distance from the full version. A house past the village entirely might not be loaded at all yet, waiting for streaming to bring it in only once the player gets close enough to actually reach it.
Why these two techniques are usually paired, not used alone LOD alone still requires every object's data to already be sitting in memory, just rendered cheaply — it saves rendering cost, not memory budget. Streaming alone still renders whatever's loaded at full detail, wasting GPU time on triangles too small to see. Together, streaming keeps memory usage bounded regardless of how large the world is, while LOD keeps rendering cost bounded regardless of how much detail nearby objects actually have — solving Lesson 17's memory problem and Unit 4's draw-call problem with one paired strategy instead of two separate, unrelated fixes.
