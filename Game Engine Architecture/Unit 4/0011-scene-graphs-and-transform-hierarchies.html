Scene Graphs and Transform Hierarchies

Lesson 10's vertex stage needs to know each triangle's exact position in the world. For anything with moving parts — a hand attached to an arm attached to a character walking through a level — "exact position" is a small chain of math, not a single number.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Rotate a character's shoulder, and the whole arm — upper arm, elbow, forearm, hand — needs to swing with it, without each part needing its own separately-computed rotation. That's exactly the problem a parent-child transform hierarchy solves.

Local space, parents, and world space

Definition A transform is an object's position, rotation, and scale. A scene graph arranges every object in a scene as a tree, where each node's transform is stored relative to its parent ("2 units forward from wherever the hand is," not "at world coordinate 47, 12, 3"). An object's actual world-space transform — where it truly is in the scene — is computed by combining its own local transform with every parent's transform, all the way up to the root. Move or rotate a parent, and every descendant's world position updates automatically, without their local transforms changing at all.

Worked example The elbow's local transform never changes as you rotate the shoulder slider alone — it's still "bent 0° relative to the upper arm," exactly as before. What changes is the elbow's world position, because computing world space means walking up to its parent (the shoulder) first. This is exactly why animating a whole character skeleton doesn't mean recomputing every bone by hand every frame — moving the hip node once correctly carries the spine, arms, and head along with it, each contributing only their own small local rotation on top.
Why this hierarchy exists instead of storing every object's world position directly Storing only world-space transforms would mean moving a character's whole arm requires manually recalculating the position of the hand, every finger bone, and anything the character happens to be holding — every single frame that arm moves. A parent-child hierarchy lets each part of a complex object store the one small, local relationship that actually stays constant (the hand is always roughly where the wrist puts it), while a single change high in the tree propagates correctly to everything below it for free.
