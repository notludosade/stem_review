Physics Engines: What They Handle So You Don't Have To

Lessons 13 and 14 built collision detection and integration from scratch, on purpose, to see how they actually work. In a real engine, you'd almost never write either by hand again — this lesson is about the dedicated subsystem that takes over from here.

← Lesson 14 Unit 6, Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Broad phase, narrow phase, integration, and one more thing neither lesson touched yet: what happens the instant after two rigid bodies are found to be overlapping? Something has to decide how hard they push apart, in which direction, and how that affects both objects' velocities — correctly, for boxes, ramps, stacks, and joints, all at once. That's what a dedicated physics engine is for.

The subsystem, not the algorithm

Definition A physics engine is the subsystem (Unit 1's term) responsible for the entire simulation loop: broad and narrow phase collision detection, collision response (resolving overlap and updating velocities so objects push apart realistically instead of passing through each other), integration, and often constraints — rules like "these two bodies must stay connected at this joint" that have to hold true even while everything else is moving. Most engines embed or integrate with a battle-tested, general-purpose physics engine (Box2D for 2D, Bullet or PhysX for 3D) rather than writing this subsystem from scratch.

Worked example A crate stacked on a ramp needs friction to stay put instead of sliding, and a chain-link bridge needs each plank to stay connected to its neighbors while still swinging under a player's weight. Both are exactly the kind of numerically finicky, easy-to-get-subtly-wrong problems (friction coefficients, joint constraint solving under many simultaneous contacts) that a mature physics engine has already spent years tuning — which is precisely why "which object took how much damage from that collision" is left to game code, while "did the crate slide off cleanly" is not.
Why almost nobody writes a full physics engine for a single game Lesson 14's two-line integrator looks simple, but a general physics engine handling stacks of boxes, ramps, joints, and dozens of simultaneous contacts without visibly jittering or exploding is genuinely difficult numerical software — the kind of problem where subtle bugs surface only in specific, hard-to-predict configurations. This mirrors Lesson 1's argument about engines generally: physics is a shared, extremely hard problem nearly every game needs solved the same way, which is exactly the profile of problem worth outsourcing to a dedicated, heavily-tested library instead of re-deriving.

1. What does "collision response" specifically refer to?

Collision response is specifically the resolve-and-update-velocities step after overlap is detected.

2. What is a physics "constraint"?

Constraints maintain relationships (like joints) between bodies across the whole simulation.

3. Why do most teams use an existing physics engine (Box2D, Bullet, PhysX) instead of writing their own?

Difficulty and existing, battle-tested solutions are why teams reuse rather than reinvent.

4. Fill in the blank: resolving overlap and updating velocities so two colliding bodies push apart realistically is called ___.

Collision response is the specific term for this resolve-and-update step.

5. Fill in the blank: a rule like "these two bodies must stay connected at this joint," which must keep holding as the simulation runs, is called a ___.

Constraints are exactly this kind of persistent relationship between bodies.
