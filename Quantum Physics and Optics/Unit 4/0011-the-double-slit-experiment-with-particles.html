The Double-Slit Experiment with Particles

Fire electrons through a double slit one at a time, and something strange happens: each lands as a single dot, yet over time the dots build up the exact same interference pattern light makes.

The Electron Double-Slit Experiment Fire electrons at a double slit, one at a time, with a detector screen recording where each one lands. Each electron arrives as a single, discrete point — clearly particle-like. But after thousands of electrons accumulate, the pattern of dots forms the same bright-and-dark fringe pattern Young's double slit (Unit 2) produced for light — clearly wave-like. This happens even though each electron passes through with no other electron anywhere nearby to interfere with.
Why this proves duality is a property of matter, not just light Unit 3 showed photons — normally treated as waves — also behave like particles. This experiment shows the reverse is equally true: electrons — normally treated as particles — also behave like waves. Wave-particle duality is not "light is secretly weird, matter is normal"; it's a universal feature of quantum objects, light and matter alike. There's a second, equally important twist: if a detector is placed at the slits to determine which slit each electron actually passes through, the interference pattern vanishes, replaced by the simple two-band pattern classical particles would make. Measuring which-path information destroys the interference — the act of finding out "which slit" changes the outcome.
Comparing three predictions If electrons were classical particles: two bands, directly behind each slit. If electrons were classical waves (with no particle detection ever): a continuous interference pattern, but no discrete landing points. What's actually observed: discrete landing points that, in aggregate, reproduce the interference pattern — genuinely both at once.
