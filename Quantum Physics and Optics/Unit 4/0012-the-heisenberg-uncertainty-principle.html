The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

Wave-particle duality has a mathematical price tag: the more precisely you pin down a quantum object's position, the less precisely its momentum can be known — and this isn't a limitation of our instruments.

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

The Uncertainty Principle Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2, where ħ = h/2π ≈ 1.055×10⁻³⁴ J·s, and Δx, Δp are the spreads (standard deviations) in position and momentum found across repeated measurements on identically prepared quantum systems. Confining Δx tightly forces Δp to be large, and vice versa — the product of the two can never be squeezed below ħ/2.
Why this isn't just "our measurement disturbs the particle" A common intuitive story: to see an electron, you need to bounce a photon off it, and that necessarily kicks the electron, disturbing its momentum. That story isn't wrong, but it undersells the point. The deeper truth is that a quantum particle's wavefunction genuinely does not possess a simultaneously well-defined position and momentum — it isn't that we can't know both, it's that both values, precisely and at once, are not there to know. A wave packet localized tightly in space is unavoidably built from a broad range of wavelengths (Unit 6 makes this concrete), and via de Broglie's λ = h/p, a broad range of wavelengths means a broad range of momenta. This is genuinely a property of waves in general — even a very sharp, localized pulse on a classical guitar string is a superposition of many frequencies. Quantum mechanics inherits this same mathematical fact and attaches physical meaning to it.
Example: minimum momentum uncertainty An electron is confined to Δx = 1×10⁻¹⁰ m (roughly one atom's width). Δp ≥ ħ/(2Δx) = (1.055×10⁻³⁴)/(2×1×10⁻¹⁰) ≈ 5.27×10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s — this is not a measurement limitation, but a hard floor on how precisely position and momentum can simultaneously be defined.

1. What does the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2, actually claim?

The principle is about what values are simultaneously well-defined, not just a limit on measurement technology.

2. If a particle's position is confined more and more tightly (Δx shrinks), what must happen to its momentum spread Δp?

Since Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 is a lower bound on the product, shrinking Δx forces Δp to grow to compensate.

3. Why does a sharply localized wave packet necessarily contain a broad range of wavelengths?

This is true of waves generally, including classical ones like a plucked guitar string's pulse — quantum mechanics inherits this mathematical fact and, via de Broglie, connects it to momentum uncertainty.

4. Fill in the blank: an electron confined to a very small region of space necessarily has a large uncertainty in its ___, according to the uncertainty principle.

Confining Δx forces Δp (momentum uncertainty) to grow, keeping Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2.
