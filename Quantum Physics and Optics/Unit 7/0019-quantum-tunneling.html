Quantum Tunneling

Classically, a ball without enough energy to clear a hill simply can't get over it — full stop. Quantum mechanically, there's a small but real chance a particle passes straight through a barrier it doesn't have the energy to climb.

← Unit 6, Lesson 18 Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

Tunneling Through a Barrier For a particle with total energy E less than a barrier's potential energy height V₀ (E < V₀), classical physics forbids it from ever being found inside or beyond the barrier. Solving the Schrödinger equation there instead gives a wavefunction that decays exponentially inside the barrier — not abruptly to zero — and reconnects to a smaller-amplitude traveling wave beyond it. The transmission probability is approximately T ~ e−2κL, where κ = √(2m(V₀ − E))/ħ and L is the barrier's width.
Why tunneling drops off so fast with width and height Since T depends exponentially on κL, even modest increases in barrier width or in "how impossible" the barrier is (V₀ − E) suppress tunneling extremely quickly. That's why tunneling is only ever practically observable for very thin barriers and very light particles like electrons. A bowling ball never visibly tunnels through a wall — not because the rule stops applying to it, but because for something that massive and a barrier that thick, the tunneling probability is fantastically, unmeasurably small (the same "unmeasurably small" reasoning already used for macroscopic de Broglie wavelengths in Unit 4).
Example: a real application, previewed A scanning tunneling microscope (STM) exploits exactly this exponential sensitivity: the tunneling current between a sharp metal tip and a surface changes enormously for sub-nanometer changes in tip-surface distance, giving atomic-resolution imaging (Lesson 20 covers this and other applications in full).

1. Classically, a particle with energy E less than a barrier's height V₀ can never be found beyond the barrier. What does quantum mechanics predict instead?

The Schrödinger equation gives an exponentially decaying (but nonzero) wavefunction inside the barrier, reconnecting to a transmitted wave beyond it — a real, if usually small, transmission probability.

2. Why is quantum tunneling never observed for everyday macroscopic objects, like a ball rolling toward a hill?

The exponential suppression of T with mass and barrier size means the rule still applies, but the probability is astronomically small for macroscopic objects.

3. If a barrier's width is doubled while its height stays the same, the tunneling transmission probability T:

Since T ~ e^(−2\u03baL), doubling L drops T by a large exponential factor, not a simple linear amount.

4. Fill in the blank: the phenomenon where a quantum particle has a nonzero probability of passing through a barrier it classically shouldn't have enough energy to cross is called ___.

Quantum tunneling is this nonzero transmission probability through a classically forbidden barrier.
