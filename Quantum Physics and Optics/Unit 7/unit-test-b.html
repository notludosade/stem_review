1. Quantum mechanically, a particle with energy E less than a barrier's height V₀ (E < V₀) has:

Quantum tunneling gives a small but genuinely nonzero transmission probability, unlike the strict classical prohibition.

2. Why is quantum tunneling never observed for everyday macroscopic objects, like a bowling ball rolling toward a wall?

The rule still applies to massive objects, but the exponential suppression of T with mass makes the effect fantastically, unmeasurably small.

3. An electron microscope achieves far higher resolution than a light microscope mainly because:

Since resolution is limited by wavelength, and electrons' de Broglie wavelength is far shorter than visible light's, electron microscopes resolve much finer detail.

4. Fill in the blank: the "T" in STM (scanning tunneling microscope) stands for the same quantum effect covered earlier in this unit: quantum ___.

STM imaging is directly named after the tunneling effect covered in this unit's first lesson.

5. Which idea finally explained why atomic energy levels are quantized, rather than simply assuming it as Bohr had to?

Quantization becomes a derived consequence of boundary conditions on the wavefunction, once the Schrödinger equation is in hand, rather than a bare postulate.

6. The approximate tunneling transmission probability is T ~ e^(−2κL), where κ = √(2m(V₀ − E))/ħ. Answer both parts:

(a) If the particle's mass m is increased while everything else stays fixed, what happens to κ?

(b) Fill in the blank: a larger κ makes the tunneling probability T ___ (larger/smaller).

(a) κ ∝ √m, so increasing mass increases κ. (b) A larger κ makes the exponent more negative, sharply decreasing T — exactly why heavier particles tunnel far less than electrons.

7. Different quantum technologies rely on different specific effects from this course. Answer both parts:

(a) A scanning tunneling microscope's imaging technique is most directly based on:

(b) Fill in the blank: LEDs and lasers both rely on quantized atomic or semiconductor energy ___, the same core idea introduced with the Bohr model.

(a) STM imaging is built directly on Lesson 19's tunneling current. (b) Quantized energy levels, first introduced via the Bohr model, underlie both LED and laser operation.

8. Trace the course's overall arc from Unit 1 through Unit 7. Answer both parts:

(a) What resolved the atomic-spectra puzzle (Unit 5) only as an assumption, not a derivation?

(b) Fill in the blank: it took the ___ equation (Unit 6) to finally derive quantization from boundary conditions, rather than assume it.

(a) Bohr's model only assumed quantization by postulate. (b) The Schrödinger equation later supplied a genuine derivation, closing the gap Bohr's model left open.

9. A tunneling barrier's width is decreased while its height stays fixed. Answer both parts:

(a) What happens to the transmission probability T?

(b) Fill in the blank: this same exponential sensitivity to width is exactly what gives a scanning tunneling microscope its atomic-scale ___.

(a) A thinner barrier means less exponential suppression, so T increases. (b) This same sensitivity is exploited by STMs to resolve individual atoms.

10. Semiconductors and MRI machines both depend on quantum effects, though different ones. Answer both parts:

(a) Semiconductors' ability to be tuned between conducting and insulating relies most directly on:

(b) Fill in the blank: MRI machines detect quantized nuclear ___ states flipping in a strong magnetic field.

(a) Semiconductor behavior is governed by quantized energy bands, an extension of the particle-in-a-box's discrete-levels idea to solids. (b) MRI relies on quantized nuclear spin states, one more example of quantization beyond atomic energy levels.
