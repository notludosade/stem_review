1. A classical (planetary) model of the atom predicts that an orbiting electron should:

Classical electromagnetism requires an accelerating (orbiting) charge to radiate continuously, predicting rapid collapse — directly contradicted by the existence of stable atoms.

2. An electron in hydrogen drops from n = 3 (E = −1.51 eV) to n = 1 (E = −13.6 eV). What is the emitted photon's energy?

E_photon = E_high − E_low = −1.51 − (−13.6) = 12.09 eV.

3. Does the Bohr model correctly predict the spectral lines of neutral helium (2 electrons)?

The Bohr model's success is essentially limited to hydrogen (and hydrogen-like one-electron ions) — it fails once electron-electron interactions matter.

4. Fill in the blank: the set of discrete wavelengths an element emits or absorbs, unique to that element, is called its atomic ___.

Each element's atomic spectrum is a unique fingerprint of discrete wavelengths, corresponding to transitions between its quantized energy levels.

5. Bohr's model treats the electron as traveling along a definite circular orbit at every instant. Why does this conflict with the Heisenberg uncertainty principle?

Tracing an exact orbital path moment to moment requires exact position and momentum together — precisely what Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 rules out.

6. Using Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n² for hydrogen. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the energy of the n = 4 level is E₄ = ___ eV (round to 2 decimal places).

(b) An electron absorbs a photon and jumps from n = 1 to n = 4. The photon's energy is:

(a) E₄ = −13.6/16 = −0.85 eV. (b) E_photon = E₄ − E₁ = −0.85 − (−13.6) = 12.75 eV.

7. A gas is heated until it glows, and its light is passed through a prism. Answer both parts:

(a) What is observed, instead of a smooth, continuous rainbow?

(b) Fill in the blank: this discrete pattern is direct evidence that atomic energy levels are ___.

(a)-(b) A discrete line spectrum is the direct experimental signature of quantized atomic energy levels — only specific transition energies (and hence photon frequencies) are possible.

8. Consider what the Bohr model gets right versus wrong. Answer both parts:

(a) Which of these is a genuine success of the Bohr model?

(b) Which of these is a genuine limitation of the Bohr model?

(a) Correctly predicting hydrogen's spectrum is a genuine, falsifiable success. (b) The model's central weakness is postulating — not deriving — quantization; Unit 6's Schrödinger equation later supplies the derivation.

9. Using Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n² for hydrogen. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the energy needed to fully ionize a hydrogen atom starting from its ground state (n = 1) is ___ eV.

(b) This ionization energy corresponds to exciting the electron from n = 1 to:

(a) Ionization means removing the electron entirely, raising it from E₁ = −13.6 eV to E∞ = 0 eV, requiring 13.6 eV. (b) n = ∞ represents a free (unbound) electron, where E = 0.

10. Is the Bohr model the final, correct picture of the atom? Answer both parts:

(a) What does full quantum mechanics (Unit 6) replace the Bohr model's "definite orbit" picture with?

(b) Fill in the blank: the one core Bohr-model idea that does survive into full quantum mechanics is energy level ___.

(a) Full quantum mechanics replaces definite orbits with a probabilistic wavefunction description. (b) Quantized energy levels remain a core feature of the full quantum treatment, even though the orbit picture itself does not survive.
