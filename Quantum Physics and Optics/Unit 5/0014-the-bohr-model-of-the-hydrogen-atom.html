The Bohr Model of the Hydrogen Atom

Bohr's fix: let the electron orbit only at certain special radii, where — for reasons he simply postulated rather than derived — it doesn't radiate at all. One assumption, and the spectral-line puzzle finally had a testable explanation.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Bohr's Postulates (1) An electron orbits the nucleus only in specific allowed circular orbits, called stationary states, without radiating energy. (2) Angular momentum is quantized: L = nħ, n = 1, 2, 3, …. (3) A photon is emitted or absorbed only when an electron jumps between allowed orbits, with photon energy equal to the energy difference between them. For hydrogen, this gives quantized energy levels Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n².
Why this successfully explained the spectral lines Quantized energy levels mean only specific transition energies — and therefore only specific photon frequencies — are possible, which naturally produces a discrete line spectrum instead of a continuous smear. Even more strikingly, Bohr's model correctly reproduced the empirical Rydberg constant R from first principles, purely from fundamental constants — a genuine, falsifiable theoretical success, not a coincidence. The honest caveat (expanded next lesson): Bohr had to simply assume the "no-radiation, stationary-state" rule by hand, without deriving why an orbiting charge would stop radiating.
Example: the n = 3 → n = 2 transition E₃ = −13.6/9 ≈ −1.51 eV. E₂ = −13.6/4 = −3.40 eV. E_photon = E₃ − E₂ = −1.51 − (−3.40) = 1.89 eV. λ = hc/E ≈ (1240 eV·nm)/(1.89 eV) ≈ 656 nm — matching the observed red hydrogen line exactly.

1. In the Bohr model, a photon is emitted or absorbed by a hydrogen atom when:

Photon emission/absorption happens only at a transition between two of the model's quantized stationary states, with photon energy equal to the energy difference.

2. An electron in hydrogen drops from n = 4 (E = −0.85 eV) to n = 1 (E = −13.6 eV). What is the emitted photon's energy?

E_photon = E_high − E_low = −0.85 − (−13.6) = 12.75 eV (photon energies are always reported as positive magnitudes).

3. Why was correctly reproducing the empirical Rydberg constant R from Bohr's postulates considered a genuine theoretical success, not just a lucky coincidence?

Deriving an already-known empirical constant from independent fundamental constants, with no fitting involved, is exactly what makes a theory falsifiable and convincing.

4. Fill in the blank: Bohr postulated that an electron can orbit only in specific allowed orbits, called ___, without radiating any energy.

Stationary states are Bohr's postulated non-radiating orbits — an assumption, not a derivation (Lesson 15 examines this limitation).
