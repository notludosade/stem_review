1. This course's treatment of the Schrödinger equation is best described as:

The equation is presented qualitatively, with simple, fully solvable cases worked through explicitly.

2. An electron is confined to a 2 nm box. Using Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²), what is its ground-state (n = 1) energy (h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg)?

E₁ = (1)²(6.626×10⁻³⁴)²/(8×9.11×10⁻³¹×(2×10⁻⁹)²) ≈ 1.51×10⁻²⁰ J — one-fourth the 1 nm box's energy, since E ∝ 1/L².

3. Why must ∫|Ψ|² dx = 1 when integrated over all space?

Total probability across all possible locations must sum to exactly 1, since the particle is somewhere — this is the normalization condition.

4. Fill in the blank: the integer n = 1, 2, 3, … that labels each allowed energy level and standing-wave shape in the particle-in-a-box problem is called the ___ number.

n is the quantum number; each value labels one allowed standing-wave solution and its corresponding energy.

5. Once V(x) is specified for a given physical situation and the Schrödinger equation is solved, what does the resulting Ψ determine about the system's future behavior?

Solving the Schrödinger equation gives probabilistic predictions via |Ψ|², not a definite classical trajectory.

6. An electron is confined to a 1 nm box. Answer both parts:

(a) Compared to the ground state (n = 1), the n = 3 energy level is:

(b) Fill in the blank: the wavefunction Ψₙ(x) has exactly ___ interior nodes for n = 4.

(a) Since Eₙ ∝ n², E₃ = 9E₁. (b) Ψₙ(x) has n − 1 interior nodes; for n = 4, that's 3.

7. For the particle-in-a-box first excited state (n = 2), |Ψ₂(x)|² has one interior node at the box's center. Answer both parts:

(a) At exactly that center node, the probability of finding the particle is:

(b) Fill in the blank: |Ψ(x)|² is always real and non-negative, even though Ψ itself can be ___ or negative.

(a) At a node, |Ψ|² = 0 exactly — zero probability of finding the particle there. (b) Ψ can be complex-valued or negative; |Ψ|² is always real and non-negative, which is what makes it a valid probability density.

8. Compare the classical wave equation, ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²)∂²y/∂t², to the Schrödinger equation. Answer both parts:

(a) A classical wave's y(x,t) represents a real, physical displacement. What does Ψ(x,t) represent by itself?

(b) Fill in the blank: the Schrödinger equation is first-order in time, while the classical wave equation is second-order in ___.

(a) Unlike a classical wave's y(x,t), Ψ has no direct physical meaning by itself — only |Ψ|² is measurable. (b) The classical wave equation involves ∂²y/∂t² (second-order in time); the Schrödinger equation involves only ∂Ψ/∂t (first-order).

9. Using Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²) for an electron in a 1 nm box (E₁ ≈ 0.376 eV). Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: E₂ = 4 × E₁ ≈ ___ eV (round to 2 decimal places).

(b) If the box width L were cut in half instead, the ground-state energy E₁ would become:

(a) E₂ = 4 × 0.376 ≈ 1.50 eV. (b) Since Eₙ ∝ 1/L², halving L multiplies Eₙ by 4.

10. A measurement simulator samples many particle positions according to |Ψₙ(x)|². Answer both parts:

(a) As more and more measurements are simulated, the resulting histogram should increasingly match:

(b) Fill in the blank: this convergence, from individual random outcomes to a predictable overall distribution, is exactly what a ___ theory (as opposed to a strictly deterministic one) predicts.

(a)-(b) Individual measurement outcomes are random, but their aggregate distribution converges to the predicted |Ψₙ(x)|² — the hallmark of a genuinely probabilistic theory.
