From Classical Waves to a Quantum Wave Equation

Every classical wave — water, sound, a plucked string — obeys its own wave equation. If matter really has wave properties (de Broglie, Unit 4), matter waves need one too. Schrödinger wrote it down in 1926.

← Unit 5, Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

A Quantum Wave Equation A classical mechanical wave obeys ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²)∂²y/∂t². The (time-dependent) Schrödinger equation plays the analogous role for a quantum wavefunction Ψ(x,t): iħ ∂Ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m)∂²Ψ/∂x² + V(x)Ψ. This course presents this equation qualitatively, and solves it only for simple cases — starting with the particle in a box, next lesson — not in full generality.
Why this is a genuinely new move, not an incremental tweak The imaginary unit i appearing here isn't a bookkeeping convenience, the way it sometimes is in classical AC circuit analysis — it means Ψ itself is a complex-valued function with no direct physical meaning as a literal displacement, the way a water wave's height is. Only via the Born rule (|Ψ|², Lesson 18) does Ψ connect to anything actually measurable. Once V(x) is specified for a given physical situation, solving this equation determines everything knowable about that system's future behavior — but only in a probabilistic sense, never a definite trajectory the way Newton's laws would give.
Example: connecting back to de Broglie For a free particle (V = 0), the Schrödinger equation is solved by a traveling wave Ψ(x,t) = Ae^(i(kx−ωt)), with the de Broglie relations p = ħk and E = ħω plugged directly in — the same matter-wave idea from Unit 4, now the actual solution to an actual equation.
