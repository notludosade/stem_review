Unit 1: Wave Optics Fundamentals
Lesson 1

Light as a Wave — A Quick Review

This course picks up right where AP Physics C: Electricity and Magnetism left off — light as an electromagnetic wave. Before the leap into quantum mechanics later in this course, that wave picture needs to be second nature.

 Lesson 2

Huygens' Principle

Every point on a wavefront can be treated as its own tiny source of wavelets — a simple picture, over two centuries old, that predicts straight-line propagation, reflection, refraction, and diffraction from one geometric rule.

 Lesson 3

Reflection and Refraction Revisited via Wave Fronts

The law of reflection and Snell's law aren't separate rules to memorize — both fall directly out of Huygens' wavelets once a wavefront meets a boundary at an angle.

Unit 2: Interference & Diffraction
Lesson 1

Young's Double-Slit Experiment

Two slits, one light source, and a screen — this single 1801 experiment gave the first undeniable proof that light interferes with itself, exactly the way water waves do.

 Lesson 2

Thin-Film Interference

A soap bubble's swirling colors and an anti-reflective camera lens coating both come from the same interference effect — light bouncing off two very close surfaces instead of two separated slits.

 Lesson 3

Single-Slit Diffraction and Diffraction Gratings

A single narrow opening spreads light into its own pattern of bright and dark bands — and scaling from one slit to thousands turns a blurry pattern into razor-sharp spectral lines.

Unit 3: The Photoelectric Effect & Photons
Lesson 1

The Photoelectric Effect and Its Puzzle

Shine light on a metal and sometimes electrons pop out — but the exact pattern of when they do, and don't, makes no sense under classical wave theory.

 Lesson 2

Einstein's Photon Explanation

Einstein's fix wasn't a tweak to wave theory — it was to say light itself arrives in discrete particle-like chunks, energy E = hf, each interacting with exactly one electron at a time.

 Lesson 3

Photon Momentum and the Compton Effect

If light really is made of particle-like photons, those photons should carry momentum too — and colliding them with electrons, billiard-ball style, is exactly what finally proved it.

Unit 4: Wave-Particle Duality
Lesson 1

De Broglie's Matter Waves

If light, long assumed a pure wave, secretly behaves like particles, de Broglie asked the mirror-image question in 1924: what if matter, long assumed pure particles, secretly behaves like waves too?

 Lesson 2

The Double-Slit Experiment with Particles

Fire electrons through a double slit one at a time, and something strange happens: each lands as a single dot, yet over time the dots build up the exact same interference pattern light makes.

 Lesson 3

The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

Wave-particle duality has a mathematical price tag: the more precisely you pin down a quantum object's position, the less precisely its momentum can be known — and this isn't a limitation of our instruments.

Unit 5: The Bohr Model & Atomic Spectra
Lesson 1

Atomic Spectra and the Puzzle They Posed

Heat a gas until it glows and look at its light through a prism: instead of a smooth rainbow, you get a handful of sharp, isolated colored lines — a fingerprint no classical model of the atom could explain.

 Lesson 2

The Bohr Model of the Hydrogen Atom

Bohr's fix: let the electron orbit only at certain special radii, where — for reasons he simply postulated rather than derived — it doesn't radiate at all. One assumption, and the spectral-line puzzle finally had a testable explanation.

 Lesson 3

Where the Bohr Model Succeeds and Where It Fails

Bohr's model nailed hydrogen's spectrum almost perfectly — and then failed almost completely for every other atom on the periodic table. Both facts matter for what comes next.

Unit 6: The Schrödinger Equation
Lesson 1

From Classical Waves to a Quantum Wave Equation

Every classical wave — water, sound, a plucked string — obeys its own wave equation. If matter really has wave properties (de Broglie, Unit 4), matter waves need one too. Schrödinger wrote it down in 1926.

 Lesson 2

The Particle in a Box

One genuinely solvable case, worked all the way through: trap a particle between two impenetrable walls, and the Schrödinger equation forces its energy into a clean, quantized ladder.

 Lesson 3

Probability Densities and What the Wavefunction Means

Ψ itself isn't directly measurable — it's |Ψ|² that tells you something you can actually check against experiment: the probability of finding the particle at a given location.

Unit 7: Quantum Mechanics Applications & Synthesis
Lesson 1

Quantum Tunneling

Classically, a ball without enough energy to clear a hill simply can't get over it — full stop. Quantum mechanically, there's a small but real chance a particle passes straight through a barrier it doesn't have the energy to climb.

 Lesson 2

Where Quantum Mechanics Shows Up in Modern Technology

None of this stayed confined to physics classrooms — quantized energy levels, tunneling, and wave-particle duality are the working principles behind lasers, semiconductors, MRI machines, and the transistors in every phone.

 Lesson 3

From Classical Optics to Quantum Mechanics — The Whole Arc

Twenty lessons ago, this course started with light as a simple wave bouncing off a mirror. It ends with a wavefunction whose only physical meaning is a probability. Trace the whole chain, one link at a time.
