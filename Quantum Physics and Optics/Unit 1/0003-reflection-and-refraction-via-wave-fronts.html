Reflection and Refraction Revisited via Wave Fronts

The law of reflection and Snell's law aren't separate rules to memorize — both fall directly out of Huygens' wavelets once a wavefront meets a boundary at an angle.

← Lesson 2 Unit 2, Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Refraction, in Wave Terms When a wavefront crosses into a medium where it travels slower, the part of the wavefront that arrives first starts slowing down while the rest is still moving at the old speed — pivoting the wavefront's direction. The result is Snell's law: n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂, where n = c/v is a medium's index of refraction.
Why the wavefront bends toward the normal in a denser medium Picture a wavefront hitting a glass surface at an angle: one edge of the wavefront reaches the glass and slows down while the opposite edge is still in air, still moving at full speed. That edge outruns the slowed edge over the same time interval, swinging the wavefront's direction of travel toward the normal — exactly the bending Snell's law predicts, derived here from wave speed alone, with no separate "bending rule" needed. The law of reflection (θᵢ = θᵣ) falls out of the identical construction applied to the reflected wavelets, which never change speed at all.
Example: finding the refraction angle Light in air (n₁ = 1.00) strikes glass (n₂ = 1.50) at θ₁ = 30°. sinθ₂ = (n₁/n₂) sinθ₁ = (1.00/1.50)(0.50) = 0.333, so θ₂ = sin⁻¹(0.333) ≈ 19.5° — bent toward the normal, as expected entering a denser medium.

1. Using Huygens' wavelets, why does a wavefront bend toward the normal when entering a medium with a higher index of refraction?

The mismatch in speed across the still-crossing wavefront is exactly what pivots its direction — the wave picture derives Snell's law rather than just stating it.

2. Light in air (n = 1.00) strikes a flat piece of glass (n = 1.50) at θ₁ = 40°. What is the refraction angle?

sinθ₂ = (1.00/1.50) sin 40° = (0.667)(0.643) ≈ 0.429, so θ₂ ≈ 25.4°.

3. Fill in the blank: the bending of a wave's direction of travel as it passes from one medium into another, caused by a change in wave speed, is called ___.

This is refraction, governed quantitatively by Snell's law.

4. The law of reflection (θᵢ = θᵣ) can be derived from the same Huygens wavelet construction used for refraction. What's different about the reflected wavelets that keeps the angle equal instead of bending it?

Refraction's bending comes specifically from a speed mismatch as the wavefront crosses into a new medium; reflected wavelets stay in the same medium at the same speed, so no such mismatch — and no bending — occurs.
