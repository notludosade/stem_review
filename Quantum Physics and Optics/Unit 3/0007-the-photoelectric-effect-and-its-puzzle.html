The Photoelectric Effect and Its Puzzle

Shine light on a metal and sometimes electrons pop out — but the exact pattern of when they do, and don't, makes no sense under classical wave theory.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

The Photoelectric Effect: What Is Observed Light striking certain metals ejects electrons (photoelectrons). Four facts about this, measured carefully around 1900: (1) below a metal-specific threshold frequency f₀, no electrons are ejected, no matter how intense the light; (2) above f₀, electrons are ejected instantly, with no measurable delay even at very low intensity; (3) above f₀, increasing intensity increases the number of electrons ejected per second, not their energy; (4) increasing frequency above f₀ increases the electrons' maximum kinetic energy.
Why brighter light alone can never eject electrons below the threshold frequency Classical wave theory says a wave's energy depends on its amplitude (intensity), delivered continuously and absorbable in any amount — so a bright enough light source, given enough time to deliver energy, should eventually free an electron at any frequency, and a dim light source should show a measurable delay while energy accumulates. Neither prediction survives contact with experiment: dim light above f₀ ejects electrons instantly, and intense light below f₀ ejects none at all, no matter how long you wait or how bright the source. A common misconception is that brighter light should eventually do the job regardless of color — it does not. Frequency, not brightness, decides whether any electrons come out at all.
Example: the puzzle, concretely A weak beam of ultraviolet light (above a given metal's f₀) ejects electrons from it immediately. A floodlight's worth of red light (below that same f₀), left shining on the same metal for hours, ejects nothing. Classical wave theory has no explanation for why color, not brightness, is what matters.

1. Below a metal's threshold frequency, increasing the intensity (brightness) of the light:

No amount of intensity below the threshold frequency ejects electrons — this is the single fact classical wave theory cannot explain.

2. What does classical wave theory predict should happen with very dim light, just above the threshold frequency, that experiments do not show?

Classical theory predicts a delay for dim light as energy slowly accumulates; experiments show emission is instantaneous instead, at any intensity above threshold.

3. Above the threshold frequency, increasing light intensity mainly changes:

Intensity controls how many electrons are ejected per second; frequency (Lesson 8) controls each electron's maximum kinetic energy.

4. Fill in the blank: the minimum light frequency below which a given metal ejects no photoelectrons at all, regardless of intensity, is called its ___.

Every metal has its own threshold frequency f₀, set by how tightly it holds its electrons.
