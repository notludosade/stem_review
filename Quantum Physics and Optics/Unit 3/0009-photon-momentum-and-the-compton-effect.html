Photon Momentum and the Compton Effect

If light really is made of particle-like photons, those photons should carry momentum too — and colliding them with electrons, billiard-ball style, is exactly what finally proved it.

← Lesson 8 Unit 4, Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

Photon Momentum A photon carries momentum p = h/λ = E/c, alongside its energy E = hf. In Compton scattering, an X-ray photon collides with a nearly-free electron, and the photon's wavelength shifts: Δλ = λ′ − λ = (h/(mec))(1 − cosθ), where θ is the scattering angle and h/(mec) ≈ 2.43×10⁻¹² m is the Compton wavelength.
Why a measurable wavelength shift proves photons carry real momentum A purely classical wave picture predicts scattered light keeps the same frequency (and wavelength) as the incident light — a wave sloshing an electron shouldn't change its own color. Compton instead measured scattered X-rays shifted to longer wavelengths (lower energy) by an amount that matches treating the collision as two particles conserving both energy and momentum, exactly like a billiard-ball collision. The photon loses momentum and energy to the electron it strikes, precisely as a particle collision requires — direct proof that photons are not just energy packets but momentum-carrying particles.
Example: wavelength shift at 90° Δλ = (2.43×10⁻¹²)(1 − cos90°) = (2.43×10⁻¹²)(1 − 0) = 2.43×10⁻¹² m ≈ 2.43 pm — a small but very measurable shift for X-ray wavelengths.

1. What does the Compton effect demonstrate that the photoelectric effect alone does not?

The photoelectric effect showed energy comes in packets; Compton scattering additionally showed those packets carry momentum, obeying ordinary collision physics.

2. A classical (non-particle) wave picture of light scattering off an electron predicts the scattered wave's frequency should:

Classically, a wave sloshing a charge back and forth re-radiates at the same frequency — the measured shift to a lower frequency (longer wavelength) is what the classical picture can't explain.

3. An X-ray photon Compton-scatters at θ = 60°. What is the wavelength shift Δλ?

Δλ = (2.43×10⁻¹²)(1 − cos60°) = (2.43×10⁻¹²)(1 − 0.5) = 1.22×10⁻¹² m.

4. Fill in the blank: a photon's p = h/λ is its ___, the same conserved quantity billiard balls exchange in a collision.

Photon momentum p = h/λ = E/c is what Compton scattering shows is conserved, just like ordinary momentum.
