Single-Slit Diffraction and Diffraction Gratings

A single narrow opening spreads light into its own pattern of bright and dark bands — and scaling from one slit to thousands turns a blurry pattern into razor-sharp spectral lines.

← Lesson 5 Unit 3, Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Single-Slit Diffraction A single slit of width a produces a central bright maximum flanked by much dimmer side bands. Dark fringes (not bright ones, unlike the double slit) occur at a sinθ = mλ (m = ±1, ±2, …) — different wavelets from across the same slit's width interfere with each other.
Diffraction Gratings A grating is a surface with many closely spaced slits. It produces bright maxima at the same-looking condition as the double slit, d sinθ = mλ, but with N slits interfering instead of 2, the bright fringes become far narrower and brighter.
Why more slits make sharper lines With only two slits, nearby angles still interfere fairly constructively, smearing the bright fringe out. With thousands of slits, only a razor-thin range of angles has every single slit's wavelet arriving in phase simultaneously — any small deviation and enough of the many wavelets cancel that the total intensity collapses almost to zero. This is exactly what makes gratings useful in spectrometers: sharp lines let you measure wavelength precisely.
Example: first dark fringe, single slit A slit of width a = 0.02 mm is illuminated by λ = 600 nm light. sinθ = λ/a = (600×10⁻⁹)/(2×10⁻⁵) = 0.03 → θ ≈ 1.72°.
Example: grating first-order angle A grating has 500 lines/mm, so d = 1/500 mm = 2000 nm. For λ = 600 nm: sinθ = λ/d = 600/2000 = 0.3 → θ ≈ 17.5°.

1. Why do diffraction gratings produce much sharper bright fringes than a plain double slit, at the same slit spacing d?

With N slits, the bright-fringe condition becomes far more restrictive — small angular deviations cause most of the N wavelets to interfere destructively, sharply narrowing the peak.

2. A single slit of width 0.03 mm is illuminated with λ = 450 nm light. What angle gives the first dark fringe?

sinθ = λ/a = (450×10⁻⁹)/(3×10⁻⁵) = 0.015 → θ ≈ 0.86°.

3. Unlike the double-slit bright-fringe condition, the single-slit condition a sinθ = mλ locates:

For a single slit this condition gives destructive interference (dark fringes), the reverse of what the same-looking equation means for a double slit or grating.

5. Fill in the blank: a surface with many thousands of closely spaced slits, used to produce sharp spectral lines, is called a ___.

A diffraction grating scales the double slit's d sinθ = mλ condition up to many slits, producing much sharper maxima.
