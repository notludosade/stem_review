Young's Double-Slit Experiment

Two slits, one light source, and a screen — this single 1801 experiment gave the first undeniable proof that light interferes with itself, exactly the way water waves do.

← Unit 1, Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

The Setup Light passes through two narrow, closely spaced slits and lands on a screen some distance away. Each slit acts as a source of Huygens wavelets (Unit 1), and the two overlapping waves interfere on the screen, producing alternating bright and dark fringes.
The Double-Slit Equation Bright fringes (constructive interference) occur where d sinθ = mλ (m = 0, ±1, ±2, …), with d the slit separation, θ the angle from the center to the fringe, and m the order number. Dark fringes (destructive interference) occur at d sinθ = (m + 1/2)λ.
Why this experiment proved light is a wave If light were purely a stream of particles, passing it through two slits should produce just two bright bands directly behind each slit. Instead, the experiment shows many alternating bright and dark fringes — the unmistakable signature of interference, something only waves do. This result, decades before Maxwell's equations, was the first strong evidence that light is fundamentally a wave. (Unit 3 complicates this picture — but doesn't erase it.)
Example: finding wavelength from fringe angle Slits separated by d = 0.02 mm produce a first-order (m = 1) bright fringe at θ = 1.5°. λ = d sinθ/m = (0.02×10⁻³)(sin 1.5°)/1 ≈ (2×10⁻⁵)(0.0262) ≈ 5.24×10⁻⁷ m = 524 nm — visible green.

1. Bright fringes in the double-slit experiment occur where the path difference between the two slits equals:

d sinθ = mλ, a whole-number multiple of λ, gives constructive interference and a bright fringe.

2. Widening the slit spacing d, while keeping wavelength fixed, does what to the spacing between fringes on the screen?

Since d sinθ = mλ, a larger d means a smaller θ is needed to reach the same order m — fringes get closer together as d increases.

3. Slits separated by 0.01 mm produce a first-order bright fringe at θ = 3°. Find the wavelength.

λ = d sinθ/m = (1×10⁻⁵)(sin 3°)/1 ≈ (1×10⁻⁵)(0.0523) ≈ 5.23×10⁻⁷ m ≈ 524 nm.

4. Fill in the blank: a bright fringe forms where the two slits' waves arrive in phase, producing ___ interference.

In-phase arrival at a whole-number path difference produces constructive interference and a bright fringe.
