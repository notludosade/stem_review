Systems of Connected Objects

A string that doesn't stretch is a calculus statement in disguise: if the string's total length is constant, its second derivative says the two objects' accelerations are locked together.

← Lesson 5 Unit 3, Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Two blocks joined by a string, or two masses hanging on either end of a pulley (an Atwood machine), can't be treated as independent objects — a constraint ties their motions together. Calculus makes that constraint precise instead of assumed.

The Constraint Equation For an Atwood machine, if x₁ is how far mass 1 has descended and x₂ is how far mass 2 has descended, an inextensible string over an ideal pulley means x₁ + x₂ = L (a constant, the fixed total string length on the two sides — mass 1 descending is exactly mass 2 ascending). Differentiating twice: d²x₁/dt² + d²x₂/dt² = 0, so a₁ = −a₂ — equal in magnitude, opposite in sign, at every instant.
Why differentiating a constraint is the standard move "The string length doesn't change" is a statement about position. Physics 1 treated the resulting "accelerations are equal in magnitude" as a given rule to memorize. Here it's derived: differentiate a true statement about position, and it stays true about velocity and acceleration — precisely the logic behind AP Calc BC's related rates (Unit 4, Lesson 3), applied to a mechanics constraint instead of a geometry one.
Example: solving the Atwood machine Masses m₁ and m₂ (m₂ > m₁) hang over a frictionless, massless pulley. Newton's second law for each mass, with the constraint a₁ = a₂ = a built in from the start:
Mass 2 (descending): m₂g − T = m₂a
Mass 1 (ascending): T − m₁g = m₁a
Adding the two equations eliminates tension T: a = (m₂ − m₁)g / (m₁ + m₂), and substituting back gives T = m₁(g + a).

The same idea, applied to blocks on a table

When two blocks sit side by side and are pushed together, or connected by a string over a table's edge with a hanging mass, the same method applies: write ΣF = ma for each object separately (respecting which forces act on which object — Newton's third law pairs never appear in the same object's equation), substitute the shared constraint acceleration, and solve the resulting system of equations. The physics doesn't change from the Atwood machine above; only the geometry of which forces enter each equation does.

1. Why must the two masses in an Atwood machine have accelerations equal in magnitude?

The string's fixed total length, x₁ + x₂ = constant, differentiated twice with respect to time, directly forces the two accelerations to be equal in magnitude and opposite in sign.

2. In an Atwood machine with m₁ = 2 kg and m₂ = 6 kg (g = 9.8 m/s²), what is the magnitude of the system's acceleration?

a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂) = (6 − 2)(9.8)/8 = 39.2/8 = 4.9 m/s².

3. Why is it invalid to write T − m₁g = m₁a and also m₂g − T = m₂a using two different tensions T₁ and T₂ for an ideal Atwood machine?

An ideal (massless) string and frictionless, massless pulley transmit tension unchanged along their length, so one tension variable T correctly describes the whole system.

4. Fill in the blank: an equation like x₁ + x₂ = L, which ties two objects' positions together because of a physical connection like a string, is called a ___ equation.

A constraint equation captures a geometric restriction (here, fixed string length) that, once differentiated, links the objects' velocities and accelerations too.
