Solving Simple Equations of Motion

Constant force reproduces Physics 1's straight-line v(t). Drag force — a force that depends on velocity itself — needs separation of variables, and gives something Physics 1 could never reach: terminal velocity.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 4 showed that constant force gives a linear v(t) — familiar territory. This lesson introduces a force that depends on the object's own velocity: linear drag, Fdrag = −bv, opposing motion and growing stronger the faster the object moves.

Case 1: Constant Force m dv/dt = F₀ → dv/dt = F₀/m, a constant. Direct integration: v(t) = v₀ + (F₀/m)t — linear in time, no upper bound on speed.
Case 2: Linear Drag Alone m dv/dt = −bv. This is separable (AP Calc Unit 7, Lesson 4): dv/v = −(b/m) dt. Integrating both sides: ln|v| = −(b/m)t + C, so v(t) = v₀ e−(b/m)t — an exponential decay toward (but never exactly reaching) zero.
Case 3: Gravity Plus Drag (Falling with Air Resistance) m dv/dt = mg − bv (taking downward as positive, drag opposing the fall). Separating variables and integrating gives v(t) = vterm + (v₀ − vterm) e−(b/m)t, where vterm = mg/b is the terminal velocity — the speed at which dv/dt = 0, so gravity and drag exactly balance.
Why terminal velocity was invisible to Physics 1 Physics 1 could describe free fall only as constant acceleration g, forever increasing speed. A force that depends on velocity — like drag — makes the acceleration itself change as the object speeds up, which is exactly the kind of equation only a differential-equations approach can solve. As v → vterm, dv/dt → 0 and the object stops accelerating even though gravity is still pulling: the two forces have become equal in magnitude.

1. Which calculus technique solves m dv/dt = −bv for v(t)?

Rearranging to dv/v = −(b/m)dt separates the v-terms and t-terms onto opposite sides, letting each side be integrated independently — the defining move of separation of variables.

2. For an object falling with both gravity and linear drag, what is its terminal velocity?

Terminal velocity occurs when dv/dt = 0: mg − bv = 0 → v = mg/b.

3. As t → ∞, an object falling with drag alone (no other force) approaches v = 0 how?

v(t) = v₀e^(−(b/m)t) is an exponential decay — mathematically, e^x is never exactly zero for any finite x, so v approaches (but never mathematically reaches) zero.

4. Fill in the blank: solving m dv/dt = −bv by separation of variables produces an ___ function of time.

v(t) = v₀e^(−(b/m)t) is exponential decay — the hallmark solution shape whenever a quantity's rate of change is proportional to the quantity itself, the same pattern as AP Calc BC's exponential growth/decay models.
