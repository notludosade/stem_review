F = ma as a Differential Equation

Newton's second law looks like simple algebra until you remember that a is d²x/dt². Then it's an equation for an unknown function — exactly the differential equations AP Calc BC Unit 7 taught you to solve.

← Unit 1, Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

AP Physics 1 used ΣF = ma to solve for a number: plug in force and mass, get out an acceleration. That's still true here — but now a itself is d²x/dt², so ΣF = ma is secretly a statement about an unknown function x(t), not just a number.

Newton's Second Law as an ODE ΣF = m d²x/dt², or equivalently ΣF = m dv/dt. "Solving" a mechanics problem now means finding the function x(t) (or v(t)) that satisfies this differential equation, given the force(s) acting and a set of initial conditions.
Why two initial conditions, not one ΣF = m d²x/dt² is a second-order differential equation — it involves a second derivative. Just as antidifferentiating twice (Lesson 2) introduces two constants of integration, solving this equation for x(t) leaves two undetermined constants, which is why a complete kinematics problem always needs both an initial position x₀ and an initial velocity v₀ to pin down a unique solution. Physics 1 always handed you both without naming why they were both necessary — this is why.
Example: recovering Newton's first law from the ODE If ΣF = 0, the differential equation becomes m d²x/dt² = 0, so d²x/dt² = 0. Integrating once: dx/dt = v = constant. Integrating again: x(t) = x₀ + vt. The object moves at constant velocity — Newton's first law isn't a separate law here, it's the ΣF = 0 solution of the same ODE that governs everything else.
Example: constant force reproduces Physics 1's equations If ΣF = F₀ (a constant), then d²x/dt² = F₀/m — a constant, call it a. Integrating twice with initial conditions x(0) = x₀, v(0) = v₀ gives exactly v(t) = v₀ + at and x(t) = x₀ + v₀t + ½at², the familiar constant-acceleration kinematics. Nothing new is happening; it's the same ODE, just for the special case where the right-hand side doesn't depend on t, x, or v.

1. Why does solving ΣF = m d²x/dt² for x(t) require two initial conditions?

Each integration (from a to v, then v to x) introduces one arbitrary constant, so a second-order equation needs two known values — typically x₀ and v₀ — to fully determine the solution.

2. If ΣF = 0 for an object, what does the differential equation m d²x/dt² = 0 predict?

Integrating d²x/dt² = 0 once gives dx/dt = constant — this is Newton's first law, recovered as the ΣF = 0 solution of the same governing equation used everywhere else in the course.

3. A 4 kg object experiences a constant net force of 12 N, starting from rest at the origin. What is x(t)?

a = F/m = 12/4 = 3 m/s². With v₀ = 0, x₀ = 0: x(t) = ½at² = 1.5t².

4. Fill in the blank: ΣF = m d²x/dt² is called a ___ differential equation, since it involves a second derivative of the unknown function x(t).

The order of a differential equation is the highest derivative it contains — here, the second derivative of position.
