Work as an Integral of Force over Distance

W = Fd cos θ only works when force stays constant. The moment force changes with position — a spring, drag, gravity over a large distance — work becomes a definite integral.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Physics 1's W = Fd cos θ silently assumed F stayed the same for the whole displacement. A spring's restoring force, F = −kx, changes at every point along the stretch — so "force times distance" needs to become "force times distance, added up piece by piece," which is exactly what a definite integral is built to do.

Work as a Definite Integral For a force F(x) acting along the direction of motion over the interval x₁ to x₂: W = ∫x₁x₂ F(x) dx. When F is constant, this integral evaluates to exactly F(x₂ − x₁) = Fd — Physics 1's formula is the constant-force special case, not a different rule.
Why this is a Riemann sum in disguise Split the displacement into n tiny pieces of width Δx. Over each piece, F is approximately constant, so the work done is approximately F(xi)Δx — a thin rectangle's area. Summing all n rectangles and letting Δx → 0 is precisely the limit definition of ∫F(x)dx from AP Calc Unit 6, Lesson 2.
Example: stretching a spring A spring has spring constant k = 200 N/m. The external force needed to stretch it at position x (measuring x from natural length) is F(x) = kx (matching the spring's own restoring force in magnitude, opposite in direction). The work done stretching it from x = 0 to x = 0.1 m is W = ∫₀0.1 200x dx = 100x² |₀0.1 = 100(0.01) = 1 J.

1. A variable force F(x) = 3x² N acts on an object from x = 0 to x = 2 m. What is the work done?

W = ∫₀² 3x² dx = x³ |₀² = 8 J.

2. Why is W = Fd cos θ from Physics 1 considered a special case of W = ∫F(x) dx, rather than a separate rule?

∫F dx with constant F pulls F out of the integral, leaving F∫dx = F(x₂ − x₁) — exactly Fd for motion along the force's direction.

3. A spring with k = 150 N/m is stretched from its natural length to x = 0.2 m. How much work is done stretching it?

W = ∫₀^0.2 150x dx = 75x² |₀^0.2 = 75(0.04) = 3 J.

4. Fill in the blank: splitting a displacement into thin slices, treating force as constant over each slice, and adding up the resulting rectangular areas is exactly a ___ for the work integral.

This is precisely a Riemann sum — the same rectangle-approximation idea from AP Calc BC Unit 6, here applied to a force-vs-position graph instead of an arbitrary function.
