Impulse as an Integral of Force over Time

Unit 3 turned Fd into ∫F dx. This lesson runs the same idea along the time axis instead of the position axis: J = FΔt becomes ∫F dt.

← Unit 3, Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Real collisions rarely apply a truly constant force — a bat hitting a ball, an airbag decelerating a passenger, both apply a force that rises and falls over a fraction of a second. J = FΔt from Physics 1 assumed constant F; a force that changes with time needs the same integral treatment work got in Unit 3.

Impulse as a Definite Integral J = ∫t₁t₂ F(t) dt = Δp — the impulse-momentum theorem, generalized. Since F = dp/dt (Newton's second law in its original, more general form), impulse is simply the definite integral that undoes that derivative, recovering the total change in momentum over the interval.
Why F = dp/dt is the more fundamental form of the second law ΣF = ma assumes mass is constant, since d(mv)/dt = m dv/dt only when m doesn't change. Writing the second law as F = dp/dt instead is what actually holds in general — a fact this unit will lean on again in Lesson 12, when mass itself starts changing.
Example: a time-varying impact force A bat strikes a ball with force F(t) = 8000t − 40000t² N, for 0 ≤ t ≤ 0.2 s (rising, then falling back to zero — a realistic impact shape). The impulse delivered is J = ∫₀0.2 (8000t − 40000t²) dt = [4000t² − (40000/3)t³]₀0.2 = 4000(0.04) − 13333.3(0.008) = 160 − 106.7 ≈ 53.3 N·s.

1. A force F(t) = 100t N acts on an object from t = 0 to t = 0.5 s. What impulse does it deliver?

J = ∫₀^0.5 100t dt = 50t² |₀^0.5 = 50(0.25) = 12.5 N·s.

2. Why is F = dp/dt considered more general than ΣF = ma?

Since p = mv, d(mv)/dt = m(dv/dt) + v(dm/dt) by the product rule. Only when dm/dt = 0 does this reduce to m(dv/dt) = ma — the special case ΣF = ma actually describes.

3. The area under a force-vs-time graph over some interval represents:

Since F = dp/dt, the definite integral of F over time — the area under the F-t graph — gives the total change in momentum, Δp, i.e. the impulse.

4. Fill in the blank: writing Newton's second law as F = dp/dt uses ___ instead of acceleration as the quantity being differentiated.

F = dp/dt differentiates momentum p = mv directly, which is why it stays valid even when mass changes — unlike F = ma.
