Choosing Between Energy and Force Methods

Seven units built two complete toolkits — F = ma as a differential equation, and energy conservation. Neither is "more correct." The skill left to build is knowing which one to reach for first.

← Unit 7, Lesson 21 Lesson 23 → Contents Glossary →

When Energy Methods Win Reach for conservation of energy (Unit 3) when the problem asks for a speed or height at a specific position, the path is complicated but every force doing work is conservative, or intermediate details of the motion (time, exact trajectory) don't matter — energy methods skip straight from start to end.
When Force Methods Win Reach for ΣF = ma (Units 1–2) when the problem needs time-dependence — "how long until…", "what is x(t)…" — or a non-conservative force (like friction) is doing work whose exact amount isn't given directly, or an instantaneous force or acceleration at one specific point is what's being asked for.
Why energy methods "lose" time information Integrating F = ma to reach the work-energy theorem (Unit 3, Lesson 8) explicitly changed the variable of integration from x to v — trading away any direct record of t along the way. That trade is exactly what makes energy methods fast: they skip the intermediate motion entirely. But it also means they can never answer "how long did that take?" without falling back to a force/kinematics approach.
Example: same setup, two questions, two methods A block slides down a frictionless curved ramp of height h. "What is its speed at the bottom?" — energy conservation, one line: v = √(2gh), regardless of the ramp's shape. "How long does it take to reach the bottom?" — energy conservation has no time variable at all; this needs the force approach, integrating a(t) along the (possibly curved) path, which can be a genuinely hard integral depending on the ramp's shape.
Try It — Two Worked Approaches, Side by Side

Problem: A 2 kg block starts at rest at the top of a frictionless 5 m tall curved ramp. Find its speed at the bottom.

Energy approach: mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = √(2·9.8·5) = √98 ≈ 9.90 m/s. One line, and the ramp's exact curve never even entered the calculation.

Same problem, asking instead: how long does the block take to reach the bottom?

Force approach: requires knowing the ramp's shape y(x) to find the tangential component of gravity at every point, then integrating a(t) = g sinθ(t) along the path — genuinely harder, and unavoidable, because the question is fundamentally about time, which energy conservation has no way to track.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A problem asks for a block's speed after sliding down a frictionless hill of known height, with the hill's shape unspecified. Which method solves this fastest?

Since only gravity (conservative) does work, mgh = ½mv² gives the answer directly, with the hill's specific shape never entering the calculation.

2. Why can't energy conservation directly answer "how long does the motion take"?

The u-substitution behind W = ΔKE (Unit 3, Lesson 8) changes variables from x to v specifically because that eliminates the need to know t — which is exactly why the resulting theorem contains no t.

3. A problem involves a known, constant friction force acting over a known distance on an otherwise simple incline. Which approach handles this most directly?

Energy methods extend naturally to non-conservative forces as long as the work they do is known or computable (like a constant friction force over a known distance) — it just gets subtracted as a loss term.

4. Fill in the blank: a question that specifically asks "how long" or "at what instant" almost always calls for a force-based (differential equation) approach, since energy methods carry no direct information about ___.

The work-energy theorem's derivation eliminated t from the picture entirely — recovering time-dependence requires going back to F = ma.
