From Algebra-Based to Calculus-Based Mechanics — What Changed

Not one physical law changed across this entire course. What changed is that every formula AP Physics 1 asked you to trust, this course showed you how to build.

← Lesson 23 Contents Glossary →

Twenty-three lessons ago, this course opened by pointing out that AP Physics 1's four kinematic equations were really one idea — derivatives and integrals of position — wearing a disguise. That pattern repeated in every unit since. None of the physics from Physics 1 was wrong; all of it was a special case, true only when acceleration (or force, or torque) happened to be constant.

Why this mattered, beyond passing tests Every "given formula" from Physics 1 broke down the moment a real problem stopped matching its assumptions — constant force, constant acceleration, uniform mass distribution. Real forces are rarely constant: drag depends on velocity, spring forces depend on position, gravity depends on separation. Calculus is what turns "this formula works in the textbook's clean cases" into "this method works for whatever force you're actually given" — the entire point of building each tool from F = ma rather than memorizing its output.
Try It — Algebra Breaks, Calculus Doesn't

Problem: a 1 kg object falls from rest through air with drag force Fdrag = −0.5v. Find v(3 s).

Algebra-only attempt: v = v₀ + at needs a single, constant a — but here a = g − 0.5v/m changes continuously as v grows. There is no constant-acceleration formula that applies; Physics 1's toolkit has nothing more to offer.

Calculus-based solution (Unit 2, Lesson 5's method): separate variables in m dv/dt = mg − bv, integrate, and get v(t) = vterm(1 − e−(b/m)t) directly, with vterm = mg/b. Plug in numbers, done — no contradiction, no missing tool.

Looking Ahead AP Physics C: Electricity and Magnetism reuses every technique from this course — Gauss's law is an integral over a surface, circuits involve differential equations for charge and current, and magnetic forces get analyzed with the same vector calculus Unit 1 introduced. The derivative-and-integral fluency built here is the actual prerequisite, more than any specific mechanics formula.
