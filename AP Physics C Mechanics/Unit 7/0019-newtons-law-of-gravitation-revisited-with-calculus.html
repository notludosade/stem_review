Newton's Law of Gravitation Revisited with Calculus

Physics 1 used F = Gm₁m₂/r² as a magnitude-only formula. Treated as a function of r, it becomes a conservative force whose potential energy Unit 3's F = −dU/dx machinery can build directly.

← Unit 6, Lesson 18 Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

Gravitation as a Function of r With r̂ the unit vector pointing outward from the central mass M, the gravitational force on a mass m is F(r) = −(GMm/r²) r̂ — the minus sign showing the force is attractive, opposite to the outward direction r̂. As a function of the single variable r, this is exactly the kind of force Unit 3, Lesson 9 built potential energy functions for.
Why gravitation is conservative F(r) depends only on separation r, never on the path taken to get there or the angle of approach — the defining property of a conservative force from Unit 3. That guarantees a potential energy function U(r) exists satisfying F(r) = −dU/dr, which Lesson 21 builds explicitly.
Example: inverse-square scaling A satellite experiences gravitational force F₀ at orbital radius r₀. At radius 3r₀, the force is F₀/3² = F₀/9 — one-ninth as strong, since F ∝ 1/r². Doubling the distance always quarters the force; this scaling is the entire content of the "inverse-square" name.

1. If the distance between two masses triples, the gravitational force between them:

F ∝ 1/r², so tripling r divides F by 3² = 9.

2. Why does gravitation qualify as a conservative force?

Path-independence is exactly the test for a conservative force from Unit 3 — F(r) depending only on r guarantees it.

3. Two masses experience a gravitational force of 40 N at some separation r₀. What is the force at separation 2r₀?

Doubling r divides F by 2² = 4: 40/4 = 10 N.

4. Fill in the blank: F ∝ 1/r² is called an ___ dependence, since force falls off with the square of distance.

"Inverse-square" describes exactly this 1/r² scaling, shared by gravitation and (later, in AP Physics C: E&M) the electric force.
