Orbital Mechanics and Kepler's Laws

Kepler's second law — equal areas in equal times — isn't a separate empirical rule. It's Unit 5's conservation of angular momentum, applied to a force that always points straight at the center.

← Lesson 19 Lesson 21 → Contents Glossary →

Kepler's Three Laws 1. Orbits are ellipses, with the central body at one focus. 2. A line from the central body to the orbiting body sweeps out equal areas in equal times. 3. T² ∝ a³, where a is the orbit's semi-major axis.
Deriving Kepler's Second Law from Angular Momentum Gravity is a central force — it always points along r, straight at (or away from) the center — so the torque it exerts about the center is τ = r × F = 0, since r and F are parallel (or antiparallel). With zero net torque, angular momentum L = mr²(dθ/dt) is conserved (Unit 5, Lesson 15). The rate at which the radius sweeps area is dA/dt = ½r²(dθ/dt) = L/(2m) — constant, since L and m are both constant. Equal areas in equal times is nothing but conservation of angular momentum, read geometrically.
Kepler's Third Law, from a Circular Orbit For a circular orbit of radius r, gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r = m(2πr/T)²/r. Solving for T²: T² = (4π²/GM)r³ — the same law generalizes to elliptical orbits with r replaced by the semi-major axis a.

1. Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times) follows directly from:

Since gravity always points along r, τ = r×F = 0, so L = mr²(dθ/dt) is conserved — and dA/dt = L/(2m) being constant is exactly Kepler's second law.

2. Where does an orbiting body move fastest?

Since dA/dt is constant and area swept scales with both r and speed, a smaller r (near perihelion) requires a larger speed to sweep the same area in the same time.

3. A planet orbits at radius r₀ with period T₀. Another planet orbits the same star at radius 4r₀. What is its period?

T² ∝ r³, so (T/T₀)² = (4r₀/r₀)³ = 64 → T/T₀ = 8.

4. Fill in the blank: gravity is called a ___ force because it always points directly along the line connecting the two masses, producing zero torque about that line.

A central force is parallel to the position vector r, guaranteeing r × F = 0 and hence angular momentum conservation about that center.
