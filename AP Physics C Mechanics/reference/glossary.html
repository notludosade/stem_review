Unit 1: Kinematics with Calculus

Velocity, v(t) = dx/dt

The first derivative of position with respect to time — the instantaneous rate of change of position, exact at every single instant.

Acceleration, a(t) = dv/dt = d²x/dt²

The first derivative of velocity, equivalently the second derivative of position.

Antiderivative and initial condition

Recovering v(t) from a(t), or x(t) from v(t), means finding an antiderivative — a family of functions differing by a constant — then using a known value (like v₀ or x₀) to fix that constant.

Position vector, r(t) = x(t) î + y(t) ĵ

Bundles x(t) and y(t) into one vector-valued function. Differentiating or integrating r(t) is done component by component.

Speed vs. velocity

Velocity v(t) is a vector; speed is its magnitude, |v(t)| = √(v_x² + v_y²), always nonnegative.

Unit 2: Newton's Laws with Calculus

Newton's second law as an ODE, ΣF = m d²x/dt²

A second-order differential equation for the unknown function x(t), requiring two initial conditions (typically x₀ and v₀) to solve uniquely.

Linear drag, Fdrag = −bv

A resistive force proportional to velocity and opposing motion, with b the drag coefficient. Solved by separation of variables.

Terminal velocity, vterm = mg/b

The speed at which gravity and drag exactly balance (dv/dt = 0) for an object falling with linear drag.

Constraint equation

An equation (like x₁ + x₂ = L for a string of fixed length) tying two connected objects' positions together — differentiating it links their velocities and accelerations too.

Unit 3: Work, Energy & Power

Work as a definite integral, W = ∫x₁x₂ F(x) dx

Generalizes W = Fd cos θ to any force that varies with position; the constant-force formula is the special case where F comes out of the integral.

Work-energy theorem, derived

Wnet = ΔKE, obtained by substituting F = ma into the work integral and changing variables from x to v via a dx = v dv.

Conservative force and potential energy, F(x) = −dU/dx

A force whose work depends only on start and end position (never on path) has a potential energy function U(x) built by integrating −F; differentiating U recovers F.

Equilibrium point

A position where dU/dx = 0, so F = 0 — stable if U has a local minimum there, unstable if a local maximum.

Unit 4: Momentum & Collisions

Impulse as a definite integral, J = ∫t₁t₂ F(t) dt = Δp

Generalizes J = FΔt to any time-varying force, using the more general form of Newton's second law, F = dp/dt.

F = dp/dt

The more general form of Newton's second law, valid even when mass changes — reduces to ΣF = ma only when dm/dt = 0.

Momentum conservation, derived

Follows from Newton's third law, F₁₂(t) = −F₂₁(t), integrated over the interaction time to give Δp₁ = −Δp₂.

Center of mass, continuous form, xcm = (1/M) ∫x dm

Generalizes the discrete sum xcm = Σmixi/Σmi to a continuous mass distribution.

Rocket equation (qualitative), Δv = ve ln(m₀/mf)

A rocket's change in speed from ejecting mass at exhaust speed ve, arising from the product-rule expansion of dp/dt = d(mv)/dt when mass itself changes.

Unit 5: Rotation & Angular Momentum

Sign convention: counterclockwise positive

Angle, angular velocity, and torque are all positive in the counterclockwise direction throughout this course, matching the right-hand rule.

Moment of inertia via integration, I = ∫r² dm

Generalizes the discrete sum I = Σmiri² to a continuous mass distribution, weighting each mass element by the square of its distance from the axis.

Rotational kinematics: ω = dθ/dt, α = dω/dt

The exact rotational analogs of velocity and acceleration, obtained by the identical calculus applied to angular position θ(t).

τnet = Iα

The rotational form of Newton's second law — a second-order differential equation for θ(t), directly paralleling ΣF = ma.

Angular momentum and conservation, L = Iω, τnet = dL/dt

Conserved when net external torque is zero; expanding dL/dt with the product rule shows why ω changes when I changes (a spinning skater pulling her arms in).

Unit 6: Oscillations

SHM differential equation, d²x/dt² = −ω²x

Arises from combining Hooke's law F = −kx with Newton's second law, with ω² = k/m.

General SHM solution, x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ)

Two constants, amplitude A and phase φ, fixed by the two initial conditions x₀ and v₀. A plain sine is only correct for the specific case x(0) = 0.

Energy in SHM, E = ½kA²

Constant throughout the motion, proven by substituting x(t) and v(t) into KE + U and applying the identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1.

Maximum speed, vmax = Aω

The speed at equilibrium (x = 0), where all the oscillator's energy is kinetic.

Damping regimes: underdamped, critically damped, overdamped

Underdamped oscillates with a decaying exponential envelope; critically damped returns to equilibrium fastest without oscillating; overdamped returns more slowly, also without oscillating.

Resonance

A dramatic buildup in oscillation amplitude when a driving force's frequency is at or near the system's natural frequency.

Unit 7: Gravitation

Gravitation as a function of r, F(r) = −(GMm/r²) r̂

An inverse-square, conservative central force — always directed along the line joining the two masses.

Kepler's second law, derived

Equal areas in equal times follows from conservation of angular momentum, since gravity's zero torque about the central body keeps L = mr²(dθ/dt) constant.

Kepler's third law, T² = (4π²/GM)r³

Derived by setting gravity equal to the centripetal force requirement for a circular orbit; generalizes to elliptical orbits with r replaced by the semi-major axis a.

Gravitational potential energy, U(r) = −GMm/r

Built from an improper integral of F(r) out to infinity, with U(∞) = 0 chosen as the reference — always negative for any finite separation.

Escape velocity, vesc = √(2GM/R)

The minimum launch speed for total mechanical energy E = ½mv² − GMm/R to reach zero, allowing the object to just barely reach r → ∞.

Bound vs. unbound orbits

E < 0 means the object is gravitationally bound (orbits or falls back); E ≥ 0 means it escapes, with E = 0 the exact boundary case.

Unit 8: Putting It Together

Energy vs. force methods

Energy conservation is fastest for finding speed at a position when the path or intermediate details don't matter; force methods (ΣF = ma) are required whenever a problem needs time-dependence, since the work-energy derivation eliminates t entirely.

Rolling without slipping, v = Rω

The constraint linking translational and rotational motion when the contact point is instantaneously at rest.

Shape factor c (I = cMR²)

Determines a rolling object's acceleration down an incline, a = g sinθ/(1+c), and final speed, v = √(2gh/(1+c)) — independent of mass or radius. Solid sphere c = 2/5, solid cylinder c = 1/2, thin hoop c = 1.
