Recovering Position and Velocity via Integration

Lesson 1 differentiated position twice to reach acceleration. This lesson runs the film backward — integrating acceleration to recover velocity, and velocity to recover position.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

If a(t) = dv/dt, then v(t) is an antiderivative of a(t) — and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (AP Calc Unit 6, Lessons 3 and 5) says exactly how to get there.

Integrating to Recover Velocity and Position v(t) = v₀ + ∫₀ᵗ a(t′) dt′ and x(t) = x₀ + ∫₀ᵗ v(t′) dt′. Equivalently, the change in velocity over an interval is the area under the acceleration-vs-time graph over that interval, Δv = ∫ a dt, and the change in position is the area under the velocity-vs-time graph, Δx = ∫ v dt.
Why the constant of integration is the initial condition ∫a(t) dt gives a whole family of antiderivatives differing by a constant, +C. Physically, C is fixed by knowing v at one instant — usually v(0) = v₀. This is the same idea AP Calc Unit 7 uses for differential equations: a derivative relationship alone has infinitely many solutions, and an initial condition selects the one that matches your actual problem.
Example: constant acceleration (a sanity check) a(t) = 4 m/s², v₀ = 3 m/s, x₀ = 0. Then v(t) = 3 + ∫₀ᵗ 4 dt′ = 3 + 4t. And x(t) = 0 + ∫₀ᵗ (3 + 4t′) dt′ = 3t + 2t². At t = 3 s: v = 15 m/s, x = 9 + 18 = 27 m — exactly what v = v₀ + at and Δx = v₀t + ½at² from Physics 1 would give, because those equations are this lesson's formulas evaluated for constant a.
Example: non-constant acceleration a(t) = 6t m/s², v₀ = 0. v(t) = ∫₀ᵗ 6t′ dt′ = 3t². At t = 2 s, v(2) = 3(4) = 12 m/s. No Physics 1 equation could touch this, since acceleration itself changes with time — but the integral doesn't care.

Definite vs. indefinite: which one a physics problem usually wants

Most kinematics problems don't actually need the full function v(t) — they need one number, like "how far did it travel between t = 2 s and t = 5 s?" That's a job for a definite integral, ∫₂⁵ v(t) dt, evaluated directly by the Fundamental Theorem without ever writing down the "+C" form. Reach for the indefinite (general) antiderivative only when the problem genuinely needs the function x(t) or v(t) at arbitrary time, not just its value at one endpoint.

1. An object has a(t) = 2t m/s² and starts from rest (v₀ = 0). What is v(t)?

∫2t dt = t² + C. Since v₀ = 0, C = 0, giving v(t) = t².

2. The area under a velocity-vs-time graph between t = 1 s and t = 4 s represents:

Since v = dx/dt, x is an antiderivative of v, and ∫₁⁴ v dt gives the net change in position — the displacement — over that interval, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

3. Why is an initial condition like v(0) = v₀ necessary to fully determine v(t) from a(t)?

Indefinite integration produces a family of antiderivatives differing by a constant C; one known value of v pins down exactly which member of that family describes this motion.

4. Fill in the blank: finding v(t) from a(t) means finding an ___ of a(t).

Since a = dv/dt, reversing that derivative relationship means v must be an antiderivative of a — exactly the operation ∫a(t) dt performs.
