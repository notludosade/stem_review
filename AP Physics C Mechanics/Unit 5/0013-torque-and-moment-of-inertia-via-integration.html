Torque and Moment of Inertia via Integration

Physics 1 handed you I = ⅓ML² for a rod and told you to trust it. This lesson builds it — moment of inertia is nothing but Lesson 12's center-of-mass integral, with an extra factor of r.

Sign Convention for Rotation Counterclockwise is the positive direction for angle, angular velocity, and torque throughout this course, matching the standard math convention and the right-hand rule: curl the right hand's fingers from r toward F, and the thumb points along the torque's direction — out of the page for counterclockwise, into the page for clockwise.
Torque τ = r × F, magnitude τ = rF sin θ, where θ is the angle between the position vector r (from the pivot to the point of application) and the force F. This is unchanged from Physics 1 — what's new is moment of inertia.
Moment of Inertia as an Integral The discrete sum I = Σmiri² from Physics 1 generalizes, exactly like Lesson 12's center of mass, to a continuous integral: I = ∫r² dm, summing every infinitesimal mass element's contribution, weighted by the square of its distance from the rotation axis.
Why mass distribution matters more than total mass The r² weighting is why a hoop and a solid disk of equal mass have different moments of inertia — the hoop's mass sits entirely at the maximum radius, while the disk's is spread from center to edge, most of it at smaller r. I isn't just "how much mass" but "how much mass, and how far from the axis" — squared.
Example: a uniform rod about its end A rod of mass M, length L, uniform density λ = M/L, rotating about one end (x = 0 to x = L). Each mass element dm = λ dx = (M/L)dx contributes r²dm = x²(M/L)dx. I = ∫₀L x²(M/L) dx = (M/L)(x³/3)|₀L = ⅓ML² — the Physics 1 formula, now derived rather than quoted. (About the center instead, the same integral from −L/2 to L/2 gives 1⁄12ML².)

1. If you curl your right hand's fingers from r toward F, your thumb points:

This is the right-hand rule for a cross product: τ = r × F, and curling from r to F gives the thumb's direction as τ's direction.

2. Why does I = ∫r² dm require integration for a continuous object, rather than a simple sum?

Just as with center of mass (Lesson 12), a continuous mass distribution has no discrete list of point masses to sum — the sum's limit as each mass element shrinks to zero is exactly the definite integral.

3. A uniform rod of mass 6 kg and length 2 m rotates about one end. What is its moment of inertia?

I = ⅓ML² = ⅓(6)(2²) = ⅓(6)(4) = 8 kg·m².

4. Fill in the blank: in I = ∫r² dm, each mass element's contribution is weighted by its distance from the axis, raised to the power ___ (write it as "r squared").

The r² weighting is why mass distribution — not just total mass — determines an object's rotational inertia.
