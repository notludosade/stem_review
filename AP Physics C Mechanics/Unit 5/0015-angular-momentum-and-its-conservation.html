Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

The figure skater who spins faster pulling her arms in isn't a special rule — it's the product rule applied to L = Iω, the exact same move Lesson 12 used on a rocket's changing mass.

← Lesson 14 Unit 6, Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Angular Momentum and Torque L = Iω (for a rigid body about a fixed axis), and τnet = dL/dt — the rotational analog of F = dp/dt from Lesson 10. When τnet = 0, L stays constant: conservation of angular momentum.
Why a skater speeds up without any torque at all Expand dL/dt = d(Iω)/dt using the product rule: dL/dt = I(dω/dt) + ω(dI/dt) = τnet. Pulling arms inward changes I (dI/dt ≠ 0) without any external torque about the spin axis (τnet = 0, since friction with the ice acts essentially through the axis). For dL/dt to stay zero while I shrinks, ω must grow — the product rule forces it, exactly the way Lesson 12's rocket thrust term fell out of expanding d(mv)/dt.
Example A skater spins at ωi = 3 rad/s with arms extended, Ii = 4 kg·m². Pulling her arms in reduces her moment of inertia to If = 1 kg·m². Since L is conserved: Iiωi = Ifωf → 4(3) = 1(ωf) → ωf = 12 rad/s — quadrupled, because I dropped to a quarter of its original value.

1. Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved whenever:

Since τ_net = dL/dt, L is unchanging exactly when the net external torque is zero — I and ω can each still change individually, as long as their product doesn't.

2. A skater spins at 2 rad/s with I = 6 kg·m², then pulls her arms in to reach I = 2 kg·m². What is her new angular velocity?

I_iω_i = I_fω_f → 6(2) = 2(ω_f) → ω_f = 6 rad/s.

3. Why can ω change even when angular momentum L is conserved?

L is the product Iω, not either factor alone — conservation constrains the product, leaving I and ω free to trade off against each other.

4. Fill in the blank: the direction of angular momentum L, like torque, is found using the ___.

L = Iω is a vector, pointing along the rotation axis in the direction the right-hand rule assigns to the counterclockwise-positive spin.
