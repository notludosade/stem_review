1. A newsletter signup form asks for email, password, full name, date of birth, home address, phone number, and gender. What does Lesson 7 call the practice of only asking for what's actually needed?

Lesson 7 defines data minimization as collecting only the data a system actually needs to do its job — a newsletter signup doesn't need a home address or phone number.

2. What does GDPR Article 5(1)(c), cited in Lesson 7, require of personal data collection?

Article 5(1)(c) requires personal data to be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" for its stated purpose — the legal name for data minimization.

3. Fill in the blank: Lesson 7 says data collected for a feature that might ship someday is a ___ that starts accumulating the moment it's collected, even before that feature ever exists.

Lesson 7 states plainly that data sitting unused is a liability from the moment it's collected, while the feature that would justify it may never ship.

4. What does Lesson 8 call the unintentional exposure of sensitive data through application logs or monitoring systems?

Lesson 8's definition box names this exact term — channels like logs usually have weaker access controls and longer retention than the primary database.

5. What is specifically wrong with logger.info(f"Login attempt: {request.body}") in Lesson 8's bad example?

request.body contains the raw submitted password, which now sits in plaintext in a log file instead of only ever being hashed.

6. Answer both parts about Lesson 7's "we might need it later" argument:

(a) What does Lesson 7 recommend instead of collecting a field now for a feature that might exist someday?

(b) Fill in the blank: every field collected is something you now have to secure, store correctly, and eventually be responsible for if it ___.

(a) The better order is: build the feature, then add the field it actually needs, with a clear reason a user could be told. (b) A field you never collected can never leak, can never be subpoenaed, and can never be sold to a data broker.

7. Answer both parts about what belongs in application logs:

(a) According to the OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet cited in Lesson 8, how should passwords and session tokens be handled in logs?

(b) Fill in the blank: the fixed logging example logs request.body['email'] specifically instead of the entire request.___.

(a) Even a truncated or partial version of a secret is still guidance to avoid — the recommendation is exclusion, not partial logging. (b) The general pattern to watch for is logging an entire object instead of the specific fields actually needed.

8. Answer both parts about Lesson 9's anonymize() function, which only deletes the name and email fields:

(a) Why does deleting just the name and email fields fail to actually anonymize the record, per Lesson 9?

(b) Fill in the blank: matching supposedly anonymous data back to a specific real person is called ___.

(a) The remaining zip_code, birth_date, and sex fields are often enough on their own to re-identify most individual people. (b) See Lesson 9's definition box — it usually works by combining several individually-harmless fields.

9. Answer both parts about the study cited in Lesson 9:

(a) What did Latanya Sweeney's 2000 research find about ZIP code, birth date, and sex combined?

(b) Fill in the blank: Sweeney's finding shows that removing a name column can still leave enough fields behind to ___ almost everyone.

(a) Roughly 87% of the U.S. population was found to be uniquely identifiable from just these three fields — none of which look like an "identifier" on their own. (b) Removing the name didn't anonymize the data, since enough fields were left over to re-identify almost everyone anyway.

10. Answer both parts about the two terms Lesson 9 distinguishes:

(a) What is the key difference between pseudonymization and true anonymization, according to Lesson 9?

(b) Fill in the blank: replacing a direct identifier with a reversible stand-in, like a user ID instead of a name, is called ___.

(a) True anonymization aims to make re-identification impossible even in principle, usually by reducing precision rather than just deleting one field. (b) It's a real and useful privacy technique — the mistake is only in calling it full anonymization when it isn't.
