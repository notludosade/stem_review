Anonymization Isn't As Simple As Deleting a Name

Removing the obvious identifier is rarely enough to make data actually anonymous.

Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

It's common to see code that removes a name or email column and calls the result "anonymized." In 2000, computer scientist Latanya Sweeney published research showing that roughly 87% of the U.S. population could be uniquely identified using only three remaining fields most datasets keep: ZIP code, birth date, and sex. Removing the name didn't anonymize the data — it just removed the most obvious column, while leaving enough left over to re-identify almost everyone anyway.

"Anonymized" by deleting one column 
def anonymize(record):
    del record["name"]
    del record["email"]
    return record
    # zip_code, birth_date, and sex remain — often enough
    # on their own to re-identify most individual people

The function above still leaves the fields that Sweeney's research showed were enough, combined, to identify most people. Real anonymization has to account for what remains, not just what was removed — and has to consider that the remaining fields might be cross-referenced against other public datasets (voter rolls, property records) to re-identify someone.

Definition Re-identification is the process of matching supposedly anonymous data back to a specific real person, usually by combining several remaining fields — none dangerous alone — that together narrow the possibilities down to one individual.

Anonymization vs. pseudonymization

These two terms are often confused. Pseudonymization replaces a direct identifier with a stand-in — a user_id instead of a name — but the original identity can still be recovered if you have the mapping table, or if enough other fields let you re-identify the person anyway (as above). True anonymization is a much higher bar: the data must not be re-identifiable even in principle, which usually requires actively reducing precision (a birth year instead of a birth date, a region instead of an exact ZIP code) rather than just deleting one field and hoping.

Why it matters here Calling data "anonymized" when it isn't is itself a form of dishonesty from Lesson 2 — it tells users and regulators that a privacy protection exists which doesn't actually hold up. The fix isn't to avoid using data at all; it's to be accurate about how protected it really is.

Check your recall

1. What did Sweeney's 2000 research find about ZIP code, birth date, and sex combined?

Roughly 87% of the U.S. population was found to be uniquely identifiable from just these three fields — none of which look like an "identifier" on their own.

2. What's the key difference between pseudonymization and true anonymization?

Pseudonymization swaps an identifier for a stand-in that can be reversed with the mapping table or through re-identification; true anonymization aims to make that reversal impossible even in principle.

3. Why does this lesson call mislabeling data as "anonymized" a form of dishonesty?

Ties back to Lesson 2's "be honest and trustworthy" — telling users their data is anonymized when it can still be re-identified is a factual misrepresentation.

4. Fill in the blank: matching supposedly anonymous data back to a specific real person is called ___.

See the definition box above — it usually works by combining several individually-harmless fields.

5. Fill in the blank: replacing a direct identifier with a reversible stand-in, like a user ID instead of a name, is called ___.

It's a real and useful privacy technique — the mistake is only in calling it full anonymization when it isn't.
