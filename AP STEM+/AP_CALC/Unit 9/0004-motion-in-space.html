Motion in Space

Unit 8 tracked a particle moving along a single line — position, velocity, distance, all in one dimension. Vector-valued functions let the same ideas describe a particle moving through the plane.

1. Position, velocity, acceleration, speed

Definition — Motion Vectors If r(t) = ⟨x(t), y(t)⟩ gives a particle's position at time t, then v(t) = r'(t) is its velocity vector and a(t) = v'(t) = r''(t) is its acceleration vector — both found by differentiating componentwise, exactly as in Lesson 3. Speed is the magnitude of velocity, a single non-negative scalar: |v(t)| = √{[x'(t)]² + [y'(t)]²}.
Worked Example — A Launched Projectile r(t) = ⟨30t, 40t − 16t²⟩ (feet, t in seconds) — a projectile launched at t = 0 and landing when y(t) = 0 again, at t = 2.5 s. v(t) = ⟨30, 40 − 32t⟩ and a(t) = ⟨0, −32⟩ — constant, since only gravity acts once it's launched. At t = 0: speed = √(30² + 40²) = 50 ft/s, matching the launch speed. At the apex, t = 1.25 s (where 40 − 32t = 0): v(1.25) = ⟨30, 0⟩, so speed drops to 30 ft/s — purely horizontal, since vertical velocity is momentarily zero.

1. If r(t) is position, v(t) = r'(t) is:

Differentiating position once gives velocity — a vector tangent to the path, pointing the direction of travel. Speed is its magnitude; acceleration comes from one more derivative.

2. For r(t) = ⟨30t, 40t − 16t²⟩, the speed at t = 0 is ___ ft/s

v(0) = ⟨30, 40⟩, so speed = √(30² + 40²) = √(900 + 1600) = √2500 = 50 ft/s.

3. At the projectile's apex (t = 1.25 s), the velocity vector is:

At the apex, the vertical component of velocity (40 − 32t) passes through 0, but the horizontal component stays a constant 30 ft/s throughout — gravity only affects the vertical component.
