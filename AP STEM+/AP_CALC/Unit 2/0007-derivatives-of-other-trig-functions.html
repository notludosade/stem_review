Derivatives of the Other Trig Functions

Four more formulas to memorize — and a Quotient Rule callback that shows where they actually come from.

← Lesson 6 Unit 3, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 5 covered sin x and cos x. The remaining four trig functions — tan, cot, sec, and csc — are all built from those two, which means their derivatives don't need a fresh limit computation. The Quotient Rule from Lesson 6 gets there directly.

1. Where sec²x comes from

Worked Example tan(x) = sin(x) / cos(x). By the Quotient Rule, with f = sin x (f′ = cos x) and g = cos x (g′ = −sin x): d/dx[tan x] = [cos(x)·cos(x) − sin(x)·(−sin(x))] / cos²(x) = [cos²x + sin²x] / cos²x = 1 / cos²x = sec²x.

The Pythagorean identity cos²x + sin²x = 1 is what collapses the numerator down to something clean — the same identity that makes so much of trigonometry work.

Rule d/dx[tan x] = sec²x

2. Cotangent, secant, and cosecant

Rules d/dx[cot x] = −csc²x · d/dx[sec x] = sec x · tan x · d/dx[csc x] = −csc x · cot x

Each of these follows the same way tan x's did: write the function as a ratio of sin x and cos x (or their reciprocals), then apply the Quotient Rule. Notice the pattern — the "co" functions (cotangent, cosecant) each pick up a minus sign, mirroring cosine's minus sign back in Lesson 5.

Check your recall

1. d/dx[tan x] equals:

tan x = sin x / cos x, and the Quotient Rule collapses its derivative (via the Pythagorean identity) down to 1/cos²x = sec²x — no minus sign, unlike its "co" counterpart.

2. d/dx[sec x] equals:

Secant's derivative is the product of secant and tangent, with no minus sign — the minus sign belongs to cosecant's derivative instead.

3. d/dx[cot x] = −___.

Cotangent's derivative is −csc²x — the "co" function pairs with the minus sign, the same pattern as cosine back in Lesson 5.
