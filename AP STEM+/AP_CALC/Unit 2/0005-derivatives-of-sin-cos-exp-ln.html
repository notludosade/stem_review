Derivatives of sin x, cos x, eˣ, and ln x

Four more shortcuts, none of them from the Power Rule — and one function that's its own derivative.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Polynomials are built from power functions, so the Power Rule covers them completely. Trig, exponential, and logarithmic functions are a different species — their derivatives have to be worked out separately, straight from the limit definition. The results are worth memorizing outright.

1. Sine and cosine

Rule d/dx[sin x] = cos x and d/dx[cos x] = −sin x.

Drag the point along sin(x) — the readout compares the curve's actual slope there to cos(x) at that same x-value.

Try It — sin(x)'s Slope Is cos(x)

—

f(x) = sin(x) over x ∈ [0, 2π]. The numerical slope estimate uses h = 0.001, and is compared to cos(x) computed directly.

2. The exponential function

Rule d/dx[eˣ] = eˣ. Uniquely among elementary functions, eˣ is its own derivative — its slope at any point equals its height there.

Drag the point along eˣ and watch the slope estimate track the curve's own height.

Try It — eˣ Is Its Own Derivative

—

f(x) = eˣ, x ∈ [−1.5, 1.5]. As x grows, the curve gets steeper exactly as fast as it gets taller — that's what "its own derivative" means, visually.

3. The natural logarithm

Rule d/dx[ln x] = 1/x, for x > 0.

This one is worth noticing precisely because it's surprising: ln x itself is not a power function, but its derivative, 1/x = x⁻¹, is. That fact traces back to ln x and eˣ being inverses of each other — a connection Unit 3 will make precise when it covers derivatives of inverse functions in general.

Check your recall

1. d/dx[cos x] equals:

Cosine's derivative flips sign as well as function: d/dx[cos x] = −sin x, the one rule in this pair carrying a minus sign.

2. Which of these functions is its own derivative?

sin x's derivative is cos x, ln x's is 1/x, and x's is 1 — all different functions from the original. Only eˣ reproduces itself exactly.

3. d/dx[ln x] = 1/___.

The derivative of the natural log is the reciprocal power function 1/x — despite ln x itself not being a power function at all.

These four rules, plus Lesson 4's four, are the full "must-memorize" derivative list — every later rule (product, quotient, chain) is about combining these building blocks, not replacing them. If any of the eight ever feels shaky, that's worth flagging before moving on.
