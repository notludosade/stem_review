Derivative Notation and Estimating Derivatives

The many ways to write "derivative" — and how to estimate one when all you have is a picture or a table.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 defined f′(a) as a limit. In practice, you'll rarely have the luxury of computing that limit by hand — a graph or a table is often all you get. This lesson covers the notation used to talk about derivatives, and two practical skills: reading a derivative's approximate value off a graph, and off a table of data.

1. The notation zoo

Notation All of the following mean exactly the same thing — "the derivative of f, with respect to x": f′(x), y′, dy/dx, d/dx[f(x)]. At a specific point a, that value is written f′(a) or dy/dx│x=a.

Leibniz notation (dy/dx) emphasizes the derivative as a ratio of tiny changes — "how much y changes per tiny change in x." Lagrange notation (f′(x)) is more compact and pairs naturally with function notation. Both show up constantly in this course; get comfortable reading either.

3. Estimating a derivative from a table

Method Given values of f near a, the symmetric difference quotient [f(a + h) − f(a − h)] / (2h) usually estimates f′(a) more accurately than a one-sided quotient, because it averages the rate of change from both directions at once.

Watch the estimate for g(x) = x³ at a = 2 (true value: g′(2) = 3·2² = 12) tighten up as h shrinks.
