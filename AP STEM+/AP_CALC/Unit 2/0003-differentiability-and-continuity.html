Differentiability and Continuity

Every differentiable function is continuous. The reverse is where things get interesting.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Continuity was Unit 1's headline property. Now that Unit 2 has a second property — differentiability — it's worth asking how the two relate. The relationship only runs one direction, and the gap in the other direction is exactly where some of the most interesting graphs live.

1. Differentiable implies continuous

Theorem If f is differentiable at a, then f is continuous at a.

The intuition: if f′(a) exists as a finite number, the difference quotient [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h stays bounded as h → 0. That forces f(a + h) − f(a) itself to shrink to 0 — which is exactly the statement that f(x) → f(a) as x → a. A derivative can't exist at a break in the graph, because the difference quotient would blow up rather than settle down.

The converse, though, is false — a function can be perfectly continuous at a point and still have no derivative there. Watch what happens to the difference quotient of f(x) = |x| at a = 0, where the graph has a corner:

2. Three of these describe a continuous graph with no tangent line; one describes a graph that isn't even continuous. Which one doesn't belong?

A removable discontinuity already fails continuity, so differentiability is moot. Corners, cusps, and vertical tangents are all cases where the function stays continuous but the tangent line still breaks down.

3. For f(x) = |x| at a = 0, the right-hand derivative is +1 and the left-hand derivative is ___.

For h < 0, |0 + h| / h = −h / h = −1 — the left side approaches slope −1, disagreeing with the right side's +1, so no single derivative exists at 0.
