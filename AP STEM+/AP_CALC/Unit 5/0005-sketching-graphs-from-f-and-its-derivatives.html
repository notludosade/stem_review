Sketching Graphs from f, f′, and f″

Every tool from this unit — direction, extrema, and concavity — combines into one skill: reading three related graphs as a single connected story.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Nothing new gets introduced here. Lessons 2 and 4 gave two separate tests — one for direction, one for bending. Used together, they let you sketch f from nothing but a sign chart, or read off f's behavior directly from a picture of f′.

1. The combined sign chart

Framework — Reading f from f′ and f″ Four sign combinations of f′ and f″ describe every piece of a smooth curve: f′ > 0, f″ > 0 — increasing, concave up (climbing, curving up like the left side of a cup). f′ > 0, f″ < 0 — increasing, concave down (climbing, curving over like a hilltop approach). f′ < 0, f″ > 0 — decreasing, concave up (falling, curving up like the bottom of a valley). f′ < 0, f″ < 0 — decreasing, concave down (falling, curving over).
Worked Example For f(x) = x³ − 3x (Lessons 2–4): on (−∞, −1), f′ > 0 and f″ < 0 — increasing, concave down. On (−1, 0), f′ < 0 and f″ < 0 — decreasing, concave down. On (0, 1), f′ < 0 and f″ > 0 — decreasing, concave up. On (1, ∞), f′ > 0 and f″ > 0 — increasing, concave up. Four sign combinations, four distinct pieces of the same curve — and every boundary between them (x = −1, 0, 1) is a critical point or inflection point already found in earlier lessons.

Notice that no new computation happened — this is entirely a reorganization of facts Lessons 2 through 4 already established about this exact function.

Check your recall

1. A curve that is decreasing and concave up looks like:

Decreasing (f′ < 0) means falling; concave up (f″ > 0) means curving upward — exactly the shape of the left half of a valley, still falling but starting to level out. A hilltop approach is the concave-down case instead, and straight lines have no concavity at all.

2. Combining the signs of f′ and f″ gives ___ distinct classifications for a piece of a curve.

Each derivative has 2 possible signs (positive or negative), and the two are independent, so 2 × 2 = four total combinations describe every piece of a smooth curve.

3. For f(x) = x³ − 3x on (1, ∞), the curve is:

Past x = 1 (the local min), f′ > 0 so the curve climbs; past x = 0 (the inflection point), f″ > 0 so it's concave up — both hold simultaneously on all of (1, ∞), giving the "increasing, concave up" classification.

The real test of this skill is going backwards — being handed only a graph of f′ (not f) and reconstructing where f increases, decreases, and has inflection points. Ask for a practice problem in that reversed direction if straight sketching feels solid but the reverse doesn't yet. Lesson 6 turns all of this into a tool for solving real optimization problems.
