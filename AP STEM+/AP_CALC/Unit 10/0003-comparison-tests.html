p-Series and Comparison Tests

One family of series is so well understood it becomes a ruler — every other series can be measured against it.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

1. The p-series family

Definition — p-Series A p-series has the form Σ (from n=1 to ∞) 1/nᵖ. By the integral test (applied to f(x) = 1/xᵖ), a p-series converges when p > 1 and diverges when p ≤ 1. The case p = 1 is the harmonic series from Lesson 2 — the borderline case that diverges.
Method — Direct Comparison Test If 0 ≤ aₙ ≤ bₙ for all n past some point, then: Σbₙ converges ⟹ Σaₙ converges (a smaller series is squeezed under a finite ceiling), and Σaₙ diverges ⟹ Σbₙ diverges (a larger series is dragged up past a series that already blows up). Comparing against a p-series or geometric series with known behavior is the usual move.
Method — Limit Comparison Test If aₙ, bₙ > 0 and limₙ→∞ (aₙ/bₙ) = c for some finite c > 0, then Σaₙ and Σbₙ either both converge or both diverge. This sidesteps the inequality bookkeeping of the direct test — useful when aₙ ≤ bₙ isn't obviously true but the two series still behave the same way for large n.
Worked Example Does Σ 1/(n² + 3) converge? Compare to bₙ = 1/n² (a convergent p-series, p = 2 > 1). Since 1/(n² + 3) < 1/n² for all n ≥ 1, direct comparison gives convergence. Equivalently, limₙ→∞ [1/(n²+3)] / [1/n²] = limₙ→∞ n²/(n²+3) = 1, a finite positive constant — limit comparison agrees.

Drag p and watch whether the running total settles down or keeps climbing. p = 1 is the hinge — everything above converges, everything at or below diverges.
