Convergence and Geometric Series

Adding infinitely many numbers sounds like it should always blow up to infinity. Sometimes it does — but sometimes the running total settles on a single, finite number instead.

← Unit 9, Lesson 6 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

1. What it means for an infinite sum to converge

Definition — Convergent and Divergent Series For a series Σaₙ, the nth partial sum is Sₙ = a₁ + a₂ + ... + aₙ — a plain, finite sum. The series converges to L if the sequence of partial sums has limₙ→∞ Sₙ = L (a finite number); otherwise the series diverges. "Does the series converge?" is really a question about a sequence — the sequence of running totals.
Method — The Geometric Series Formula A geometric series Σ (from n=0 to ∞) a·rⁿ has a constant ratio r between consecutive terms. It converges when |r| < 1, with sum a / (1 − r), and diverges when |r| ≥ 1. This is one of the few series types with a known exact sum — most convergence tests only say yes or no, not what the total actually is.
Worked Example Σ (1/2)ⁿ from n=0 to ∞: a = 1, r = 1/2, |r| < 1, so it converges to 1/(1 − 1/2) = 2. Contrast: Σ 3ⁿ from n=0 to ∞ has r = 3, |r| ≥ 1, so it diverges — the partial sums 1, 4, 13, 40, ... grow without bound, never settling near any single number.

1. A series Σaₙ converges to L means:

Convergence is defined through the sequence of partial sums Sₙ, not the individual terms. A series can have terms shrinking to 0 and still diverge, as Lesson 2 will show.

2. Σ (1/3)ⁿ from n=0 to ∞ converges to ___

a = 1, r = 1/3, |r| < 1, so the sum is a/(1 − r) = 1/(1 − 1/3) = 1/(2/3) = 3/2.

3. Σ 5·(1.2)ⁿ from n=0 to ∞:

r = 1.2, and |r| ≥ 1 means the series diverges outright — the formula a/(1−r) only applies when |r| < 1; plugging in anyway gives a meaningless negative number here.
