Taylor Series and Power Series Representations

Lesson 7 stopped at a finite polynomial with a bounded error. Let the degree run to infinity, and — for the right functions — the error vanishes completely.

← Lesson 8 Contents Glossary →

1. From polynomial to full series

Definition — Taylor Series, Maclaurin Series The Taylor series of f centered at x = a is the infinite series Σ (from n=0 to ∞) f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n! · (x−a)ⁿ — Lesson 7's Pₙ(x), with n allowed to run to infinity. A Taylor series centered at a = 0 is called a Maclaurin series. When the Lagrange remainder Rₙ(x) → 0 as n → ∞, the series converges exactly to f(x), not just an approximation of it.
Method — Representing Functions via Substitution or Term-by-Term Calculus Rather than compute derivatives directly, a new power series can often be built from a known one: substitute an expression for x (e.g. −x² for x in 1/(1−x) = Σxⁿ), or differentiate or integrate a known series term by term within its interval of convergence. Both operations preserve the radius of convergence and avoid re-deriving derivatives from scratch.
Worked Example Start from 1/(1−x) = Σxⁿ (|x| < 1). Substitute −x² for x: 1/(1+x²) = Σ(−1)ⁿx²ⁿ. Integrating both sides term by term (and matching arctan 0 = 0) gives arctan x = Σ(−1)ⁿ x²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n+1). At x = 1 (the interval's endpoint, where convergence holds by the alternating series test): arctan 1 = π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + ... — an infinite series built entirely from substitution and integration, with no fresh derivative ever taken, that computes π.

2. Watching an infinite series build π

Each additional term of Σ(−1)ⁿ/(2n+1) nudges the partial sum toward π/4 ≈ 0.7854 — slowly, since this series converges only conditionally, but unmistakably.

Check your recall

1. A Maclaurin series is a Taylor series centered at:

"Maclaurin series" is simply the name for the special case a = 0 — every Maclaurin series is a Taylor series, but the reverse naming isn't used for other centers.

2. Substituting x² for x in 1/(1−x) = Σxⁿ gives a series for ___

Replacing every x with x² throughout both sides of the identity gives 1/(1−x²) = Σx²ⁿ, valid for |x²| < 1 — the same substitution trick used for the 1/(1+x²) example in this lesson.

3. A power series representation built by integrating a known series term by term:

Term-by-term integration (and differentiation) preserves the radius of convergence exactly, though the behavior at the endpoints can change — which is exactly why arctan 1 = π/4 converges even though the original geometric series 1/(1−x) diverges at x = 1.
