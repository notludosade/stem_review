Cross Sections: Squares and Semicircles

A 2D base region can be the footprint of a 3D solid. Stack a known cross-sectional shape at every x, and integrating the area gives the volume.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

1. Volume as a stack of slices

Method — Volume from Known Cross Sections Take a base region in the xy-plane, bounded above by f(x) and below by g(x). At each x, build a 3D cross section perpendicular to the x-axis whose defining length is the base's width there: s(x) = f(x) − g(x). If A(x) is that cross section's area, then V = ∫ab A(x) dx — the same accumulation idea as area between curves, just with a cross-sectional area function instead of a height.
Reference — Cross-Sectional Area Formulas Square, side s(x): A = s². Rectangle, side s(x) and height h: A = s·h. Equilateral triangle, side s(x): A = (√3/4)s². Semicircle, diameter s(x): A = (π/8)s². Each formula only needs the base's width s(x) — the shape determines how that width becomes an area.
Worked Example The base region is bounded by y = √x and the x-axis on [0, 4]. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. s(x) = √x, so A(x) = x. V = ∫₀⁴ x dx = 8. Swap to semicircular cross sections with the same base: A(x) = (π/8)x, so V = (π/8)∫₀⁴x dx = π.

1. For a solid with square cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis, the volume integral uses:

A square with side length s(x) has area s(x)² by definition — that becomes the integrand once s(x) is known from the base region.

2. With base y = √x on [0, 4] and square cross sections, the volume is ___

s(x) = √x, so A(x) = x, and ∫₀⁴x dx = [x²/2]₀⁴ = 8.

3. The quantity s(x) in these formulas represents:

s(x) = f(x) − g(x) is exactly the vertical extent of the 2D base region at x — the same quantity Lesson 3 integrated directly for area, now repurposed as a cross section's defining length.
