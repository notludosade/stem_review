Area Between Curves — More Than Two Intersections

When two curves cross more than twice, "top minus bottom" isn't fixed for the whole region — it flips at every crossing, so the integral has to be split.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

1. Same formula, more bookkeeping

Method — Splitting at Every Crossing Find every intersection of the two curves on the interval of interest, not just the two endpoints. Between each consecutive pair of intersections, decide which curve is on top by testing one point, then integrate |top − bottom| on that piece. The total area is the sum of all the pieces — exactly the same logic Unit 8 Lesson 2 used for total distance when velocity changed sign.

1. When two curves cross three times on an interval, the area between them requires:

Three crossings create two subintervals, and the curve that's "on top" can differ between them — each piece needs its own top-minus-bottom integrand.

2. The area between y = sin x and y = sin 2x on [0, π] is ___

Splitting at x = π/3 gives ∫₀^(π/3)[sin2x − sinx]dx + ∫_(π/3)^π[sinx − sin2x]dx = 1/4 + 9/4 = 5/2.

3. To find where "top" and "bottom" switch between two curves, you should:

Every point where the two curves are equal is a candidate switch point — solving f(x) = g(x) across the whole interval finds all of them, not just the outer bounds.

The hardest part of this skill is rarely the integration — it's correctly testing which curve is on top in each piece. Sketching the two curves, even roughly, is often faster and safer than reasoning about signs symbolically.
