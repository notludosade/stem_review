Position, Velocity, and Applied Accumulation

The Net Change Theorem from Unit 6 turns motion problems and rate-in-context problems into the same pattern: integrate a rate to find a total.

1. Position, velocity, and acceleration via integrals

Method — Motion via the Net Change Theorem Since v(t) = s′(t) and a(t) = v′(t), the Net Change Theorem gives s(b) = s(a) + ∫ab v(t) dt and v(b) = v(a) + ∫ab a(t) dt. Displacement is the signed integral of velocity; total distance traveled is ∫|v(t)| dt, splitting at every t where velocity changes sign — exactly the distinction Unit 6 introduced.
Worked Example v(t) = t² − 4t + 3 ft/sec on [0, 4]. This factors as (t − 1)(t − 3), positive on [0,1), negative on (1,3), positive on (3,4]. With F(t) = t³/3 − 2t² + 3t: displacement = F(4) − F(0) = 4/3 ft. Total distance = |F(1)−F(0)| + |F(3)−F(1)| + |F(4)−F(3)| = 4/3 + 4/3 + 4/3 = 4 ft — the particle backtracked, so distance exceeds the net displacement.

2. Applied accumulation in context

Method — Reading a Rate Function in Context Whenever a problem gives a rate — gallons per minute, people per hour, degrees per second — the amount accumulated from time a to b is ∫ab (rate) dt, and the total amount at time b is the starting amount plus that integral. When only a table of rate values is given rather than a formula, approximate the integral with a Riemann sum built directly from the table's data points.
Worked Example Oil leaks from a tank at L(t) = 5e−0.1t gallons/minute. How much leaks out over the first 10 minutes? ∫₀¹⁰ 5e−0.1t dt = [−50e−0.1t]₀¹⁰ = −50e−1 + 50 = 50(1 − e−1) ≈ 31.6 gallons.

Backtracking adds extra distance that partially cancels out in net displacement — splitting the integral at each sign change and taking absolute values captures the true distance traveled instead.
