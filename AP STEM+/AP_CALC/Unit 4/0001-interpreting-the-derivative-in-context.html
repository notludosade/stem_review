Interpreting the Derivative in Context

Every derivative you've computed so far has been a bare number or formula. Attach it to a real quantity, and it becomes a rate of change with units — and a sentence you can say out loud.

← Unit 3, Lesson 6 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Units 2 and 3 built a toolbox of derivative rules. Unit 4 spends its five lessons putting that toolbox to work — starting here, with the single skill every other lesson in the unit leans on: reading f′(a) as a rate, not just a slope.

1. The derivative as a rate of change, with units

Formula f′(a) is the instantaneous rate of change of f with respect to x, at x = a. Its units are always (units of f) per (units of x) — the same "per" you'd expect from any rate. If f measures dollars and x measures hours, f′(x) measures dollars per hour, full stop; the rule never changes, no matter what the two quantities are.
Worked Example A kettle's water temperature is T(t) = 20 + 60(1 − e−0.15t) °C, t minutes after it's switched on. Differentiating: T′(t) = 9e−0.15t °C/min. At t = 0: T′(0) = 9 °C/min — the water heats fastest right at the start. At t = 10: T′(10) = 9e−1.5 ≈ 2.01 °C/min — heating has slowed considerably as the temperature climbs toward its 80 °C ceiling.

2. Rates of change in other applied contexts

Motion gets its own lesson next, since position/velocity/acceleration is the single most common context on the exam. Every other context follows the identical pattern: identify the two quantities, state the units, and read the sign.

Formula — Sign and magnitude A positive derivative means the quantity is increasing at that instant; negative means decreasing. The magnitude tells you how fast — a small |f′(a)| means the quantity is changing sluggishly right then, regardless of how large f(a) itself is.
Worked Example A factory's cost to produce x units is C(x) dollars. C′(100) = 5 means: at a production level of 100 units, cost is increasing at a rate of $5 per additional unit — roughly, the 101st unit costs about $5 to make. If instead P(t) is a city's population t years after 2020 and P′(3) = −1200, the population was shrinking by about 1,200 people per year in 2023.

Check your recall

1. h(t) is a rocket's height in meters, t seconds after launch. The units of h′(t) are:

Units of a derivative are always (units of output)/(units of input): meters per second. Flipping the ratio, multiplying instead of dividing, and squaring the input's units (that one's actually acceleration's unit, one derivative further) are the three distractors.

2. P(t) is a company's profit in dollars, t months after launch. If P′(6) = 1200, then one month after month 6, profit is predicted to grow by approximately $___.

P′(6) = 1200 dollars/month is the instantaneous rate at month 6 — over one more month, profit is predicted to rise by approximately that same 1200 dollars, treating the rate as roughly constant over such a short step.

3. g(t) is a lake's fish population. If g′(3) < 0, the population is ___ at t = 3.

A negative derivative always means the quantity is decreasing at that instant, regardless of context — g′(3) > 0 would mean increasing, g′(3) = 0 would mean momentarily constant, and "unstable" isn't a sign-based interpretation at all.
