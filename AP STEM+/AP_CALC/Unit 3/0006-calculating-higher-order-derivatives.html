Calculating Higher-Order Derivatives

Differentiate a derivative, and a whole new layer of meaning opens up.

← Lesson 5 Unit 4, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Nothing stops you from differentiating a derivative. f′(x) is itself a function of x — so f″(x), the derivative of f′(x), is just another application of everything you already know.

1. Notation and the pattern

Notation f′(x): first derivative. f″(x) = d/dx[f′(x)]: second derivative. f‴(x): third. Beyond that, f⁽⁴⁾(x), f⁽⁵⁾(x), and so on — the parenthesized exponent means "differentiated this many times," not "raised to this power." (f²(x), with no parentheses, would mean [f(x)]², a completely different thing.) In Leibniz notation: d²y/dx², d³y/dx³, dⁿy/dxⁿ.
Worked Example f(x) = x⁴ − 3x³ + 2x.
f′(x) = 4x³ − 9x² + 2
f″(x) = 12x² − 18x
f‴(x) = 24x − 18
f⁽⁴⁾(x) = 24
f⁽⁵⁾(x) = 0 — and every derivative after that stays 0. A degree-4 polynomial's 5th derivative (and every one after) is always 0, since each differentiation drops the degree by one.

Try It — Three Derivatives, One Point

f(x) = x⁴ − 3x³ + 2x, x ∈ [−1, 2.5]. Numeric first derivative uses a symmetric difference quotient; numeric second derivative uses the central three-point formula [f(x+h) − 2f(x) + f(x−h)] / h², both with h = 0.001.

2. Higher-order derivatives of implicit relations

The method from Lesson 2 still applies — differentiate the equation implicitly a second time, then substitute the first derivative's already-known formula in to simplify.

Worked Example From Lesson 2: x² + y² = 9 gives 2x + 2yy′ = 0, so y′ = −x/y. Differentiating 2x + 2yy′ = 0 again with respect to x (Product Rule on the 2yy′ term): 2 + 2(y′)² + 2yy″ = 0. Solving: y″ = −[1 + (y′)²] / y. Substituting y′ = −x/y: y″ = −[1 + x²/y²] / y = −(y² + x²)/y³ = −9/y³, using x² + y² = 9 to simplify the numerator to a constant.

Check your recall

1. For f(x) = x⁴ − 3x³ + 2x, f″(x) equals:

f′(x) = 4x³ − 9x² + 2. Differentiating again: 4x³ → 12x², −9x² → −18x, and the constant 2 → 0. Forgetting to double the −9x² term's coefficient, misapplying the Power Rule to the first term, or forgetting to drop each exponent by one all lead to the wrong three distractors.

2. If f(x) = sin(x), then f‴(x) (the third derivative) equals:

sin x → cos x → −sin x → −cos x, one differentiation at a time. The three distractors are exactly the other three stops along that same four-step cycle.

3. For f(x) = x⁴ − 3x³ + 2x, f⁽⁴⁾(x) (the fourth derivative) is the constant ___.

f‴(x) = 24x − 18, and differentiating once more drops the 24x term to 24 and the constant −18 to 0 — leaving the constant 24, matching a degree-4 polynomial's degree-0 fourth derivative.
