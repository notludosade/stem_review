Applying Properties of Definite Integrals

A handful of algebraic rules let a definite integral be split, combined, reversed, or bounded — without ever touching a Riemann sum again.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

1. The core properties

Properties — Definite Integrals Zero width: ∫ₐᵃ f(x) dx = 0 — no interval, no area. Reversing bounds: ∫ᵦᵃ f(x) dx = −∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx — running the sum backward flips its sign. Constant multiple: ∫ₐᵇ c·f(x) dx = c∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx. Sum/difference: ∫ₐᵇ (f ± g) dx = ∫ₐᵇ f dx ± ∫ₐᵇ g dx. Additivity over intervals: ∫ₐᵇ f dx + ∫ᵦᶜ f dx = ∫ₐᶜ f dx, true even when b isn't between a and c.
Worked Example Given ∫₁⁵ f(x) dx = 8 and ∫₅⁹ f(x) dx = 3, additivity gives ∫₁⁹ f(x) dx = 8 + 3 = 11 — the two adjacent pieces combine into the whole without recomputing anything.
Worked Example Given ∫₀³ f(x) dx = 4 and ∫₀³ g(x) dx = 7, linearity gives ∫₀³ (2f(x) + g(x)) dx = 2(4) + 7 = 15 — constants pull out, and the sum splits term by term.
