The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2, and the Net Change Theorem

Part 1 said an accumulation function's derivative is the integrand back again. Part 2 turns that fact around: it evaluates any definite integral instantly, with no limit of a sum required.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

1. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2

Theorem — FTC Part 2 If f is continuous on [a, b] and F is any antiderivative of f (meaning F′ = f), then ∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a). Written [F(x)]ab. Every antiderivative of f differs from another only by a constant, and that constant cancels in the subtraction — so any one of them works.
Worked Example ∫13 2x dx: an antiderivative of 2x is F(x) = x². [x²]13 = 3² − 1² = 9 − 1 = 8 — the exact value, found without ever building a Riemann sum.

2. The Net Change Theorem

Theorem — Net Change Rewriting FTC Part 2 with F′ in place of f: ∫ab F′(x) dx = F(b) − F(a). The integral of a rate of change over [a, b] equals the net change in the original quantity over that interval — velocity integrates to net displacement, marginal cost integrates to total cost change, a flow rate integrates to net volume change.
Worked Example v(t) = 3 − t (m/s) is a particle's velocity on [0, 6]. An antiderivative is F(t) = 3t − t²/2, so net displacement = F(6) − F(0) = (18 − 18) − 0 = 0 — the particle ends up back where it started, even though it never stopped moving.
Fact — Net Change vs. Total Distance ∫ab v(t) dt gives net displacement — forward motion and backward motion cancel. Total distance traveled is ∫ab |v(t)| dt instead, which never cancels. Computing it means splitting [a, b] at every point where v changes sign and adding the absolute value of each piece — for v(t) = 3 − t on [0, 6], that split is at t = 3, giving |F(3) − F(0)| + |F(6) − F(3)| = 4.5 + 4.5 = 9 meters traveled, despite ending at a net displacement of 0.
