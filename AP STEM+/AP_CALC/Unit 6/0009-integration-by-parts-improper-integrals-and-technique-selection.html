Integration by Parts, Improper Integrals, and Selecting Techniques

One more technique for products that don't substitute cleanly, one new kind of integral whose bounds stretch to infinity, and a closing guide for picking the right tool.

← Lesson 8 Contents Glossary →

1. Integration by parts

Method — Integration by Parts Reversing the Product Rule gives ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du. Choose u to be the factor that gets simpler when differentiated, and dv to be the factor that's easy to antidifferentiate.
Worked Example ∫ x eˣ dx: let u = x, dv = eˣ dx, so du = dx and v = eˣ. Then ∫ x eˣ dx = xeˣ − ∫ eˣ dx = xeˣ − eˣ + C. Picking u = eˣ instead would have made the remaining integral harder, not easier — the choice of u matters.

2. Improper integrals

Definition — Improper Integral An integral is improper if an interval of integration is infinite, or the integrand is unbounded somewhere in the interval. Either case is handled the same way: replace the problem bound with a variable, and take a limit. ∫a∞ f(x) dx = limb→∞ ∫ab f(x) dx. If the limit exists (is finite), the improper integral converges; otherwise it diverges.
Worked Example — Convergence vs. Divergence ∫1∞ 1/x² dx = limb→∞ [−1/x]1b = limb→∞ (1 − 1/b) = 1 — it converges, even though the region has infinite width. But ∫1∞ 1/x dx = limb→∞ [ln x]1b = limb→∞ ln(b), which grows without bound — it diverges. The dividing line: ∫1∞ 1/xᵖ dx converges exactly when p > 1 — a fact Unit 10 will meet again as the p-series test.

4. Selecting a technique

Guide — Choosing an Antidifferentiation Technique Table match already? Use Lesson 6's basic rules directly. A composed function times its inner derivative? u-substitution (Lesson 7). Numerator's degree ≥ denominator's? Long division first (Lesson 8). Irreducible quadratic denominator? Complete the square (Lesson 8). Rational function with a factorable denominator? Partial fractions (Lesson 8, and Precalc Lesson 34). A product of two unrelated functions (like x and eˣ)? Integration by parts (this lesson). An infinite bound or an unbounded integrand? Rewrite as a limit — it's improper (this lesson). Real problems often need two of these in sequence: divide, then decompose; substitute, then integrate by parts.

Check your recall

1. ∫ x eˣ dx, using integration by parts with u = x and dv = eˣ dx, equals:

uv − ∫v du = xeˣ − ∫eˣ dx = xeˣ − eˣ + C — the sign on the second term comes directly from the integration-by-parts formula.

2. ∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx converges to ___

lim(b→∞) (1 − 1/b) = 1 − 0 = 1: the improper integral converges to a finite value despite the infinite interval.

3. ∫₁^∞ 1/x dx (the p = 1 case):

ln(b) grows without bound as b → ∞, so the limit doesn't exist as a finite number — the integral diverges, right at the p = 1 boundary of the convergence rule.
