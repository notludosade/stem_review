Riemann Sums and Approximating Area

Units 1–5 built the derivative. Unit 6 turns around and asks the opposite question: given a rate, how much has actually accumulated? It starts with rectangles.

← Unit 5, Lesson 7 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Suppose f(x) is a velocity, and the question is total distance traveled — the area between the velocity curve and the x-axis. For a straight line, that area is a rectangle or triangle, easy geometry. For a curve, there's no formula yet. The fix: chop the region into thin rectangles, whose areas are easy, and add them up.

1. Rectangular approximation

Method — Riemann Sum Split [a, b] into n subintervals of equal width Δx = (b − a)/n. On each subinterval, build a rectangle whose height is f at some sample point in that subinterval, and whose width is Δx. Adding all n rectangle areas gives a Riemann sum — an approximation of the area under the curve.
Definition — Left, Right, and Midpoint Sums The three most common choices for the sample point: a left sum uses each subinterval's left endpoint, a right sum uses its right endpoint, and a midpoint sum uses the point exactly halfway between. All three converge to the same value as n → ∞, but for a fixed, small n, they typically disagree.

2. A worked comparison

Worked Example f(x) = x² on [0, 4], with n = 4 rectangles, so Δx = 1. Left sum samples x = 0, 1, 2, 3: heights 0, 1, 4, 9, total 14. Right sum samples x = 1, 2, 3, 4: heights 1, 4, 9, 16, total 30. Midpoint sum samples x = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5: heights 0.25, 2.25, 6.25, 12.25, total 21. Since f is increasing here, the left sum underestimates and the right sum overestimates — the true area (found in Lesson 2) turns out to be 64/3 ≈ 21.33, which the midpoint sum lands closest to.

3. Watching the approximation improve

Choose a method, then slide out to more rectangles. Both left and right sums squeeze toward the same value as n grows — they just approach it from opposite sides.

Left, right, and midpoint sums are really just three specific choices out of infinitely many possible sample points — and every one of them converges to the same limit. Ask what happens with a decreasing function, where the over/underestimate roles flip, if that still feels unclear. Lesson 2 takes that limit as n → ∞ and gives it a name and a notation of its own: the definite integral.
