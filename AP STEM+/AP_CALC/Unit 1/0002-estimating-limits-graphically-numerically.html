Estimating Limits Graphically & Numerically

Reading a limit off a graph or a table — and recognizing when one doesn't exist at all.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1's table always agreed from both sides. That's not guaranteed. A limit only exists if the function approaches the same value from the left and from the right — and graphs make disagreement between the two sides easy to see.

1. One-sided limits, and when they disagree

Definition limx→c⁻ f(x) is the left-hand limit — what f approaches as x nears c only from values less than c. limx→c⁺ f(x) is the right-hand limit, from values greater than c. The two-sided limx→c f(x) exists only when both one-sided limits exist and agree.

Try It — One-Sided Limits at a Jump

—

h(x) = x + 1 for x < 1, and h(x) = 4 − x for x ≥ 1. The hollow circle is where the left piece is heading but never reaches; the filled circle is h(1) itself.

Approaching from the left, h(x) heads toward 2. Approaching from the right, it heads toward 3. They disagree — so limx→1 h(x) does not exist (written DNE), even though h(1) = 3 is perfectly well-defined. A limit failing to exist and a function being undefined are two different problems.

2. When algebra alone won't reveal the limit

Lesson 1's example simplified with algebra: (x² − 4)/(x − 2) reduces to x + 2 once you factor. Not everything reduces so easily. g(x) = sin(x) / x is undefined at x = 0 (0/0 again) but doesn't factor into anything simpler — the only way in is to look at the graph and the numbers directly.
