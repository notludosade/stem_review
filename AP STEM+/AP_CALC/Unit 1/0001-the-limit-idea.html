The Limit Idea

What it means to ask what's happening at a single instant — and the tool Calculus invents to answer it.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

An average rate of change — the slope of a secant line between two points — is something you can already compute (see Precalculus Lesson 3 if that's unfamiliar). Calculus starts from a harder question: what is the rate of change at a single point, not across an interval? Divide by zero width and the formula breaks — so Calculus needs a new tool. That tool is the limit.

1. Shrinking the interval to nothing

Below is f(x) = x² with two points on it. Point A is anchored at x = 1. Drag point B toward it and watch the secant slope in the readout.

Try It — Drag Point B Toward Point A

—

Point A stays at x = 1. As B slides toward x = 1, the gap shrinks toward zero and the secant slope stops changing much — it's homing in on one number.

Why it matters That number the slope homes in on is the instantaneous rate of change at x = 1 — what Calculus calls the derivative. You can't compute it by plugging the gap width directly into the slope formula (that's 0/0), but you just watched it anyway, by asking what the slope approaches as the gap shrinks. That "approaches, without ever quite dividing by zero" idea is exactly what a limit formalizes.

2. Limit notation, and a case where it's the only way in

Definition limx→c f(x) = L means: as x gets arbitrarily close to c (from either side, without ever equaling c), f(x) gets arbitrarily close to L. Read aloud as "the limit of f(x) as x approaches c is L."

Here's a function where plugging in x = 2 directly fails outright: f(x) = (x² − 4) / (x − 2). At x = 2 that's 0/0 — undefined. Move the slider to shrink the distance from 2 and watch both sides of the table.

Both sides start at 3 and 5 — far from agreeing yet.

f(2) itself is undefined — there's a hole in the graph at x = 2. But the table shows both sides sliding toward the same number as x closes in on 2. That shared number is the limit, even though the function never actually reaches it at x = 2 itself. A limit describes behavior near a point, and doesn't require the function to be defined at that point at all.

3. A rate of change over an interval is an average rate of change; a rate of change at a single point is called the ___ rate of change.

That's the number Widget 1's secant slope was homing in on as the gap shrank to zero — the derivative, in embryo.
