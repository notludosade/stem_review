Infinite Limits and Asymptotes

Taking Lesson 4's infinite discontinuity further — locating every vertical asymptote, and what a function settles toward as x runs off forever.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 4's r(x) = 1/(x − 2) had one vertical asymptote, spotted by inspection. Real rational functions often have more than one, and the question shifts from "does this blow up?" to "exactly where, and how do I know I found all of them?" There's a second, opposite question too: instead of asking what happens as x closes in on one point, what happens as x runs off to infinity itself — does the function settle down, or keep changing forever?

1. Vertical asymptotes: locating every one

Definition A rational function has a vertical asymptote at x = c whenever the denominator is zero at c and the numerator is not also zero there. Near such a c, limx→c⁻ f(x) and/or limx→c⁺ f(x) is +∞ or −∞ — the function grows without bound rather than converging.

That "numerator not also zero" clause matters: if both factor to zero at the same c, that c is a hole (Lesson 4's removable case), not an asymptote — the (x − c) cancels before you ever divide by zero. Take g(x) = 1/((x − 1)(x + 2)). The denominator is zero at x = 1 and x = −2, and the numerator (just 1) is never zero anywhere — so both are genuine vertical asymptotes. Drag the point across all three branches.

g(x) = 1/((x − 1)(x + 2)). Three branches, split at the two zeros of the denominator. Drag near x = 1 or x = −2 and watch g(x) grow without bound.

Notice the middle branch behaves differently from the two outer ones: it dips toward −∞ near both of its edges, since the function is negative everywhere between the two asymptotes. Sign matters as much as location — knowing where the asymptotes are is only half the picture; knowing which way the function shoots on each side of each one is the other half.

2. Limits at infinity: what happens as x runs off forever

Definition limx→∞ f(x) = L means f(x) gets arbitrarily close to L as x grows without bound (and similarly for x → −∞). When either limit is a finite number L, the line y = L is a horizontal asymptote — a height the function settles toward, though unlike a vertical asymptote it's a value the function is often free to actually reach or cross.

Take f(x) = (2x + 3)/(x + 7). Push x far in both directions and read the table.

Shortcut For a rational function, compare the degree of the numerator to the degree of the denominator: denominator's degree wins → horizontal asymptote y = 0. Degrees are equal → horizontal asymptote y = (ratio of leading coefficients). Numerator's degree wins → no horizontal asymptote at all. f(x) = (2x + 3)/(x + 7) has equal degrees (both degree 1), so the shortcut gives y = 2/1 = 2 directly — matching the table, without pushing x out by hand.
