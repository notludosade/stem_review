Continuous Delivery vs. Continuous Deployment

Unit 2 got every merge to a green, tested build artifact. This unit is about what happens next — and it turns out "green" and "in production" are two different moments that a team gets to decide whether to merge.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

A CI pipeline (Unit 2) ending in a green build only proves the code is correctly built and tested — it says nothing about whether that build has actually reached the users it was written for. Getting it the rest of the way is a deliberate choice with two common shapes, and the difference between them is smaller than the names suggest.

Two names for one very similar pipeline

Definition Continuous Delivery (CD) means every change that passes CI is automatically packaged into a release that's ready to deploy at any time — but an actual human still decides when, usually clicking a button to trigger the release. Continuous Deployment removes that last manual step: every change that passes CI deploys straight to production automatically, with no human approval gate at all. The pipeline up through "ready to release" is identical either way — the only difference is whether a person or the pipeline itself pulls the final trigger.
Definition A release gate is the manual approval step that separates Continuous Delivery from Continuous Deployment. It can be as small as one click, but its presence is the whole distinction — remove it, and delivery becomes deployment without changing anything else about the pipeline.

In Delivery mode, "Approve pending release" is the only way changes reach production. In Deployment mode, it does nothing — merges go straight through.

Why teams keep a release gate at all, if the pipeline can already be trusted Continuous Deployment isn't strictly "more advanced" than Continuous Delivery — it's a trust decision. A team ships Continuous Deployment when its test suite (Unit 2) and deployment strategy (Lesson 8) are trustworthy enough that a human approval step adds delay without adding real safety. A team with regulatory requirements, a less mature test suite, or changes that need business timing (a coordinated marketing launch, say) keeps the gate on purpose — not because their pipeline is worse, but because a human decision genuinely belongs in that spot for reasons the pipeline itself can't evaluate.

1. What is the core difference between Continuous Delivery and Continuous Deployment?

The presence or absence of a manual release gate is the entire distinction.

2. In Continuous Delivery, what does "ready to deploy at any time" actually mean?

Delivery guarantees a release is always ready — it just waits for a human to pull the trigger.

3. Why might a team deliberately keep a release gate instead of moving to full Continuous Deployment?

Keeping the gate is a deliberate trust and timing decision, not a sign of a worse pipeline.

4. Fill in the blank: the practice where every change that passes CI deploys straight to production automatically, with no manual approval step, is called ___.

Continuous Deployment removes the human approval step that Continuous Delivery keeps.

5. Fill in the blank: the manual approval step that separates Continuous Delivery from Continuous Deployment is called a ___.

Removing the release gate is literally the only thing that turns Delivery into Deployment.
