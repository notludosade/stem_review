Alerting Without Drowning in Noise

Lesson 16 covered three ways to look at a system. None of them help if nobody's actually looking at the right moment — which is exactly what alerting is supposed to fix, and exactly where a well-intentioned setup usually goes wrong.

← Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Nobody watches a dashboard around the clock. The whole point of an alert is to replace that impossible task with an automated tripwire — something crosses a line, and the right person finds out immediately instead of whenever they next happen to check. Getting the tripwire right turns out to be much harder than it sounds: set it too sensitive, and it fires constantly for things that don't matter; set it too loose, and it stays silent through something that does.

The tripwire, and what happens when it's wrong

Definition An alert is an automated notification triggered when a monitored signal — a metric crossing a threshold, an error rate spike, a specific log pattern matching — indicates something needs a human's attention right now. The point of an alert is that nobody has to watch a dashboard waiting for it to happen; the system does the watching and interrupts a human only when its defined condition is met.
Definition Alert fatigue is the desensitization that sets in when alerts fire too often, or for conditions nobody can actually do anything about, until people start muting notifications or skimming past them out of habit — including the one alert that actually mattered. It's the alerting equivalent of Unit 2's flaky test eroding trust in a red build: once an alert stops reliably meaning "something needs you," people stop treating it that way.

Why tuning the threshold isn't the whole fix Even a perfectly-tuned threshold doesn't help if the alert it fires isn't actionable — one with a specific, known response a human can actually take right now. A CPU-usage alert that pages someone at 3 a.m. with no clear next step just adds fatigue without adding safety; an alert worth waking someone up for should come with an obvious first move, even if that move is just "check whether the on-call runbook covers this." Teams that fix alert fatigue usually do two things at once: tune thresholds to reduce false positives, and delete or downgrade any alert that's technically correct but never actually actionable.

1. What is the purpose of an alert?

An alert's job is to interrupt a human automatically, at the moment its condition is actually met.

2. What is alert fatigue?

Alert fatigue is the erosion of trust that comes from too much noise, not a technical failure of the alerting tool itself.

3. Why isn't tuning a threshold's sensitivity the whole fix for alert fatigue?

An accurately-firing alert can still be useless noise if there's no actionable response attached to it.
