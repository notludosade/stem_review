On-Call and Incident Response

Lesson 17 built a well-tuned tripwire. This lesson is about what's actually supposed to happen the moment it fires — who gets paged, what they're supposed to do first, and how a chaotic, everyone-jumps-in scramble turns into a coordinated response.

← Lesson 17 Unit 7, Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

An actionable alert (Lesson 17) still has to reach an actual person, and that person still has to know what happens next. Without a plan for both of those, a real page at 3 a.m. turns into exactly the kind of chaos this unit has been trying to prevent — several people independently discovering the problem, nobody sure who's actually in charge of fixing it, and no shared record of what was tried.

Who gets paged, and who's in charge once they are

Definition An on-call rotation is a schedule where responsibility for responding to pages rotates among a defined group of engineers on a set cadence — a week at a time, say — so no single person is always on the hook, and the burden of being interrupted is shared roughly evenly across the team over time.
Definition An incident is a formally declared event where a system is degraded or unavailable in a way that affects real users, which kicks off a coordinated response instead of leaving each person who notices to independently investigate on their own. The incident commander is the person responsible for coordinating that response — tracking status, deciding when to escalate or pull in more people, and calling it resolved — and isn't necessarily the engineer actually fixing the underlying code.

Worked example A checkout error-rate alert pages the on-call engineer at 2:17 a.m. She acknowledges it within two minutes — stopping the page from escalating further up the rotation — and within five minutes decides this is bigger than a quick fix: she formally declares an incident and takes the incident commander role herself. She pulls in a second engineer with checkout-service context, uses Lesson 9's feature flag to disable the broken code path as a fast mitigation, confirms the error rate drops back to normal, and declares it resolved at 2:41 a.m. — twenty-four minutes from page to resolution, with a written timeline the whole way, ready for a postmortem later that week.
Why declaring an incident formally is worth the overhead it feels like It's tempting to skip the formal declaration when a problem seems small — just quietly fix it and move on. The cost shows up when a "small" problem turns out not to be: without a declared incident and a named incident commander, a second engineer who notices the same symptom independently starts investigating in parallel, possibly trying a conflicting fix at the same time, with neither person aware the other is even looking. Declaring early costs almost nothing if the problem turns out to be minor; not declaring costs real coordination time if it turns out not to be — and by the time that's obvious, the chaotic version has usually already started. The incident closes, appropriately, with the same blameless postmortem Unit 1 introduced — reviewing what happened and why, not who to blame for it.

1. What does an on-call rotation accomplish?

Rotation shares the burden of being paged evenly across a team over time.

2. What is the incident commander's role during an incident?

The incident commander coordinates the response; someone else may well be the one actually debugging.

3. Why does formally declaring an incident, even for a problem that seems small, tend to save time overall?

A declared incident with a clear owner avoids the coordination cost of an uncoordinated, parallel scramble.
