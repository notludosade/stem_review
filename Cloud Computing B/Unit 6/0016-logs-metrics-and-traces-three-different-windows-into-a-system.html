Logs, Metrics, and Traces: Three Different Windows Into a System

Lesson 3 mapped "Observe & Respond" as the pipeline's last stage without saying what it actually looks at. A deployed, self-healing service (Unit 5) can still be slow, wrong, or quietly failing for some users — "it deployed" and "it's working right now" are different claims, and this unit is about closing that gap.

← Unit 5, Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

An orchestrator's self-healing (Lesson 15) only reacts to one narrow signal: is the running count below the desired count. It has nothing to say about a service that's fully up, fully healthy by that measure, and still returning the wrong answer, or taking eight seconds to do it. Seeing that requires actually looking at the system while it runs — and "looking" turns out to mean three genuinely different kinds of data, each answering a question the other two can't.

Three signals, three different jobs

Definition A log is a timestamped, discrete record of a single event — a request received, an error thrown, a job completed — usually written as either free-form text or, in modern systems, structured key/value fields that are easy to search and filter later. A log answers "what exactly happened, in this one specific instance," in as much detail as whoever wrote the log line chose to include.
Definition A metric is a numeric measurement, sampled at regular intervals and stored as a running series over time — CPU usage every 15 seconds, request count every minute. Metrics are cheap to store and fast to graph, and they're good at showing trends, but a metric alone can't tell you about any one specific request. A trace follows a single request's entire path across multiple services, stitched together into one timeline; a trace is built out of spans, and each span records how long that one request spent inside one particular service along the way.

Interactive: logs, metrics, or traces?

Worked example A metrics dashboard shows the checkout service's error rate jump from 0.1% to 4% at 2:03 p.m. — a metric caught the trend, fast and cheap, across every request. Confirming that a single one of those failed requests belongs to a real customer, and reading the exact exception it hit, means pulling up the log line for that specific request. If the checkout service calls an inventory service and a payments service to complete one order, a trace is what shows whether the 4% is failing inside checkout itself or inside one of the two services it depends on.
Why you need all three, not just the cheapest one Logs alone don't scale: storing and searching a detailed log line for every single event, at real production volume, gets expensive fast, and a log by itself says nothing about a trend across millions of requests. Metrics alone don't explain: they'll tell you latency went up at 2:03 p.m. with no detail on which specific requests, or why. Traces alone are usually sampled, not exhaustive, because capturing a full timeline for every single request is expensive at scale — great for the request you're chasing, useless as a trend line. Each one trades detail for cost differently, which is exactly why real observability stacks run all three side by side instead of picking a favorite.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What question does a log answer that a metric can't?

A log's strength is a detailed record of one specific event, not an aggregated trend.

2. Why are metrics cheaper to store than logging every single event in full detail?

Sampling numeric values at intervals is far cheaper than a detailed record of every single event.

3. Why is a trace specifically useful when a request passes through multiple services?

A trace's whole value is following one request across service boundaries, unlike a log or metric scoped to one place.

4. Fill in the blank: a numeric measurement sampled at regular intervals and stored as a series over time is called a ___.

A metric is cheap, fast to graph, and good at showing trends over time.

5. Fill in the blank: within a trace, the record of how long one request spent inside one particular service is called a ___.

A trace is built from spans, one per service the request passed through.
