Bias and Fairness in AI Models

Everything built since Unit 2 learns its behavior from data. When that data reflects a real-world pattern of unfair treatment, the model learns that pattern too — and can apply it at a scale no individual person ever could.

Lesson 29 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 2 described bias in data — outliers, skew, missing values. This lesson is about a narrower, higher-stakes case: algorithmic bias, where a model's predictions systematically disadvantage a group of people, usually because its training data reflects historical human decisions that were themselves unfair. A hiring-screening model trained on ten years of past hiring decisions doesn't just learn "who was hired" — it learns whatever bias, conscious or not, shaped those past decisions, and reproduces it automatically on every future application.

Python — checking recall across groups, not just overall 
from sklearn.metrics import recall_score

# group_a and group_b are subsets of the same validation set (Unit 2),
# split by some protected attribute rather than treated as one pool
recall_a = recall_score(y_true_group_a, y_pred_group_a)
recall_b = recall_score(y_true_group_b, y_pred_group_b)

print(f"recall, group A: {recall_a:.2f}")
print(f"recall, group B: {recall_b:.2f}")
# a large gap here is a fairness problem invisible in one overall score

Lesson 10's recall_score reappears here unchanged — the only difference is computing it separately per group instead of pooling everyone into one number. A model can post excellent overall accuracy while its recall for one group is dramatically worse than another's, and that gap simply doesn't show up unless someone deliberately looks for it. This is exactly why bias review has to be a deliberate, separate check, not something that overall accuracy happens to catch on its own.

Definition Algorithmic bias is a model's predictions systematically disadvantaging a group of people, typically because training data reflects historical unfair treatment of that group.
Why it matters here Computer Programming Ethics, Lesson 13, "Where Bias Enters a Program" already covers where bias comes from and how to think about it as a developer — that reasoning applies directly here and isn't repeated. What's specific to this course is the mechanism: a trained model doesn't just risk containing bias the way hand-written code can, it actively learns and can amplify whatever bias already exists in its training data, at the scale of every prediction it ever makes.
