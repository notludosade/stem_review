Nuclear Structure and Radioactivity

Zoom into the atom's nucleus itself, and quantization shows up yet again — this time governing which nuclei are stable and which spontaneously fall apart.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Nuclear Structure A nucleus contains protons (positive charge) and neutrons (neutral), collectively called nucleons. Atomic number Z = number of protons (defines the element); mass number A = total nucleons (protons + neutrons). Isotopes of an element share Z but differ in neutron count (and therefore A).
Why the nucleus doesn't fly apart from proton-proton repulsion Protons packed within a nucleus experience enormous Coulomb repulsion (Unit 2) at such short range, yet nuclei hold together — this is only possible because of the strong nuclear force, an attractive force far stronger than the electric repulsion at nuclear distances, but with an extremely short range (roughly the size of the nucleus itself). Beyond that tiny range, the strong force vanishes almost entirely, which is exactly why very large nuclei (many protons spread across a larger volume) become unstable — the strong force can no longer reach across the whole nucleus to counteract the cumulative electric repulsion, making heavy elements prone to radioactive decay.
Types of Radioactive Decay Alpha decay: nucleus emits a helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons), reducing Z by 2 and A by 4. Beta decay: a neutron converts to a proton (emitting an electron), increasing Z by 1 with A unchanged. Gamma decay: nucleus emits a high-energy photon, releasing excess energy with no change to Z or A.
Half-Life Half-life (t1/2) is the time for half of a radioactive sample to decay. Remaining amount: N = N₀(1/2)^(t/t1/2). Each isotope has a fixed, unchangeable half-life, independent of sample size or environmental conditions.
Example: identifying a decay type Uranium-238 (Z=92, A=238) decays to thorium-234 (Z=90, A=234). Z drops by 2 and A drops by 4 — this is alpha decay.
Example: applying the half-life formula A sample starts with 80 g of an isotope with a half-life of 10 years. After 30 years (3 half-lives): N = 80(1/2)^(30/10) = 80(1/2)³ = 80(1/8) = 10 g remaining.

Check your recall

1. Very large nuclei tend to be unstable because:

The strong force's very short range means it can't fully counteract electric repulsion once a nucleus grows large enough, leading to instability.

2. In beta decay, what happens to the atomic number Z and mass number A?

Beta decay converts a neutron to a proton (increasing Z by 1) while total nucleon count A is unchanged.

3. A sample starts with 100 g of an isotope with a half-life of 5 years. How much remains after 20 years?

20 years = 4 half-lives. N = 100(1/2)^4 = 100(1/16) = 6.25 g.

4. Fill in the blank: the fixed time it takes for half of a radioactive sample to decay is called its ___.

Half-life is fixed per isotope and independent of sample size or conditions.
