1. Find the energy of a photon with frequency 4×10¹⁴ Hz (h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s).

E = hf = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(4×10¹⁴) ≈ 2.65×10⁻¹⁹ J. Forgetting to multiply by f leaves just h; using f as the answer ignores Planck's constant entirely.

2. A metal's threshold frequency is f₀. Shining light with frequency slightly below f₀, but far more intense than before:

Each photon interacts individually with one electron. If a single photon's energy hf is below the work function, no amount of additional (still individually too-weak) photons can add up — there's no gradual accumulation.

3. Answer both parts about photons:

(a) Find the momentum of a photon with wavelength 400 nm.

(b) Fill in the blank: the principle that light (and matter) shows wave-like behavior in some experiments and particle-like behavior in others is called wave-particle ___.

(a) p = h/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/(400×10⁻⁹) ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s. (b) Wave-particle duality: both the wave and particle descriptions are simultaneously valid, each surfacing depending on what's measured.

4. Find the energy of the n = 3 level in hydrogen (Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n²).

E₃ = −13.6/3² = −13.6/9 ≈ −1.51 eV. Dividing by 3 instead of 9 gives −4.53; forgetting to divide gives the ground-state value −13.6; −0.85 eV is actually the n = 4 level.

5. Answer both parts about atomic spectra:

(a) Why does an isolated hydrogen atom emit light only at specific, discrete wavelengths rather than a continuous spectrum?

(b) An electron in hydrogen drops from n = 4 (E₄ = −0.85 eV) to n = 2 (E₂ = −3.4 eV). What is the energy of the emitted photon?

(a) Sharply defined, discrete energy levels mean only specific transition energies (and thus wavelengths) are possible. (b) E_photon = E₄ − E₂ = −0.85 − (−3.4) = 2.55 eV.

6. Fill in the blank: the experiment in which a photon collides with a free electron, exchanging energy and momentum exactly as in a particle collision, is called ___ scattering.

Compton scattering settled the photon-momentum question: the measured wavelength shift only matches predictions if the photon is treated as a real particle with momentum p = h/λ.

7. Answer both parts about the de Broglie wavelength:

(a) Why is an electron's de Broglie wavelength often large enough to observe (e.g., through crystal diffraction), while a thrown baseball's never is?

(b) Fill in the blank: an electron (m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg) moves at 5×10⁶ m/s. Its de Broglie wavelength is λ ≈ ___ picometers (1 pm = 10⁻¹² m; nearest whole number).

(a) λ = h/(mv) has mass in the denominator, so a baseball's huge mv gives an astronomically tiny wavelength, while an electron's tiny mass keeps λ in a measurable range. (b) λ = h/(mv) = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)/((9.11×10⁻³¹)(5×10⁶)) ≈ 1.45×10⁻¹⁰ m = 145 pm.

8. Answer both parts about radioactive decay:

(a) Polonium-210 (Z = 84, A = 210) decays into lead-206 (Z = 82, A = 206). What type of decay is this?

(b) A sample starts with 120 g of an isotope with a half-life of 8 years. How many grams remain after 32 years?

(a) Z drops by 2 (84→82) and A drops by 4 (210→206) — the signature of alpha decay (loss of a helium nucleus). (b) 32 years = 4 half-lives. N = 120(1/2)⁴ = 120/16 = 7.5 g.

9. Carbon-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons) and Carbon-14 (6 protons, 8 neutrons) are best described as:

Both have Z = 6 (6 protons, defining the element carbon) but differ in neutron count and therefore mass number A — exactly the definition of isotopes.

10. Answer both parts about mass-energy equivalence:

(a) Nuclear binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron (mass number ≈ 56). This explains why:

(b) Fill in the blank: a fusion reaction has a mass defect of 4×10⁻²⁸ kg. Using E = Δmc² (c = 3×10⁸ m/s), the energy released is ___ ×10⁻¹¹ J (just the decimal coefficient).

(a) Moving toward the peak binding energy per nucleon (near iron) releases energy — true when heavy nuclei split (fission) or light nuclei merge (fusion), but not the reverse. (b) E = Δmc² = (4×10⁻²⁸)(3×10⁸)² = (4×10⁻²⁸)(9×10¹⁶) = 3.6×10⁻¹¹ J.
