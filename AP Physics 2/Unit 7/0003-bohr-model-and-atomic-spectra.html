Bohr Model and Atomic Spectra

If energy comes in discrete packets, maybe electrons in an atom can only exist at discrete energy levels too — an idea that explained, for the first time, why atoms emit light only at very specific colors.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

The Bohr Model Niels Bohr proposed that an atom's electrons can only occupy specific, quantized energy levels — not any arbitrary energy. For hydrogen: En = −13.6 eV/n² (n = 1, 2, 3, ..., the principal quantum number), with n = 1 being the ground state (lowest, most negative energy).
Atomic Spectra An electron dropping from a higher energy level ni to a lower one nf emits a single photon with energy exactly equal to the difference: Ephoton = Ei − Ef = hf. Since only specific energy differences are allowed, atoms emit (and absorb) light only at specific, discrete wavelengths — a unique spectral fingerprint for each element.
Why atomic spectra are discrete lines, not a continuous rainbow A hot, glowing solid emits a continuous spread of every wavelength (blackbody radiation, Lesson 1) because its atoms are so densely packed and interacting that their energy levels blur together into an effective continuum. But an isolated atom (as in a low-pressure gas) has electrons confined to sharply defined energy levels with nothing in between — so the only photon energies (and therefore wavelengths) it can emit are the specific differences between its allowed levels. This is exactly why heating a gas produces a spectrum of sharp, separated bright lines rather than a smooth rainbow, and why each element's line pattern is as distinctive as a fingerprint.
Example: finding a hydrogen energy level Find the energy of the n = 2 level in hydrogen. E₂ = −13.6/2² = −13.6/4 = −3.4 eV.
Example: photon energy from a transition An electron drops from n = 3 (E₃ = −13.6/9 ≈ −1.51 eV) to n = 2 (E₂ = −3.4 eV). Ephoton = E₃ − E₂ = −1.51 − (−3.4) = 1.89 eV — this specific transition produces the well-known red line in hydrogen's visible spectrum.

1. In the Bohr model, an electron dropping from a higher to a lower energy level:

A single photon carries away energy exactly equal to Ei − Ef, matching hf.

2. Why does an isolated, low-pressure gas emit sharp spectral lines rather than a continuous rainbow?

Discrete, well-separated energy levels in an isolated atom mean only specific photon energies (and thus wavelengths) can be emitted or absorbed.

3. Find the energy of the n = 4 level in hydrogen.

E4 = −13.6/4² = −13.6/16 = −0.85 eV.

4. Fill in the blank: the lowest, most negative energy level of an atom (n = 1 for hydrogen) is called its ___.

The ground state is the atom's most stable, lowest-energy configuration.
