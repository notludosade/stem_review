Electric Charge and Coulomb's Law

Gravity only ever pulls — electric force can push or pull, and it's vastly stronger between everyday amounts of charge.

← Unit 1, Lesson 6 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Electric Charge Electric charge (unit: coulomb, C) comes in two types, positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge is conserved (never created or destroyed, only transferred) and quantized in units of the elementary charge, e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C — the charge of a single proton (+e) or electron (−e).
Coulomb's Law F = k|q₁q₂|/r², where k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C² is Coulomb's constant, q₁ and q₂ are the two charges, and r is the distance between them. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs, repulsive if they have the same sign, and always acts along the line connecting the two charges.
Why Coulomb's law looks just like Newton's law of gravitation Both are inverse-square laws: F ∝ 1/r², falling off with the square of distance because force spreads out over an ever-larger sphere as distance grows (the same geometric reasoning behind gravity, AP Physics 1). The key difference is that mass only ever attracts, while charge can either attract or repel depending on the signs involved — and the constant k is enormously larger relative to everyday quantities than gravitational G, which is why electric forces between even small charges dwarf gravitational forces between the same objects.
Example: force between two point charges Two charges, q₁ = +2×10⁻⁶ C and q₂ = −3×10⁻⁶ C, are separated by 0.5 m. F = k|q₁q₂|/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(2×10⁻⁶)(3×10⁻⁶)/(0.5)² = (8.99×10⁹)(6×10⁻¹²)/0.25 = 0.0539/0.25 ≈ 0.216 N — attractive, since the charges have opposite signs.
Example: how force changes with distance If the distance between two fixed charges is tripled, the force becomes F_new = k|q₁q₂|/(3r)² = (1/9)·k|q₁q₂|/r² — the force drops to 1/9 of its original value, a direct consequence of the inverse-square relationship.

1. Coulomb's law and Newton's law of gravitation are similar in that both are:

Both fall off with the square of separation distance, though gravity is always attractive while electric force can be either attractive or repulsive.

2. Two charges of the same sign are brought closer together, from 4 m apart to 1 m apart. How does the force between them change?

Distance drops by a factor of 4, and force scales as 1/r², so force increases by a factor of 4² = 16.

3. Two charges, q₁ = +4×10⁻⁶ C and q₂ = +1×10⁻⁶ C, are separated by 2 m. What is the magnitude of the force between them?

F = k|q₁q₂|/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(4×10⁻⁶)(1×10⁻⁶)/(2)² = (8.99×10⁹)(4×10⁻¹²)/4 = 0.03596/4 ≈ 0.00899 N.

4. Fill in the blank: the inverse-square law giving the force between two point charges, F = kq₁q₂/r², is called ___.

Coulomb's law mirrors Newton's law of gravitation in form, but allows for both attraction and repulsion.
