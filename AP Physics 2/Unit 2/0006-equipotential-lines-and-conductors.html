Equipotential Lines and Conductors

Every surface of a conductor in equilibrium sits at one single potential — and this single fact explains a whole cluster of conductor behaviors.

← Lesson 5 Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Equipotential Lines and Surfaces An equipotential line (in 2D) or surface (in 3D) connects all points at the same electric potential. Moving a charge along an equipotential requires zero work, since ΔV = 0 there. Equipotentials are always perpendicular to field lines at every point they cross.
Why equipotentials must be perpendicular to field lines If a field line had any component along an equipotential surface, moving a charge along that surface would involve a force component doing work — but by definition, moving along an equipotential does zero work (ΔV = 0 everywhere on it). The only way to guarantee zero work in every direction along the surface is for the field to have no component along it at all, meaning the field must point straight through the surface, perpendicular to it.
Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium In electrostatic equilibrium, a conductor has: (1) zero electric field inside it, (2) all excess charge residing on its outer surface, and (3) its entire surface at one constant potential.
Why a conductor's interior field must be zero Conductors contain free charges (electrons) that move in response to any field present. If an internal field existed, those free charges would keep accelerating in response to it — but "equilibrium" specifically means charges have already stopped rearranging. The only way for free charges to have stopped moving is for the internal field to have dropped to exactly zero, which is why charge migrates to the surface until it cancels any field inside completely. Since E = 0 throughout the interior, and potential differences require nonzero field to build up, the entire conductor — surface and interior alike — sits at one uniform potential.
Example: work along an equipotential A charge of +5×10⁻⁶ C moves along a circular equipotential surrounding a point charge, from one side to the other. Since both points are on the same equipotential, ΔV = 0, so W = qΔV = 0 J — no work is done, regardless of the path length traveled along that surface.
Example: applying conductor equilibrium A charged metal sphere in electrostatic equilibrium has 5×10⁻⁶ C of excess charge. Where does this charge reside, and what is the field at the sphere's center? All the excess charge resides on the outer surface (none in the interior), and the field at the center is exactly 0 N/C, consistent with the interior field being zero everywhere inside a conductor in equilibrium.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Electric field lines and equipotential surfaces always meet at:

Since moving along an equipotential does zero work, the field can have no component along the surface, forcing it to be perpendicular.

2. Why is the electric field exactly zero inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium?

Free charges redistribute to the surface until the internal field cancels to zero, which is precisely what "reaching equilibrium" means for a conductor.

3. A charge moves from one point to another, both lying on the same equipotential surface. How much work is done?

W = qΔV = 0 since ΔV = 0 between any two points on the same equipotential, no matter the path or charge sign.

4. Fill in the blank: a line or surface connecting all points at the same electric potential is called ___.

Equipotentials are always perpendicular to field lines, since moving along one does zero work.
