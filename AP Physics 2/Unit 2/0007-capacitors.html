Capacitors

Two parallel conducting plates can store electric energy the way a compressed spring stores mechanical energy — and this device is the foundation of Unit 3's circuits.

← Lesson 6 Unit 3, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Capacitance A capacitor is a device (typically two parallel conducting plates separated by a gap) that stores charge and electric energy. C = Q/V (unit: farad, F = C/V), where C is capacitance, Q is the magnitude of charge stored on each plate, and V is the potential difference between the plates. Capacitance depends only on the capacitor's geometry (plate area, separation), not on Q or V themselves.
Why capacitance is a fixed property of the device, not of how much charge happens to be stored C = Q/V looks like it depends on Q and V, but experimentally, doubling the charge placed on a capacitor's plates also doubles the voltage between them — their ratio stays constant. This is analogous to how a spring's stiffness constant k = F/x stays fixed regardless of how far you stretch it (AP Physics 1): C, like k, is a fixed property of the object's construction (plate area and separation, for a parallel-plate capacitor), while Q and V are the specific "how much" values that scale together for that device.
Energy Stored in a Capacitor U = (1/2)QV = (1/2)CV² — the electric potential energy stored between the plates. Unlike a point charge's potential energy, this accounts for the fact that voltage builds up gradually from 0 to V as charge accumulates, rather than acting on the full charge at the full voltage the whole time.
Example: finding capacitance A capacitor stores 4×10⁻⁴ C of charge when connected to a 12 V source. C = Q/V = (4×10⁻⁴)/12 ≈ 3.33×10⁻⁵ F = 33.3 μF.
Example: energy stored in a capacitor A 50 μF capacitor is charged to 20 V. U = (1/2)CV² = (1/2)(50×10⁻⁶)(20)² = (1/2)(50×10⁻⁶)(400) = 0.01 J.
