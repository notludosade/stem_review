Field of Multiple Charges and Field Lines

Fields add up like vectors — and the resulting field-line pattern between two charges reveals how they'll interact.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Superposition of Electric Fields The total electric field at a point due to multiple source charges is the vector sum of the individual fields each charge would create alone. Each charge's contribution is computed with E = kQ/r² pointing along the line from that charge to the point, then all contributions are added as vectors (accounting for direction, not just magnitude).
Why fields simply add (the principle of superposition) Electric fields don't interact with or block each other — each source charge creates its field completely independently of any other charges present, exactly as if it were alone. This means the net effect of many charges is found by simple vector addition, the same superposition principle used for combining multiple forces in AP Physics 1 — nothing new mathematically, just applied to a new quantity.
Why field lines never cross A field line represents the direction of the net field at every point along it. If two lines crossed, the field at that intersection would need to point in two different directions simultaneously — which is impossible, since the net field at any point is a single, well-defined vector (the vector sum described above). This is why field-line diagrams always show smooth, non-intersecting curves.
Example: field from two charges along a line Two charges, +4×10⁻⁶ C and +4×10⁻⁶ C, sit 0.4 m apart. Find the net field at the midpoint. Each charge is 0.2 m from the midpoint: E = kQ/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(4×10⁻⁶)/(0.2)² = 35,960/0.04 = 899,000 N/C from each charge. Since both charges are positive, their fields at the midpoint point in opposite directions (each pushing away from itself), so they cancel: E_net = 0 N/C.
Example: field from opposite charges at the midpoint Now suppose the two charges are +4×10⁻⁶ C and −4×10⁻⁶ C instead, same 0.4 m separation. Each still creates 899,000 N/C at the midpoint, but now both fields point the same direction (away from the positive charge, toward the negative charge) — so they add: E_net = 899,000 + 899,000 = 1,798,000 N/C, directed from the positive charge toward the negative one.

1. The electric field at a point due to multiple source charges is found by:

The principle of superposition means each charge's field contribution is added as a vector, since each source acts independently of the others.

2. Why can electric field lines never cross one another?

Since the net field is well-defined and single-valued at every point, field lines cannot cross without implying two directions at once.

3. Two equal positive charges sit symmetrically on either side of a point. What is the net field at that midpoint?

Each positive charge's field points away from itself, so at the midpoint between two equal positive charges, the two contributions point opposite directions and cancel exactly.

4. Fill in the blank: the principle stating that the net field from multiple charges is the vector sum of each charge's individual field is called ___.

Superposition applies because each source charge creates its field independently of any others present.
