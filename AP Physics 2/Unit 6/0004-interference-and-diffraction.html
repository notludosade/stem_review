Interference and Diffraction

Two of the clearest signatures that something is a wave, not a stream of particles: waves can combine to cancel each other out, and they bend around obstacles.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Superposition and Interference When two waves overlap, the resulting displacement is the sum of each wave's individual displacement (the principle of superposition, same idea as Unit 2's field superposition). Constructive interference (crest meets crest) produces a larger amplitude; destructive interference (crest meets trough) produces a smaller one, potentially canceling entirely.
Diffraction Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles or through openings. Diffraction is most noticeable when the opening or obstacle size is comparable to the wave's wavelength — a wave passing through a much larger opening barely bends at all, while one passing through an opening near its own wavelength spreads out dramatically.
Why light's diffraction is so much harder to notice than sound's Visible light has wavelengths around 500 nanometers, while sound wavelengths are on the order of centimeters to meters — everyday openings (doorways, room corners) are enormous compared to light's wavelength but comparable to sound's, which is exactly why you can hear around a corner but not see around one. Only when light passes through extremely narrow slits or small apertures (comparable to its wavelength) does its diffraction become obvious, which sets up the double-slit experiment in the next lesson.
Example: identifying constructive vs. destructive interference Two identical waves arrive at a point exactly in phase (crest aligns with crest). Their amplitudes add directly — this is constructive interference, producing a wave with twice the individual amplitude.
Example: path difference and interference type Two speakers emit identical in-phase sound waves of wavelength 2 m. At a point where one speaker's sound has traveled exactly 1 m farther than the other's, the path difference is 1 m = λ/2 — a half-wavelength offset means the waves arrive completely out of phase, producing destructive interference (a quiet spot).

1. The principle of superposition states that when two waves overlap, the resulting displacement is:

Superposition means displacements add directly — constructively when in phase, destructively when out of phase.

2. Why is light's diffraction much less noticeable in everyday life than sound's diffraction?

Diffraction is most noticeable when the opening size is comparable to the wavelength — true for sound at everyday doorway scales, but not for visible light.

3. Two in-phase waves of wavelength 4 m arrive at a point with a path difference of 2 m. What type of interference occurs?

2 m is exactly λ/2 for a 4 m wavelength — a half-wavelength offset always produces destructive interference for in-phase sources.

4. Fill in the blank: the bending of waves around obstacles or through narrow openings is called ___.

Diffraction is most pronounced when the opening or obstacle size is comparable to the wavelength.
