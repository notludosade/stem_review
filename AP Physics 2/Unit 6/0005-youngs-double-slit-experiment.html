Young's Double-Slit Experiment

One experiment, first done in 1801, settled a century-long argument by proving light behaves as a wave — and gave a precise way to measure its wavelength.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

The Setup Light passes through two narrow, closely spaced slits and lands on a screen some distance away. Each slit acts as a source of diffracted waves (Lesson 4), and these two waves overlap and interfere on the screen, producing an alternating pattern of bright and dark fringes.
The Double-Slit Equation Bright fringes (constructive interference) occur where d sin θ = mλ (m = 0, ±1, ±2, ...), where d is the slit separation, θ is the angle from the center to the fringe, and m is the order number. Dark fringes (destructive interference) occur at d sin θ = (m + 1/2)λ.
Why bright fringes appear only at specific angles Light from the two slits travels slightly different path lengths to reach any given point on the screen, and this path difference is exactly d sin θ (from the slit geometry). Where that path difference equals a whole number of wavelengths (mλ), the two waves arrive in phase and interfere constructively (bright fringe); where it equals a half-integer number of wavelengths, they arrive out of phase and cancel (dark fringe) — the same path-difference logic from Lesson 4's speaker example, just applied to light instead of sound.
Why this experiment proved light is a wave If light were purely a stream of particles, passing it through two slits should produce just two bright bands directly behind each slit, with no pattern in between. Instead, the experiment showed a repeating pattern of many bright and dark fringes — the unmistakable signature of interference, something only waves can do. This result, decades before Faraday's law or Maxwell's equations, was the first strong evidence that light is fundamentally a wave phenomenon (a picture only complicated later, in Unit 7, by the discovery that light also behaves like particles in certain situations).
Example: finding wavelength from fringe angle Slits separated by d = 0.02 mm produce a first-order (m = 1) bright fringe at θ = 1.5°. λ = d sin θ / m = (0.02×10⁻³)(sin 1.5°)/1 = (2×10⁻⁵)(0.0262) ≈ 5.24×10⁻⁷ m = 524 nm — in the visible green range.
Example: locating a dark fringe Using the same slits and light (λ = 524 nm), find the angle of the first dark fringe (m = 0 in the dark-fringe formula). d sin θ = (0 + 1/2)λ → sin θ = (0.5)(524×10⁻⁹)/(2×10⁻⁵) = 0.0131 → θ ≈ 0.75° — halfway between the central bright fringe and the first-order bright fringe, as expected.

1. Bright fringes in the double-slit experiment occur where the path difference between the two slits equals:

d sin θ = mλ (whole-number multiples of λ) gives constructive interference, hence bright fringes.

2. Why did Young's double-slit result convince physicists that light is a wave?

A pure particle stream would only produce two bright bands; the multi-fringe pattern is the signature of wave interference.

3. Slits separated by 0.01 mm produce a first-order bright fringe at θ = 3°. Find the wavelength.

λ = d sin θ/m = (1×10⁻⁵)(sin 3°)/1 = (1×10⁻⁵)(0.0523) ≈ 5.23×10⁻⁷ m ≈ 524 nm.

4. Fill in the blank: a bright fringe forms where the two slits' light waves arrive in phase, producing ___ interference.

In-phase arrival at a whole-number path difference produces constructive interference and a bright fringe.
