Diffraction Gratings and Thin-Film Interference

Scale the double slit up to thousands of slits, or shrink interference down to a soap bubble's skin, and the same wave logic explains a rainbow of new phenomena.

← Lesson 5 Unit 7, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Diffraction Gratings A diffraction grating is a surface with thousands of closely spaced slits or grooves per millimeter. It obeys the exact same equation as the double slit, d sin θ = mλ, but with d now the (much smaller) spacing between adjacent grating lines.
Why more slits produce sharper, brighter fringes than just two With only two slits, light from many different angles still contributes some brightness between the main fringes, blurring them. Add thousands of evenly-spaced slits, and only the exact angles satisfying d sin θ = mλ have light from every single slit arriving in phase simultaneously — any angle even slightly off causes waves from distant slits to be far enough out of phase to cancel completely. More slits means stricter cancellation everywhere except the precise constructive-interference angles, producing narrow, sharp, brilliant fringes instead of the softer bands of a double slit. This is why diffraction gratings (not double slits) are used in real spectrometers to precisely measure wavelengths.
Thin-Film Interference Light reflecting off the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film (a soap bubble, an oil slick) travels slightly different path lengths — the extra distance through the film (2t, twice the thickness) causes the two reflected waves to interfere, producing the shifting colors seen in soap bubbles and oil slicks as thickness and viewing angle vary.
Why thin films show colors, while thick films just look gray White light contains every visible wavelength at once, and the condition for constructive versus destructive interference (based on 2t compared to λ) depends on wavelength — so at any given thickness, some colors interfere constructively (appear bright) while others interfere destructively (are suppressed), and the surviving mix of colors is what you see reflected. In a thick film, the extra path length 2t is so large relative to any visible wavelength that the constructive/destructive condition changes almost randomly as thickness varies even slightly, averaging out to a uniform gray rather than clean, structured color bands.
Example: grating spacing from lines per millimeter A grating has 500 lines per millimeter. d = 1 mm/500 = 0.002 mm = 2×10⁻⁶ m. For green light (λ = 550 nm) at first order (m=1): sin θ = mλ/d = (550×10⁻⁹)/(2×10⁻⁶) = 0.275 → θ ≈ 16°.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why do diffraction gratings produce much sharper fringes than a simple double slit?

Thousands of slits enforce much stricter constructive-interference conditions, narrowing the bright fringes dramatically compared to just two slits.

2. Why does a thin soap film show colorful bands, while a thick piece of glass of similar material looks uniformly gray in reflected white light?

The 2t path difference relative to each wavelength changes so rapidly with thickness in a thick film that colors wash out into gray; a thin film keeps this stable enough to show clear bands.

3. A grating has 1000 lines per millimeter. What is its slit spacing d?

d = 1 mm/1000 = 0.001 mm = 1×10⁻⁶ m.

4. Fill in the blank: a surface with thousands of closely spaced slits, used to produce sharp interference fringes, is called a ___.

A diffraction grating obeys the same d sin θ = mλ equation as a double slit, with many more slits producing sharper fringes.
