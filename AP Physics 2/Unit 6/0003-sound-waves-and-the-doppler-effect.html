Sound Waves and the Doppler Effect

Sound is a longitudinal wave in a material medium — and when the source or listener is moving, the pitch you hear shifts in a way that reveals something deep about wavefronts.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Sound as a Longitudinal Wave Sound travels as alternating regions of compression (higher pressure) and rarefaction (lower pressure) through a medium (air, water, solids). Unlike light, sound requires a medium — it cannot travel through vacuum.
Why sound needs a medium but light doesn't Sound is literally particles of the medium bumping into their neighbors, transmitting a pressure disturbance — with no particles present (a vacuum), there's nothing to bump, so no sound wave can exist. Light, by contrast, is an electromagnetic wave (oscillating electric and magnetic fields, previewed in Unit 4 and formalized in Unit 7) that doesn't need any material substance to propagate — which is exactly why sunlight reaches Earth through the vacuum of space, but no one can hear an explosion in space.
The Doppler Effect When a sound source and listener are moving relative to each other, the observed frequency shifts: fobs = fsource (v ± vlistener)/(v ∓ vsource), where v is the speed of sound. Motion that decreases the distance between source and listener raises the observed frequency (higher pitch); motion that increases the distance lowers it.
Why approaching sources sound higher-pitched A moving source "crowds" wavefronts closer together on its leading (approaching) side, since it travels part of the way toward the listener between emitting one wave crest and the next — compressing the effective wavelength the listener receives. A shorter received wavelength means a higher observed frequency (from v = fλ, Lesson 1: smaller λ at fixed v gives larger f). Receding sources stretch the wavefronts apart instead, lowering the observed pitch — this is the classic effect of a passing ambulance siren.
Example: approaching source A train horn emits 400 Hz, approaching a stationary listener at 30 m/s (speed of sound v = 340 m/s). fobs = fsource · v/(v − vsource) = 400 · 340/(340−30) = 400 · 340/310 ≈ 439 Hz — higher than the emitted 400 Hz.
Example: receding source The same train now moves away at 30 m/s. fobs = fsource · v/(v + vsource) = 400 · 340/(370) ≈ 368 Hz — lower than 400 Hz, consistent with a receding source sounding lower-pitched.

1. Why can sound not travel through a vacuum, unlike light?

Sound needs particles to bump into each other; light is an electromagnetic wave that needs no medium at all.

2. A sound source approaching a stationary listener causes the listener to hear:

An approaching source compresses the wavefronts the listener receives, shortening the effective wavelength and raising the observed frequency.

3. A source emits 500 Hz while receding from a stationary listener at 20 m/s (v = 340 m/s). Approximately what frequency does the listener hear?

fobs = fsource · v/(v + vsource) = 500 · 340/360 ≈ 472 Hz — lower than the emitted frequency, as expected for a receding source.

4. Fill in the blank: the shift in observed frequency due to relative motion between a sound source and a listener is called the ___.

The Doppler effect explains why an ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched approaching and lower-pitched receding.
