Current, Resistance, and Ohm's Law

Charge flowing through a wire behaves a lot like water flowing through a pipe — and one simple equation ties flow rate, pressure, and resistance together.

← Unit 2, Lesson 7 Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Electric Current I = ΔQ/Δt (unit: ampere, A = C/s), the rate at which charge flows past a point. By convention, current direction is defined as the direction positive charge would flow, even in metals where it's actually negative electrons moving the opposite way.
Resistance and Ohm's Law Ohm's law: V = IR, where V is the potential difference across a component, I is current through it, and R is resistance (unit: ohm, Ω = V/A) — a measure of how strongly a material opposes current flow.
Why the water-pipe analogy works (and where it breaks down) Voltage plays the role of pressure difference driving flow, current plays the role of flow rate, and resistance plays the role of a pipe's narrowness restricting flow — a bigger pressure difference (V) drives more flow (I) through a given restriction (R), matching V = IR directly. The analogy breaks down in one place: water is incompressible and conserved in a simple flow, while charge conservation in a circuit is why current is the same at every point along a single wire with no branches (an idea used constantly in the next several lessons) — the pipe analogy captures the driving/resisting relationship well, but circuit current is really about a continuous conserved flow of charge, not literal fluid.
Resistivity R = ρL/A, where ρ is a material's resistivity (an intrinsic property, unit Ω·m), L is the conductor's length, and A is its cross-sectional area. Longer, thinner wires have more resistance; shorter, thicker wires have less.
Example: applying Ohm's law A resistor with R = 25 Ω has a 5 A current through it. V = IR = (5)(25) = 125 V — the potential difference across the resistor.
Example: resistance from resistivity A copper wire (ρ = 1.68×10⁻⁸ Ω·m) is 2 m long with a cross-sectional area of 1×10⁻⁶ m². R = ρL/A = (1.68×10⁻⁸)(2)/(1×10⁻⁶) = 3.36×10⁻⁸/1×10⁻⁶ = 0.0336 Ω — a very small resistance, typical of good conductors like copper.

1. In the water-pipe analogy for circuits, electric current corresponds to:

Current (charge flow rate) plays the same role as flow rate in the water analogy, with voltage as pressure and resistance as pipe narrowness.

2. Doubling a wire's length while keeping its cross-sectional area fixed will:

R = ρL/A is directly proportional to length, so doubling L doubles R.

3. A resistor has 12 V across it and 3 A of current through it. What is its resistance?

R = V/I = 12/3 = 4 Ω.

4. Fill in the blank: the relationship V = IR between voltage, current, and resistance is called ___.

Ohm's law holds for a wide range of materials called "ohmic" conductors, including most simple resistors.
