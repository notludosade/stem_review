Power in Circuits and RC Circuits

Every resistor dissipates energy as heat at a predictable rate — and adding a capacitor turns an instant on/off switch into a smooth, gradual charge and discharge.

← Lesson 5 Unit 4, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Electric Power P = IV = I²R = V²/R (unit: watt, W), the rate at which electrical energy is converted (usually to heat, in a resistor). These three equivalent forms come from combining P = IV with Ohm's law, useful depending on which two quantities are already known.
Why resistors get hot As charge carriers move through a resistor, they repeatedly collide with the material's atomic lattice, transferring kinetic energy to it with every collision — exactly the same friction-like energy dissipation covered conceptually in AP Physics 1, just occurring at the microscopic, atomic scale instead of between macroscopic surfaces. This lost electrical energy appears as heat, which is why resistors (and devices built from them, like toasters and incandescent bulbs) warm up during operation.
RC Circuits An RC circuit combines a resistor and capacitor. When charging, capacitor charge grows as q(t) = Qmax(1 − e−t/RC); when discharging, it decays as q(t) = Q₀e−t/RC. The time constant τ = RC sets how quickly this charging or discharging happens.
Why RC circuits charge and discharge gradually, not instantly As the capacitor accumulates charge, its own voltage rises and increasingly opposes further charge flowing in (recall V = Q/C from Lesson 7, Unit 2) — the closer the capacitor gets to fully charged, the weaker the net driving voltage across the resistor becomes, and the slower charging proceeds. This self-limiting feedback is exactly why charging isn't instantaneous but instead approaches its maximum gradually, slowing down as it gets closer, which the exponential e−t/RC term captures.
Example: power dissipated by a resistor A 20 Ω resistor carries 3 A of current. P = I²R = (3)²(20) = (9)(20) = 180 W.
Example: the RC time constant A 100 Ω resistor is in series with a 50 μF capacitor. τ = RC = (100)(50×10⁻⁶) = 5×10⁻³ s = 5 ms — after this much time, the capacitor has charged to about 63% of its maximum (since 1 − e⁻¹ ≈ 0.63).

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A resistor dissipates electrical energy as heat because:

Microscopic collisions between charge carriers and the lattice convert electrical energy to heat, the same friction-like dissipation idea from AP Physics 1.

2. Why does a charging capacitor in an RC circuit approach its maximum charge gradually rather than instantly?

The capacitor's rising voltage progressively opposes further charge flow, creating the self-limiting exponential approach to maximum charge.

3. A resistor has 10 V across it and a resistance of 5 Ω. What power does it dissipate?

P = V²/R = (10)²/5 = 100/5 = 20 W.

4. Fill in the blank: the quantity τ = RC, which sets the characteristic timescale of charging or discharging in an RC circuit, is called the ___.

After one time constant, a charging capacitor reaches about 63% of its maximum charge; a discharging one drops to about 37% of its initial charge.
