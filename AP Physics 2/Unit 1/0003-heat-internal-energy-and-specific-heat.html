Heat, Internal Energy, and Specific Heat

"Heat" isn't a substance stored inside a hot object — it's energy in transit, and different materials resist changing temperature by very different amounts.

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Heat and Internal Energy Internal energy is the total kinetic and potential energy of all the molecules within a substance. Heat, Q, is energy that flows between objects because of a temperature difference — heat is not something an object "contains," it's energy in the act of transferring, always flowing from hotter to colder.
Why "heat" and "temperature" are not the same thing Temperature reflects average molecular kinetic energy (Lesson 2); internal energy is the total across every molecule. A large pot of lukewarm water can have far more internal energy than a small ember at a much higher temperature, simply because it contains vastly more molecules. Heat is neither of these — it's specifically the energy that moves between two objects as a result of a temperature difference between them, and stops flowing once they reach thermal equilibrium (Lesson 1).
Specific Heat Capacity Q = mcΔT, where m is mass, c is the substance's specific heat capacity (J/(kg·°C)), and ΔT is the temperature change. Specific heat is how much energy is needed to raise one kilogram of a substance by one degree — water's is unusually high (c ≈ 4186 J/(kg·°C)) compared to most metals.
Why water's high specific heat matters Water resists temperature change far more than most materials do, which is why coastal climates are milder than inland ones at the same latitude — large bodies of water absorb or release enormous amounts of heat while changing temperature only slightly, moderating the surrounding air temperature across the seasons.
Example: heating water How much heat is needed to raise 2 kg of water from 20°C to 80°C? Q = mcΔT = (2)(4186)(60) = 502,320 J ≈ 502 kJ.
Example: mixing two substances (calorimetry) A 0.5 kg block of metal (c = 900 J/(kg·°C)) at 200°C is dropped into 1 kg of water (c = 4186 J/(kg·°C)) at 20°C, and the system reaches a common final temperature Tf with no heat lost to the surroundings. Heat lost by the metal equals heat gained by the water: (0.5)(900)(200 − Tf) = (1)(4186)(Tf − 20). Solving: 90,000 − 450Tf = 4186Tf − 83,720 ⟹ 173,720 = 4636Tf ⟹ Tf ≈ 37.5°C — much closer to water's starting temperature, since water's specific heat is so much larger.

1. Which statement correctly distinguishes heat from internal energy?

Heat is energy in transit due to a temperature difference; internal energy is the total energy a substance already contains at any given moment.

2. Why do coastal climates tend to have milder seasonal temperature swings than inland climates at the same latitude?

Because c is high for water, large quantities of heat correspond to only small temperature changes, moderating the surrounding air temperature.

3. How much heat is required to raise 3 kg of water from 10°C to 30°C?

Q = mcΔT = (3)(4186)(20) = 251,160 J.

4. Fill in the blank: the amount of heat needed to raise one kilogram of a substance by one degree Celsius is called its ___.

Specific heat capacity varies enormously between materials, with water's being unusually high.
