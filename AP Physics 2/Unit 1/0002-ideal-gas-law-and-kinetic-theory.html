Ideal Gas Law and Kinetic Theory

One equation ties together a gas's pressure, volume, and temperature — and it falls straight out of picturing gas molecules as tiny bouncing balls.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

The Ideal Gas Law PV = nRT, where P is pressure (Pa), V is volume (m³), n is the number of moles, T is temperature (K), and R = 8.31 J/(mol·K) is the universal gas constant. This describes an idealized gas well at ordinary densities and temperatures.
Where the ideal gas law comes from: kinetic theory Kinetic theory pictures a gas as a huge number of tiny particles in constant, random motion, colliding elastically with each other and the container walls. Pressure is the cumulative effect of countless molecular collisions against the walls per unit area; temperature turns out to be a direct measure of the molecules' average kinetic energy. Squeezing the same number of molecules into a smaller volume (smaller V) packs in more collisions per unit area (higher P) for fixed T — exactly the inverse relationship PV = nRT captures.
Average Kinetic Energy of Gas Molecules KEavg = (3/2)kBT, where kB = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant. Average molecular kinetic energy depends only on temperature — not on the type of gas, its pressure, or its volume.
Why temperature is really just a measure of molecular motion This is the deepest idea kinetic theory offers: temperature isn't a separate, independent property of matter — it's a direct readout of how fast molecules are jostling around on average. Raising a gas's temperature means literally speeding up its molecules; at absolute zero (0 K), this formula gives zero average kinetic energy, matching Lesson 1's description of absolute zero as minimal molecular motion.
Example: finding pressure from the ideal gas law A rigid tank holds 2 mol of gas at temperature 300 K in a volume of 0.05 m³. P = nRT/V = (2)(8.31)(300)/0.05 = 4986/0.05 = 99,720 Pa.
Example: comparing two gases at the same temperature A sample of helium and a sample of oxygen are both at 400 K. Despite oxygen molecules being about 8 times more massive than helium atoms, KEavg = (3/2)kB(400) is identical for both gases — average kinetic energy depends only on temperature, so the (much lighter) helium atoms must be moving much faster on average to have the same average kinetic energy as the heavier oxygen molecules.
