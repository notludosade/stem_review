Unit 1: Thermodynamics

Lesson 1

Temperature, Thermal Equilibrium, and the Zeroth Law

Before heat can be measured, "same temperature" has to be defined precisely — and that definition turns out to need its own law.

 Lesson 2

Ideal Gas Law and Kinetic Theory

One equation ties together a gas's pressure, volume, and temperature — and it falls straight out of picturing gas molecules as tiny bouncing balls.

 Lesson 3

Heat, Internal Energy, and Specific Heat

"Heat" isn't a substance stored inside a hot object — it's energy in transit, and different materials resist changing temperature by very different amounts.

 Lesson 4

Phase Changes and Latent Heat

Ice at 0°C and water at 0°C are wildly different amounts of energy away from each other — even though a thermometer can't tell them apart.

 Lesson 5

The First Law of Thermodynamics

Energy conservation, one more time — now tracking heat and work flowing into and out of a gas instead of a single object's motion.

 Lesson 6

The Second Law of Thermodynamics, Entropy, and Heat Engines

Why no engine is perfectly efficient, why heat never flows uphill on its own, and why a broken egg never unbreaks itself.

Unit 2: Electric Force, Field, and Potential

Lesson 1

Electric Charge and Coulomb's Law

Gravity only ever pulls — electric force can push or pull, and it's vastly stronger between everyday amounts of charge.

 Lesson 2

The Electric Field

Instead of tracking force between two specific charges, define a field that fills space — then any charge dropped in feels a force immediately.

 Lesson 3

Field of Multiple Charges and Field Lines

Fields add up like vectors — and the resulting field-line pattern between two charges reveals how they'll interact.

 Lesson 4

Electric Potential Energy

Pushing two like charges together stores energy in their configuration, exactly the way lifting a mass stores gravitational potential energy.

 Lesson 5

Electric Potential

"Voltage" is potential energy per unit charge — a property of a location in space, not of any particular charge sitting there.

 Lesson 6

Equipotential Lines and Conductors

Every surface of a conductor in equilibrium sits at one single potential — and this single fact explains a whole cluster of conductor behaviors.

 Lesson 7

Capacitors

Two parallel conducting plates can store electric energy the way a compressed spring stores mechanical energy — and this device is the foundation of Unit 3's circuits.

Unit 3: Electric Circuits

Lesson 1

Current, Resistance, and Ohm's Law

Charge flowing through a wire behaves a lot like water flowing through a pipe — and one simple equation ties flow rate, pressure, and resistance together.

 Lesson 2

EMF and Simple Circuits

A battery doesn't supply current directly — it supplies the "push" that drives current around a closed loop, and it always has some cost of doing business.

 Lesson 3

Series Circuits

One path, one current — but voltage splits up as it drops across each resistor in turn.

 Lesson 4

Parallel Circuits

Multiple paths share the same voltage but split the current — and adding more paths only ever makes it easier for current to flow.

 Lesson 5

Kirchhoff's Rules and Combination Circuits

Two bookkeeping rules — charge in equals charge out, and energy gained equals energy lost — solve any circuit, no matter how tangled.

 Lesson 6

Power in Circuits and RC Circuits

Every resistor dissipates energy as heat at a predictable rate — and adding a capacitor turns an instant on/off switch into a smooth, gradual charge and discharge.

Unit 4: Magnetism and Electromagnetic Induction

Lesson 1

Magnetic Fields and Force on Moving Charges

A stationary charge never feels a magnetic force — only a moving one does, and the force always points sideways to the motion.

 Lesson 2

Force on Current-Carrying Wires

A wire carrying current is just a coordinated stream of moving charges — so it feels a magnetic force too, scaled up to a whole conductor.

 Lesson 3

Magnetic Fields from Currents

Every current creates its own magnetic field circling around it — the source of magnetism turns out to be moving charge, not some separate "magnetic charge."

 Lesson 4

Faraday's Law

A changing magnetic field creates an electric current out of nowhere — the discovery that makes generators, transformers, and induction cooktops possible.

 Lesson 5

Lenz's Law

Induced currents always fight back against the change that created them — nature's way of enforcing energy conservation in electromagnetism.

 Lesson 6

Generators, Motors, and Transformers

Three of the most important machines in the modern world all come from combining just a few ideas from this unit in different orders.

Unit 5: Geometric Optics

Lesson 1

Reflection and Plane Mirrors

Unit 4 was about invisible fields bending charges and currents. Unit 5 turns to something you can see directly: light bouncing off surfaces in perfectly predictable ways.

 Lesson 2

Refraction and Snell's Law

Light doesn't just bounce off surfaces — it also bends when it crosses from one material into another, and by a perfectly predictable amount.

 Lesson 3

Total Internal Reflection and Fiber Optics

Push Snell's law far enough and refraction simply stops happening — light gets trapped inside a medium entirely, which is exactly how a fiber optic cable moves data at the speed of light.

 Lesson 4

Curved Mirrors

Curve the reflecting surface and the same law of reflection from Lesson 1 now produces real, magnified, or shrunken images — the basis of telescopes, headlights, and makeup mirrors alike.

 Lesson 5

Thin Lenses

Lenses use refraction instead of reflection to form images — but the same equation from Lesson 4 describes them almost unchanged. The eye, the camera, and the microscope all come down to this.

Unit 6: Waves, Sound, and Physical Optics

Lesson 1

Wave Properties

Unit 5 treated light as rays traveling in straight lines. That model breaks down at small scales — to explain interference and diffraction, light needs to be understood as a wave.

 Lesson 2

Standing Waves and Resonance

Trap a wave between two fixed points and something new happens — it stops traveling and instead appears to oscillate in place, at only certain special frequencies.

 Lesson 3

Sound Waves and the Doppler Effect

Sound is a longitudinal wave in a material medium — and when the source or listener is moving, the pitch you hear shifts in a way that reveals something deep about wavefronts.

 Lesson 4

Interference and Diffraction

Two of the clearest signatures that something is a wave, not a stream of particles: waves can combine to cancel each other out, and they bend around obstacles.

 Lesson 5

Young's Double-Slit Experiment

One experiment, first done in 1801, settled a century-long argument by proving light behaves as a wave — and gave a precise way to measure its wavelength.

 Lesson 6

Diffraction Gratings and Thin-Film Interference

Scale the double slit up to thousands of slits, or shrink interference down to a soap bubble's skin, and the same wave logic explains a rainbow of new phenomena.

Unit 7: Modern Physics

Lesson 1

Blackbody Radiation and the Photoelectric Effect

Two puzzles physicists couldn't explain with wave theory around 1900 both had the same surprising resolution: energy comes in discrete chunks, not a continuous stream.

 Lesson 2

Photons and Wave-Particle Duality

Unit 6 proved light is a wave. Lesson 1 just showed light also behaves like discrete particles. Both are true — this lesson makes peace between them.

 Lesson 3

Bohr Model and Atomic Spectra

If energy comes in discrete packets, maybe electrons in an atom can only exist at discrete energy levels too — an idea that explained, for the first time, why atoms emit light only at very specific colors.

 Lesson 4

Compton Scattering and de Broglie Wavelength

Photons carrying momentum like particles was the final proof light isn't purely a wave. Then de Broglie asked the reverse question: what if matter has wave properties too?

 Lesson 5

Nuclear Structure and Radioactivity

Zoom into the atom's nucleus itself, and quantization shows up yet again — this time governing which nuclei are stable and which spontaneously fall apart.

 Lesson 6

Nuclear Reactions: Fission and Fusion

Splitting or joining nuclei releases energy on a scale no chemical reaction can match — the closing idea of the course, and the same E = mc² that ties mass and energy together at the deepest level.
