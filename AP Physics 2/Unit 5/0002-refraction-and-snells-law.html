Refraction and Snell's Law

Light doesn't just bounce off surfaces — it also bends when it crosses from one material into another, and by a perfectly predictable amount.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Index of Refraction n = c/v, where c is the speed of light in vacuum and v is the speed of light in a given material. Every transparent material has its own n ≥ 1 (light always slows down when entering a material — it never speeds up past c).
Snell's Law n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2, where θ1 and θ2 are the angles of incidence and refraction (both from the normal), and n1, n2 are the indices of refraction of the two materials.
Why light bends at all when it changes speed Think of light as a wavefront, like a marching line of soldiers crossing at an angle from pavement onto sand — the soldiers hitting the sand first slow down while the rest are still moving at full speed on pavement, so the line pivots. Light does the same: the part of the wavefront entering the slower (higher-n) medium first is "held back" while the rest catches up, bending the whole wavefront's direction toward the normal. This is why light bends toward the normal entering a denser medium (slowing down) and away from the normal entering a less dense one (speeding up).
Example: applying Snell's law Light travels from air (n = 1.00) into glass (n = 1.5) at an incidence angle of 40°. n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ → (1.00)(sin 40°) = (1.5) sin θ₂ → sin θ₂ = 0.643/1.5 = 0.429 → θ₂ ≈ 25.4° — bent toward the normal, as expected entering a denser medium.
Example: finding an unknown index of refraction Light hits a material at 30° from air and refracts to 20°. n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ → (1.00)(sin 30°) = n₂(sin 20°) → n₂ = 0.5/0.342 ≈ 1.46.
