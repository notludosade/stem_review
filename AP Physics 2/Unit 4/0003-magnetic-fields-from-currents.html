Magnetic Fields from Currents

Every current creates its own magnetic field circling around it — the source of magnetism turns out to be moving charge, not some separate "magnetic charge."

← Lesson 2 Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Field Around a Long Straight Wire B = μ₀I/(2πr), where μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A is the permeability of free space, I is current, and r is distance from the wire. Field lines form circles around the wire, direction given by a second right-hand rule: thumb points along current, fingers curl in the direction of B.
Why field lines circle the wire instead of radiating outward Unlike a point charge's field (which radiates straight outward, Unit 2), a current has an inherent direction along the wire — the field pattern that results must respect this directionality, and the only field-line shape consistent with a straight, directional current source is a set of circles wrapped around the wire's axis, decreasing in strength with distance (1/r, one power weaker than the point-charge field's 1/r² since the source here is an infinite line rather than a single point).
Field Inside a Solenoid A solenoid (a tightly wound coil of wire) produces a strong, uniform field inside it: B = μ₀nI, where n is the number of turns per unit length. This is how electromagnets are built — a coil concentrates and strengthens the field compared to a single straight wire.
Example: field from a straight wire Find the magnetic field 0.1 m from a wire carrying 8 A of current. B = μ₀I/(2πr) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(8)/(2π(0.1)) = (4×10⁻⁷)(8)/(0.2) = 3.2×10⁻⁶/0.2 = 1.6×10⁻⁵ T.
Example: field inside a solenoid A solenoid has 2000 turns per meter and carries 3 A of current. B = μ₀nI = (4π×10⁻⁷)(2000)(3) ≈ (1.2566×10⁻⁶)(2000)(3) ≈ 7.54×10⁻³ T — much stronger than a single straight wire's field at a comparable distance, since a solenoid's many loops reinforce each other.

1. The magnetic field lines around a long, straight current-carrying wire form:

Since the current has an inherent direction along the wire, the resulting field must circle around it, following the second right-hand rule.

2. Why does a solenoid produce a much stronger field than a single straight wire at a similar distance?

B = μ0nI shows the field scales with the number of turns per length, since each loop's field adds to the others.

3. Find the magnetic field 0.2 m from a wire carrying 10 A of current.

B = μ0I/(2πr) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(10)/(2π(0.2)) = (4×10⁻⁷)(10)/(0.4) = 4×10⁻⁶/0.4 = 1×10⁻⁵ T.

4. Fill in the blank: a tightly wound coil of wire that produces a strong, uniform magnetic field inside it is called a ___.

A solenoid's field, B = μ0nI, is the basis for electromagnets.
