Generators, Motors, and Transformers

Three of the most important machines in the modern world all come from combining just a few ideas from this unit in different orders.

← Lesson 5 Unit 5, Lesson 1 → Contents Glossary →

Generators A generator rotates a loop of wire within a magnetic field (mechanical energy in), continuously changing the angle θ between the loop and field, which continuously changes flux and induces an alternating EMF: ε = εmax sin(ωt), where ω is the rotation's angular frequency.
Why generators produce alternating (not constant) current As the loop rotates at constant angular speed, the angle θ between the loop's normal and the field cycles smoothly, so flux ΦB = BA cos θ rises and falls in a repeating pattern rather than changing at a constant rate — Faraday's law then produces an EMF that similarly oscillates, sinusoidally, back and forth in sign as the loop spins past the orientations where it's cutting through field lines fastest versus slowest. This is exactly why household electricity is alternating current (AC): it comes from rotating generators at power plants.
Motors A motor runs the generator concept in reverse: current is supplied to a loop in a magnetic field (electrical energy in), and the resulting force on the current-carrying loop (Lesson 2) causes it to rotate (mechanical energy out). A generator converts motion to electricity; a motor converts electricity to motion — the same physical setup, opposite energy-flow direction.
Transformers A transformer uses two coils sharing a common iron core: an alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing flux, which by Faraday's law induces an EMF in the secondary coil. Vs/Vp = Ns/Np, where N is the number of turns in each coil — more turns in the secondary steps voltage up; fewer steps it down.
Why transformers only work with alternating, not direct, current A transformer relies entirely on Faraday's law, which requires a changing flux to induce anything (Lesson 4) — a steady direct current produces a constant flux with no induced EMF in the secondary coil at all. Alternating current's continuously changing magnitude and direction is precisely what keeps the flux perpetually changing, making continuous induction (and therefore continuous power transfer) possible.
Example: transformer voltage ratio A transformer's primary coil has 100 turns and receives 120 V. Its secondary coil has 500 turns. Vs = Vp(Ns/Np) = 120(500/100) = 120(5) = 600 V — a step-up transformer.
Example: identifying a step-down transformer A transformer steps 2400 V down to 120 V using a primary coil of 4000 turns. Find the secondary turns. Ns = Np(Vs/Vp) = 4000(120/2400) = 4000(0.05) = 200 turns — far fewer turns than the primary, consistent with stepping voltage down.

1. A generator produces alternating (rather than constant) current because:

Continuous rotation cycles the flux through the loop, producing a sinusoidally oscillating induced EMF via Faraday's law.

2. A motor and a generator share the same basic physical setup, but differ in that:

A generator runs motion-to-electricity, a motor runs electricity-to-motion — the same underlying loop-and-field configuration in opposite energy-flow directions.

3. Why can't a transformer step voltage up or down using direct current?

Without changing flux, there is no induced EMF in the secondary coil regardless of the turns ratio.

4. Fill in the blank: a device using two coils and a shared iron core to step AC voltage up or down via mutual induction is called a ___.

The voltage ratio Vs/Vp = Ns/Np depends entirely on the relative number of turns in each coil.
