Thermodynamics

Thermal equilibrium

The state two objects reach when they stop exchanging net heat — their temperatures are equal.

Zeroth law of thermodynamics

If two objects are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other — what makes temperature a well-defined, comparable quantity.

Kelvin scale, absolute zero

The temperature scale required in gas-law and thermal equations, since it starts at absolute zero (0 K, where molecular motion is minimized) rather than an arbitrary reference point.

Ideal gas law, PV = nRT

Relates a gas's pressure, volume, moles, and absolute temperature, with R the universal gas constant.

Internal energy, Heat (Q)

Internal energy is the total kinetic and potential energy of a substance's molecules. Heat is energy transferred between objects due to a temperature difference — not a substance stored inside an object.

Specific heat capacity, Q = mcΔT

The heat required to change a substance's temperature, scaled by its mass and its specific heat capacity c — a property that differs by material.

Latent heat, Q = mL

The heat absorbed or released during a phase change at constant temperature, with no temperature change during the transition itself.

First law of thermodynamics, ΔU = Q − W

A system's change in internal energy equals heat added to it minus work done by it — energy conservation applied to thermal systems.

Isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric processes

Special thermodynamic processes: isothermal (constant temperature, ΔU = 0), adiabatic (no heat exchange, Q = 0), isochoric (constant volume, W = 0).

Entropy, second law of thermodynamics

Entropy is a measure of a system's disorder. The second law states total entropy of an isolated system never decreases — giving the universe a preferred direction in time.

Heat engine

A device converting heat into work by moving heat from a hot reservoir to a cold one. No heat engine can be 100% efficient, a direct consequence of the second law.

Electric Force, Field, and Potential

Electric charge

A fundamental property of matter producing electric and magnetic effects, existing in two types (positive and negative) that attract or repel according to sign.

Coulomb's law, F = k|q₁q₂|/r²

The electric force between two point charges, directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them — the same inverse-square form as Newton's law of gravitation.

Electric field, E = F/q = kQ/r²

The force per unit charge that would be experienced by a small test charge at a given point in space, defined independently of any particular test charge actually being there.

Superposition (of electric fields)

The net electric field from multiple charges is the vector sum of the individual fields each charge would produce alone.

Electric potential energy, U = kq₁q₂/r

Stored energy of a pair of charges due to their separation, directly paralleling gravitational potential energy.

Electric potential, V = U/q = kQ/r

Potential energy per unit charge at a point in space. Potential difference (voltage) is the change in V between two points, independent of the path taken between them.

Equipotential lines/surfaces

Lines or surfaces along which electric potential is constant everywhere; no work is done moving a charge along one, and they are always perpendicular to field lines.

Capacitor, capacitance, C = Q/V

A capacitor stores charge (and energy) on two separated conductors. Capacitance measures how much charge it stores per volt applied. Stored energy: U = ½QV = ½CV².

Electric Circuits

Current, I = ΔQ/Δt

The rate at which charge flows past a point in a circuit, measured in amperes.

Ohm's law, V = IR

The voltage across a resistor equals the current through it times its resistance — holding for ohmic materials over a wide range of conditions.

Resistance, resistivity, R = ρL/A

Resistance opposes current flow, depending on a material's resistivity (an intrinsic property), its length, and its cross-sectional area.

EMF (ε), V = ε − Ir

Electromotive force is the energy per unit charge a source (like a battery) supplies. Terminal voltage is EMF minus the drop across the source's own internal resistance r.

Series circuit

Components connected end-to-end along a single path, so the same current flows through each, and voltages across each component add to the total.

Parallel circuit

Components connected across the same two nodes, so each shares the same voltage, and currents through each branch add to the total.

Kirchhoff's rules

The junction rule: current into a node equals current out. The loop rule: Σ(voltage changes around loop) = 0 — both express conservation of charge and energy in a circuit.

Power in circuits, P = IV = I²R = V²/R

The rate at which electrical energy is delivered to or dissipated by a circuit element, expressible in three equivalent forms via Ohm's law.

RC circuit, time constant τ = RC

A circuit with a resistor and capacitor whose charging or discharging follows an exponential curve; τ sets the characteristic time scale of that process.

Magnetism and Electromagnetic Induction

Magnetic force on a moving charge, F = qvB sin θ

A charge moving through a magnetic field feels a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field, found using the right-hand rule.

Force on a current-carrying wire, F = BIL sin θ

A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force, since current is simply many moving charges — the same right-hand-rule geometry as a single moving charge.

Magnetic field from a current, B = μ₀I/(2πr)

A current-carrying wire itself generates a magnetic field circling the wire. A solenoid (tightly wound coil) produces a strong, nearly uniform field inside: B = μ₀nI.

Faraday's law, ε = −N(ΔΦ_B/Δt)

A changing magnetic flux through a loop induces an EMF (and therefore a current, if the loop is part of a closed circuit), proportional to how fast the flux changes and the number of loops N.

Lenz's law

The induced current's direction always opposes the change in flux that created it — the source of the minus sign in Faraday's law, and a direct consequence of energy conservation.

Generator, motor, transformer

A generator converts mechanical motion into EMF via changing flux (Faraday's law); a motor reverses this, using current in a magnetic field to produce motion. A transformer changes AC voltage between two coils via V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p, requiring changing flux and therefore working only with AC.

Geometric Optics

Law of reflection

The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal (a line perpendicular to the surface).

Plane mirror image

A virtual, upright, same-size image formed behind a flat mirror — virtual because the reflected rays only appear to diverge from behind the mirror, never actually passing through that point.

Index of refraction, n = c/v

The ratio of light's speed in vacuum to its speed in a given medium, always ≥ 1.

Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

Relates the angles of incidence and refraction to the refractive indices of the two media a light ray crosses.

Critical angle, total internal reflection

The critical angle is the incidence angle (going from a denser to a less-dense medium) beyond which light no longer refracts out at all, but reflects entirely back into the denser medium — total internal reflection, the principle behind fiber optics.

Concave and convex mirrors

Concave mirrors curve inward and can form real or virtual images depending on object distance; convex mirrors curve outward and can only ever form virtual, upright, reduced images.

Mirror/thin-lens equation, 1/d₀ + 1/dᵢ = 1/f

Relates object distance, image distance, and focal length — the same equation form for both curved mirrors and thin lenses, with only sign conventions differing.

Magnification, m = −dᵢ/d₀

The ratio of image height to object height; negative m means an inverted image, |m| > 1 means enlarged.

Converging and diverging lenses

A converging (convex) lens bends parallel rays toward a single focal point; a diverging (concave) lens spreads them apart as if radiating from a virtual focal point on the incoming side.

Waves, Sound, and Physical Optics

Amplitude, wavelength, period, frequency

Amplitude is a wave's maximum displacement from equilibrium; wavelength is the distance between repeating points; period is the time for one full cycle; frequency is cycles per unit time.

Wave speed, v = fλ

Relates a wave's speed to its frequency and wavelength — true for any periodic wave.

Transverse and longitudinal waves

In a transverse wave, particle motion is perpendicular to wave travel (like light or a wave on a string). In a longitudinal wave, particle motion is parallel to wave travel (like sound), requiring a medium.

Standing wave, nodes, antinodes

A standing wave forms from two identical waves traveling in opposite directions, creating stationary points of zero displacement (nodes) and maximum displacement (antinodes).

Resonance

The dramatic buildup of standing-wave amplitude when a system is driven at one of its natural harmonic frequencies.

Doppler effect

The shift in observed frequency of a wave caused by relative motion between source and observer — higher pitch when approaching, lower when receding.

Principle of superposition, constructive/destructive interference

Overlapping waves add algebraically at every point. Constructive interference occurs where waves add to a larger amplitude; destructive interference where they cancel.

Diffraction

The bending of waves around obstacles or through openings, most noticeable when the obstacle or opening size is comparable to the wavelength.

Double-slit equation, d sin θ = mλ (bright), d sin θ = (m + 1/2)λ (dark)

Predicts the angles of bright (constructive) and dark (destructive) fringes in Young's double-slit experiment, proving light behaves as a wave.

Diffraction grating

A surface with many closely spaced slits, using the same d sin θ = mλ relation as a double slit but producing much sharper, brighter fringes due to many-slit interference.

Thin-film interference

Interference between light reflecting off the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film, producing visible colors when the film thickness is comparable to a wavelength of light.

Modern Physics

Planck's hypothesis, E = hf

Energy is emitted or absorbed only in discrete packets (quanta) proportional to frequency, resolving the ultraviolet catastrophe of classical blackbody radiation.

Photoelectric effect, KE_max = hf − φ

Light striking a metal ejects electrons only above a threshold frequency, regardless of intensity — proof that light delivers energy in discrete photons, not a continuous wave. φ is the work function, the minimum energy needed to eject an electron.

Photon, p = h/λ

A discrete packet (quantum) of light energy, carrying energy E = hf and momentum p = h/λ.

Wave-particle duality

The principle that light (and matter) displays wave-like behavior (interference, diffraction) in some experiments and particle-like behavior (discrete energy exchange, momentum) in others — both simultaneously valid.

Bohr model, Eₙ = −13.6 eV/n²

An atom's electrons occupy only specific, quantized energy levels; n = 1 is the ground state, the lowest and most stable energy level.

Atomic spectra

The discrete set of wavelengths an element emits or absorbs, corresponding to transitions between its quantized energy levels — a unique spectral fingerprint per element.

Compton scattering

The scattering of a photon off a free electron with measurable wavelength shift, proving photons carry real, particle-like momentum obeying ordinary collision physics.

De Broglie wavelength, λ = h/(mv)

Every moving mass has an associated wavelength, extending wave-particle duality from light to matter — observable for particles as light as electrons, but unmeasurably small for everyday objects.

Nuclear structure: protons, neutrons, Z, A

A nucleus contains protons and neutrons (together, nucleons). Atomic number Z is the proton count (defines the element); mass number A is the total nucleon count. Isotopes share Z but differ in A.

Strong nuclear force

A short-range attractive force holding the nucleus together against proton-proton Coulomb repulsion; its limited range makes very large nuclei unstable.

Radioactive decay: alpha, beta, gamma

Alpha decay emits a helium nucleus (Z −2, A −4). Beta decay converts a neutron to a proton, emitting an electron (Z +1, A unchanged). Gamma decay emits a high-energy photon (Z, A unchanged).

Half-life, N = N₀(1/2)^(t/t½)

The fixed time for half of a radioactive sample to decay, independent of sample size or environmental conditions.

Mass-energy equivalence, E = Δmc²

Mass and energy are interchangeable; a nuclear reaction's "missing" mass (mass defect, Δm) is released as energy.

Fission and fusion

Fission splits a heavy nucleus into lighter ones; fusion joins light nuclei into a heavier one. Both release energy because both move the resulting nuclei's binding energy per nucleon closer to its peak near iron.
