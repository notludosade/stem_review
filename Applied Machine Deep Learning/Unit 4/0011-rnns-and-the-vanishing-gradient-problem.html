RNNs and the Vanishing Gradient Problem

AI Developer's Lesson 16 named vanishing gradients as the "historically important problem" behind why LSTMs exist, without deriving why they actually happen. This lesson does the derivation — the same backpropagation from Unit 2, just run across time instead of across layers.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

A recurrent neural network updates its hidden state at every time step with the same recurrence, applied over and over: h_t = tanh(W_hh · h_{t−1} + W_xh · x_t + b). Same weights, every step — exactly the parameter sharing Lesson 7 introduced for images, just shared across time instead of across space. Training this requires backpropagation through time (BPTT): unroll the recurrence into what looks like an ordinary deep network, one layer per time step, and backpropagate through all of it, exactly as Unit 2 covered.

Python — why the gradient shrinks over many steps 
import numpy as np

def tanh_derivative(h):
    return 1 - h ** 2   # tanh'(z) = 1 - tanh(z)^2, maximum value 1, at z = 0

# Backpropagating the loss gradient back through 20 time steps
gradient = 1.0
for step_back in range(20):
    local_factor = tanh_derivative(0.5) * 0.9   # a typical per-step factor, well under 1
    gradient *= local_factor
    if step_back in (0, 4, 9, 19):
        print(step_back + 1, 'steps back:', gradient)

# 1 steps back:  0.675
# 5 steps back:  0.1385
# 10 steps back: 0.01917
# 20 steps back: 0.0003676   -- already nearly zero

Every step back through time multiplies the running gradient by another local factor — tanh's own derivative, times whatever the recurrent weight matrix contributes. tanh's derivative maxes out at exactly 1 (at input 0) and is smaller everywhere else, so a typical per-step factor lands well under 1. Multiplying dozens of numbers under 1 together is exactly how a vanishing gradient happens: not from any single broken step, but from BPTT's chain rule compounding many small shrinkages into one that's effectively zero by the time it reaches an early time step. The opposite failure, an exploding gradient, happens the same way in reverse — if the per-step factor exceeds 1, the product grows without bound instead. Gradient clipping — capping a gradient's magnitude before it's used to update weights — is a direct, practical fix for exploding gradients specifically; vanishing gradients need a different kind of fix, which is exactly what Lesson 12 covers.

Definition Backpropagation through time (BPTT) unrolls a recurrence across time steps and backpropagates through the result like an ordinary deep network. A vanishing gradient results from multiplying many per-step factors under 1 together across many time steps. An exploding gradient is the same compounding effect with factors over 1. Gradient clipping caps a gradient's magnitude before the weight update, directly addressing exploding gradients.
Why a plain RNN's failure mode is usually vanishing, not exploding tanh's derivative can never exceed 1, and most of the time it's meaningfully smaller than that (its output is only near 0, where the derivative peaks, for part of training). That caps the typical per-step factor below 1 far more often than above it, which is why plain RNNs earned their reputation for effectively forgetting long-range context specifically — not for the more dramatic instability of exploding, which tends to show up only when the recurrent weight matrix itself has grown unusually large, and is comparatively easy to catch and clip.

Go to the source

Pascanu, Mikolov, and Bengio's "On the difficulty of training Recurrent Neural Networks" derives both vanishing and exploding gradients rigorously and is the paper most credited with popularizing gradient clipping as a fix.

Wondering why gradient clipping is a full fix for exploding gradients but only a partial one for vanishing gradients? Ask your teacher — capping a gradient that's too large is a straightforward ceiling, but there's no equivalent floor that can inject real signal back into a gradient that's already collapsed toward zero.
