What Is Engineering, Really?

Not applied science, not applied math — a third thing, with its own goal and its own way of deciding when it's done.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Ask most people what an engineer does and you'll get something like "applies science and math to build things." That's not wrong, but it misses what actually makes engineering its own discipline rather than a subset of physics. This lesson is about that missing piece.

Three different goals

Science, math, and engineering all use rigorous reasoning — but they're aimed at different targets, and confusing them causes real trouble later.

Definition Science seeks to discover true statements about how the world already works. Mathematics seeks to prove statements true within a formal system, independent of the physical world entirely. Engineering seeks to design something that doesn't yet exist, to meet a specific need, within real constraints.

A physicist studying friction wants to know the truth about how friction behaves. A mathematician might prove a theorem about optimization that has nothing to do with anything physical. An engineer designing a braking system doesn't get to stop at "now I understand friction" — the job isn't done until a working brake exists, ships within budget, and doesn't hurt anyone.

Why it matters This is why an engineering answer can be "good enough" where a scientific one can't be. Science aims at truth, which has no finish line short of certainty. Engineering aims at a working, safe, affordable solution by a deadline — "good enough" is a legitimate stopping point, not a compromise.

Constraints are the job, not an obstacle to it

A common misconception is that engineering is just "figuring out the right answer" the way a math problem has one. In practice, most engineering problems have many workable answers, and the real skill is navigating trade-offs between constraints that pull in different directions at once:

Example Design a phone battery. Bigger capacity means longer battery life — but also more weight, more cost, more heat, and a thicker phone. There's no single "correct" battery size. There's only a defensible choice given which constraints matter most to this specific product.

Without constraints, there's no engineering problem — just an unlimited wish list. A bridge with infinite budget and infinite time is trivial to build safely. The moment you add "for $2 million, finished in 18 months, using materials that survive this climate," you have an actual engineering problem, and the constraints are the substance of it.

Engineering borrows, it doesn't discover

Engineers rely constantly on scientific results — Newton's laws, material properties, circuit theory — but they don't re-derive them. An engineer treats well-established science as a trusted tool, the same way you use a hammer without re-deriving the physics of leverage each time you swing it.

Definition Applied science and engineering are often used loosely as synonyms, but engineering specifically means using established knowledge to design and build a solution under real-world constraints — cost, time, safety, and materials among them.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the primary goal of engineering, as distinguished from science?

Science aims at truth about an existing world; engineering aims at a working solution to a need, which doesn't yet exist and must meet real-world constraints.

2. Why can an engineering answer be "good enough" in a way a scientific one typically isn't?

Truth has no finish line short of certainty, but a working, safe, affordable solution by a deadline is a legitimate, well-defined stopping point.

3. Why are constraints described as "the substance of" an engineering problem, not an obstacle to it?

A bridge with unlimited budget and time is trivial. Add real limits on cost, time, and materials, and you have an actual engineering problem — the constraints are what make it one.

4. Fill in the blank: most engineering problems have many workable answers, and the real skill is navigating trade-offs between competing ___.

Cost, time, safety, weight — the trade-offs between these constraints are the actual work, not a single "correct" formula.

5. Fill in the blank: engineers use well-established science the way you use a ___ — without re-deriving the underlying physics each time.

Engineers treat established scientific results as trusted tools to build with, not open questions to re-investigate on every project.
