Unit 1 Test — Version B

What Engineers Do — how engineering differs from science and math, the design process, and the major disciplines. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A mathematician proves a theorem about optimal packing shapes. A structural engineer is asked to design a shipping container using insight from that theorem. What is the key difference in what each one is trying to accomplish?

Mathematics proves statements true within a formal system, independent of the physical world; engineering must produce something that doesn't yet exist, meeting a real need under real constraints.

2. Fill in the blank: add a real budget, a fixed deadline, and a limited set of available materials to an otherwise open-ended design question, and you get an actual engineering problem — because the ___ are what make it one.

Without constraints there's no real engineering problem to solve — they're the substance of the problem, not an obstacle to it.

3. An engineering team ships a bridge design that meets every safety and budget requirement, even though a more exhaustive study might reveal marginally more detail about the soil beneath it. Why is this an acceptable stopping point for engineering, in a way it might not be for an open-ended scientific study of that same soil?

"Good enough" is a legitimate stopping point in engineering because the goal is a working solution by a deadline, not certainty about the physical world.

4. Fill in the blank: an engineer designing a circuit uses established circuit theory without re-deriving it from first principles — the same way you'd use a ___ without re-deriving the physics of leverage each time you swing it.

Engineers treat established scientific results as trusted tools to build with, not open questions to reinvestigate on every project.

5. A project is centered on how current flows through a power distribution grid. Which discipline does this problem most fundamentally belong to?

Current, voltage, and signal flow are the dominant questions of electrical engineering.

6. A team is designing a new water bottle. Answer both parts:

(a) Which of these is a properly stated problem definition for this project?

(b) Fill in the blank: before committing to a full production run, the team builds a rough, cheap version of their leading design — a stage of the process called ___.

(a) A real problem definition states precisely what must be true when the design is finished — specific and testable, not a vague wish. (b) Prototyping comes after generating options and before testing, and is meant to be rough and cheap.

7. Testing reveals the water bottle's rough prototype leaks at the lid threads. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does this finding send the team back to an earlier stage, rather than ending the process?

(b) Fill in the blank: folding this new information back into generating better lid options is an example of the process's final stage, called ___.

(a) The loop structure is what lets a test failure send the project back to whichever earlier stage the new information actually points to. (b) Iteration is what turns a single pass through the process into a loop that converges on a working design.

8. One team builds and tests a $50 cardboard-and-tape mockup of a phone case before manufacturing; another team skips straight to a $200,000 injection-mold run without any mockup. Answer both parts:

(a) Which team is following the reasoning behind the prototyping stage, and why?

(b) If the injection-mold run reveals a design flaw only after the $200,000 was spent, what does this illustrate?

Prototyping exists specifically so that flaws surface while they're still cheap to fix — the same flaw found after full-scale production costs vastly more.

9. A retaining wall holding back a hillside for 50 years and a robot arm's support bracket both rely on similar statics principles. Answer both parts:

(a) What most distinguishes the retaining wall (civil engineering) from the robot arm bracket (mechanical engineering)?

(b) Fill in the blank: despite sharing the same underlying physics as mechanical engineering, civil engineering applies it at a much larger and more permanent ___.

Civil and mechanical engineering share the same underlying physics — civil engineering's distinguishing feature is scale and multi-decade public-safety stakes.

10. A modern smartphone requires mechanical engineers (the case and internal layout), electrical engineers (the circuitry and battery), and software engineers (the operating system). Answer both parts:

(a) What does this best illustrate about the four core discipline categories introduced in this unit?

(b) Fill in the blank: because a single complex product like a smartphone draws on several disciplines at once, the boundaries between them constantly ___.

No single discipline covers the whole product — mechanical, electrical, and software engineering combine, which is exactly why the boundaries between disciplines blur in real projects.
