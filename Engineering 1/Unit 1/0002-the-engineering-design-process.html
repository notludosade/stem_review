The Engineering Design Process

The loop underneath every engineering project, from a paperclip to a spacecraft.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Last lesson established that engineering means designing something new under real constraints. This lesson covers the actual process engineers use to get there — not a rigid checklist, but a loop that gets revisited constantly, often out of order.

The six stages

Definition The engineering design process is the cycle of defining a problem, setting constraints, generating candidate solutions, prototyping the most promising ones, testing them against the constraints, and iterating based on what testing reveals.

Each stage, in a little more depth:

1. Define the problem Before any solving, state precisely what needs to be true when you're done. "Make the phone last longer" is not a problem statement. "The battery must power the phone for 24 hours of typical use" is.
2. Set constraints List everything the solution must respect: budget, deadline, available materials, safety requirements, regulations. Constraints don't get discovered later — as much as possible, they're nailed down up front.
3. Generate options Brainstorm multiple candidate approaches before committing to one. The first idea is rarely the best idea, and comparing options is only possible if more than one exists.
4. Prototype Build a rough, cheap version of the leading candidate — a sketch, a 3D-printed part, a breadboard circuit. The goal is to learn quickly and cheaply, before committing real money to the full version.
5. Test Check the prototype against the constraints from step 2, honestly. Does it actually meet the requirement, or does it just look like it does?
6. Iterate Testing almost always reveals something the design didn't account for. Go back — often to step 3, sometimes all the way to step 1 — and refine.

It's a loop, not a line

The numbered list above makes it look sequential. In practice, real projects jump backward constantly: a test failure in step 5 might reveal that the original problem definition in step 1 was wrong, not just the design.

Why it's drawn as a loop If the process were truly linear, a flaw discovered during testing would be unfixable without starting over. Treating it as a loop — where any stage can send you back to an earlier one — is what makes real, imperfect information usable instead of paralyzing.

Failing early is the entire point of prototyping

A cheap prototype that fails teaches you something for very little cost. A finished product that fails teaches you the same thing for enormous cost — in money, time, and sometimes safety. This is why step 4 exists at all: it's deliberately designed to fail cheaply and quickly, so step 6 has real information to work with.

Example Before building a full bridge, engineers build small-scale physical models and computer simulations. A model that reveals a design flaw costs a few thousand dollars and a few weeks. The same flaw discovered after construction could cost millions — or lives.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which of these is a properly stated problem definition?

A real problem definition states precisely what must be true when the design is finished — specific and testable, not vague.

2. Why is the design process better understood as a loop rather than a straight line?

A flaw found during testing might trace back to the option chosen, or even the original problem definition — the loop lets you revisit whichever stage the new information points to.

3. What is the main purpose of prototyping before building a final version?

Prototypes are deliberately rough and inexpensive so that flaws surface while they're still cheap to fix — the same flaw found after full construction costs vastly more.

4. Fill in the blank: budget, deadline, materials, and safety requirements are all examples of ___, which are ideally nailed down before generating candidate solutions.

Constraints define what any candidate solution must respect — set early so options can be judged against them.

5. Fill in the blank: the final stage of the design process, where insights from testing get folded back into an earlier stage, is called "___."

Iteration is what turns a single pass through the process into a loop that converges on a working design.
