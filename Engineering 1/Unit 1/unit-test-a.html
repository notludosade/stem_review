Unit 1 Test — Version A

What Engineers Do — how engineering differs from science and math, the design process, and the major disciplines. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A physicist wants to understand why a certain alloy conducts heat the way it does. An engineer is asked to design a heat sink for a processor using that same alloy. What is the key difference between their two goals?

Science aims at true statements about an existing world; engineering aims at a working solution to a specific need, built within real-world constraints like cost and time.

2. Fill in the blank: a bridge design with an unlimited budget and unlimited time is trivial to solve — real engineering problems are defined by the ___ placed on cost, materials, time, and safety.

Without constraints there's no real engineering problem to solve — they're the substance of the problem, not an obstacle to it.

3. Why can an engineering team declare a design "done" at a defensible, working solution, in a way a scientific investigation into the same phenomenon typically can't?

"Good enough" is a legitimate stopping point in engineering because the goal is a working solution by a deadline, not certainty about the physical world.

4. Fill in the blank: engineers rely on established results like Newton's laws without re-deriving them each time — treating well-established science the way you'd use a ___, without re-deriving the physics of leverage on every swing.

Engineers treat established scientific results as trusted tools to build with, not open questions to reinvestigate on every project.

5. A project is centered on designing the suspension and braking system of a vehicle — forces, motion, and load-bearing parts. Which discipline does this problem most fundamentally belong to?

Motion, forces, and how objects bear or transform load are the dominant questions of mechanical engineering.

6. A team is designing a new backpack. Answer both parts:

(a) Which of these is a properly stated problem definition for this project?

(b) Fill in the blank: after brainstorming candidate designs, the team builds a rough, cheap version of the leading one — a stage of the design process called ___.

(a) A real problem definition states precisely what must be true when the design is finished — specific and testable, not a vague wish. (b) Prototyping comes after generating options and before testing, and is meant to be rough and cheap.

7. During testing, the backpack team discovers the shoulder straps dig in painfully under the target 15 kg load. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is the design process better modeled as a loop rather than a straight line, given this discovery?

(b) Fill in the blank: folding what's learned from a failed test back into an earlier stage is the final stage of the process, called ___.

(a) The loop structure is what lets a test failure send the project back to whichever earlier stage the new information actually points to. (b) Iteration is what turns a single pass through the process into a loop that converges on a working design.

8. Instead of building a rough prototype, a team skips straight to manufacturing 10,000 finished backpacks, and only then discovers the strap design fails under load. Answer both parts:

(a) What did skipping the prototype stage cost this team, according to the reasoning behind that stage?

(b) What is the main purpose of a rough, cheap prototype, according to the design process?

Prototyping exists specifically so that flaws surface while they're still cheap to fix — the same flaw found after full-scale production costs vastly more.

9. A dam project and a hydraulic pump project both rely on similar principles of forces and materials. Answer both parts:

(a) What most distinguishes the dam (a civil engineering project) from the pump (a mechanical engineering project)?

(b) Fill in the blank: civil engineering leans on the same underlying statics and materials principles as mechanical engineering, applied at a much larger and more permanent ___.

Civil and mechanical engineering share the same underlying physics — civil engineering's distinguishing feature is scale and multi-decade public-safety stakes.

10. An electric car requires engineers working on the chassis and suspension, the battery and motor control, and the software tying it all together. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this example demonstrate about the four core engineering disciplines from this unit?

(b) Fill in the blank: the four discipline categories from this unit are a starting map, not a strict partition — in practice, the boundaries between them constantly ___.

No single discipline covers the whole vehicle — mechanical, electrical, and software engineering combine, which is exactly why the boundaries between disciplines blur in real projects.
