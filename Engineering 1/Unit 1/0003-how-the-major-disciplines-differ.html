How the Major Disciplines Differ

Mechanical, electrical, civil, and chemical engineering all use the same design process — on very different kinds of problems.

← Lesson 2 Unit 2, Lesson 4 → Contents Glossary →

Every discipline you're about to meet uses the exact same design process from the last lesson. What differs is what kind of system they're designing, and which physical laws dominate the problem. This lesson is a map, not a deep dive — the goal is recognizing which discipline a given problem belongs to.

Mechanical engineering

Mechanical engineering Designs systems involving motion, forces, and energy — engines, machines, vehicles, robots, HVAC systems. The dominant question: how do forces move, support, or transform this object?

If a problem is about something physically moving, bearing load, or converting one form of energy into motion, it's probably mechanical. Units 3 and 4 of this course (statics and materials) are foundational mechanical engineering topics.

Electrical engineering

Electrical engineering Designs systems involving electricity and electromagnetic signals — circuits, power grids, communications, computer hardware. The dominant question: how does current, voltage, or a signal flow through this system?

If a problem is about electrical current, signal processing, or power distribution, it's electrical. Unit 6 of this course (circuits) is a foundational electrical engineering topic.

Civil engineering

Civil engineering Designs large-scale, fixed infrastructure — bridges, buildings, roads, water systems, dams. The dominant question: will this structure stand up safely, for decades, under real-world loads like wind, traffic, and earthquakes?

Civil engineering leans heavily on the same statics and materials principles as mechanical engineering, but applied at a much larger, more permanent scale, and with public safety over decades as a central design driver — not just years.

Chemical engineering

Chemical engineering Designs processes that transform raw materials through chemical or physical change at scale — refineries, pharmaceutical production, water treatment. The dominant question: how do you take a chemical reaction that works in a small beaker and run it safely, continuously, and profitably at industrial scale?

Chemical engineering is the discipline this course touches least, since it depends heavily on chemistry as a prerequisite. It's included here so the map is complete — you'll recognize a chemical engineering problem when a project is fundamentally about a chemical or physical transformation, not just moving or wiring something.

The boundaries blur constantly

These four categories are a starting map, not a strict partition. Most real projects mix disciplines:

Example An electric car involves mechanical engineers (the chassis, suspension, and braking), electrical engineers (the battery management and motor control), and — increasingly — software engineers (the control systems that tie it all together). No single discipline covers the whole vehicle.
Why this survey matters You don't need to pick a specialization yet. But recognizing "this is fundamentally a forces problem" versus "this is fundamentally a circuit problem" versus "this is fundamentally a large-structure problem" is what lets you eventually choose a specialization based on real interest — not a guess.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. A project centered on how current flows through a signal-processing board is most fundamentally a problem in which discipline?

Current, voltage, and signal flow are the dominant questions of electrical engineering.

2. What most distinguishes civil engineering from mechanical engineering, given that both rely on similar statics and materials principles?

Both disciplines share the underlying physics of forces and materials — civil engineering's distinguishing feature is scale and multi-decade public-safety stakes.

3. Why does the electric car example show that discipline boundaries "blur"?

The chassis, battery, motor control, and software each fall into a different discipline's core expertise — a real project routinely spans several at once.

4. Fill in the blank: taking a reaction that works in a small beaker and running it safely and profitably at industrial scale is the central concern of ___ engineering.

Scaling a chemical or physical transformation from lab to industrial production is the defining chemical engineering problem.

5. Fill in the blank: a problem centered on how forces move, support, or transform a physical object is most fundamentally a ___ engineering problem.

Motion, forces, and energy transformation in physical objects are the core of mechanical engineering.
