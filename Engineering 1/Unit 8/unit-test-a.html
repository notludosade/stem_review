1. The original Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in November 1940, just four months after opening. What actually caused the collapse?

No earthquake, overload, or material defect was involved — an ordinary wind speed the bridge was expected to easily withstand tore it apart.

2. Fill in the blank: the bridge's twisting motion changed how it shed wind, which changed the aerodynamic force on it, which fed back into more twisting — a self-reinforcing pattern called positive ___.

This is exactly the positive feedback loop from Unit 5's systems thinking — each cycle reinforced the next, rather than damping out.

3. Why did the bridge's oscillation keep growing for roughly an hour instead of settling back down?

The force matched a natural twisting mode of the structure, so each cycle amplified rather than damped the oscillation, eventually exceeding what the steel connections could survive.

4. Fill in the blank: the investigation into the collapse directly led to routine ___-tunnel testing of scale models becoming standard practice for long-span bridge design.

This is a direct, traceable example of Lesson 20's principle: a documented failure becomes a permanent, binding requirement for every future project of that type.

5. Why does the Tacoma Narrows case study serve as a capstone connecting nearly every unit in this course?

Units and estimation, forces and equilibrium, materials and failure modes, systems thinking, and codes and ethics all appear in this one, real, well-documented failure.

6. A team is briefed to design a small footbridge across a 6-meter creek for pedestrians and occasional maintenance carts, on a fixed budget, that must survive moderate wind and occasional flooding. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does "occasional maintenance cart" matter during the Define stage, even though the bridge is mainly for pedestrians?

(b) Fill in the blank: constraints like the budget, span, flood exposure, and cart weight aren't obstacles to a "pure" design — per Unit 1, they are the actual design ___ being solved.

A precise problem definition and its constraints, including the cart's added load, are the substance of the design problem, not a footnote to it.

7. For the footbridge's 6-meter span, the team compares a simple solid beam design against a triangulated truss design. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is the truss likely to be lighter than the beam for the same strength?

(b) Fill in the blank: once a truss geometry and material are chosen, Unit 3's equilibrium conditions (ΣF = 0, ΣM = 0) and stress calculation (σ = F/A) confirm that each ___ is sized adequately for the estimated load.

Triangulated members carry load efficiently through pure tension and compression, often achieving the needed strength with less material than a solid beam.

8. The team compares steel, treated timber, and aluminum for the footbridge, using the "eliminate, then rank" method. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is timber eliminated first in this comparison?

(b) Fill in the blank: after timber is eliminated, steel and aluminum are ranked on strength-to-weight, cost, and ___ — with the final choice depending on which of these the budget weighs most heavily.

Timber is disqualified by a hard requirement (flood-driven rot risk against the budget), leaving steel and aluminum to be ranked against the remaining criteria.

9. Once the footbridge's stress is calculated for the estimated load, the team doesn't design it to just barely survive that load. Answer both parts:

(a) What do they apply on top of the raw stress calculation, and why?

(b) Fill in the blank: local building ___ for pedestrian bridges are also consulted directly, since they encode decades of prior public-structure failures this design shouldn't have to relearn firsthand.

A factor of safety absorbs real-world uncertainty, and consulting local codes brings in decades of collective, hard-won lessons the team shouldn't have to relearn firsthand.

10. Even though the footbridge's short span makes wind-driven oscillation a low risk, the team still deliberately asks what dynamic or systemic effect they could be missing, and later resists park-budget pressure to skip a code requirement. Answer both parts:

(a) Which past lesson most directly motivates asking about dynamic or systemic effects, even for a low-risk span?

(b) Fill in the blank: if budget pressure pushes toward skipping a code requirement on this public structure, Unit 7's engineering ___ principle says public safety still outranks that pressure.

The Tacoma Narrows habit of checking for missed dynamic effects and the public-first ethics principle both apply directly, even to a modest, low-risk footbridge.
