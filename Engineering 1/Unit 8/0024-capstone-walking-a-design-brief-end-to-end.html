Capstone — Walking a Design Brief End to End

One open-ended problem, worked through every stage of the design process — pulling together everything from all 23 lessons before this one.

← Lesson 23 Contents Glossary →

This is the last lesson of Engineering 1. Instead of new content, it walks one realistic design brief through the entire design process from Lesson 2 — define, constrain, generate, prototype, test, iterate — using tools from every unit along the way. Treat this as a rehearsal for how engineers actually think, not a new topic to memorize.

The brief

The problem Design a small footbridge across a 6-meter-wide creek in a public park, intended for pedestrians and occasional maintenance carts, with a target budget and a requirement that it survive local weather, including moderate wind and an occasional flood that submerges the creek bed.

Stage 1 — Define

Before any structure is sketched, the actual problem must be pinned down: who uses this bridge, how often, carrying what, and under what conditions. A footbridge for pedestrians is a very different problem from a bridge rated for vehicles — and "occasional maintenance cart" quietly adds a real load requirement that "pedestrians only" would not.

Stage 2 — Constrain

Applying Unit 1: constraints as substance, not obstacle The budget, the 6-meter span, the flood exposure, and the cart's weight aren't obstacles to a "pure" design — they are the design problem. A structure with no constraints has no real specification to be judged against at all.
Applying Unit 2: units, sig figs, and estimation Before any detailed analysis, a Fermi-style estimate (Lesson 6) bounds the problem: a loaded maintenance cart might weigh on the order of a few hundred kilograms, pedestrian loads add up to perhaps another few hundred kilograms at once — giving a rough total design load in the low thousands of newtons, long before any precise calculation is needed. This estimate alone rules out some materials and spans immediately, cheaply, before committing real design effort.

Stage 3 — Generate and analyze candidate designs

Applying Unit 3: statics and structural form A simple beam bridge across 6 meters is compared against a truss design. The truss (Lesson 9) distributes load through triangulated tension and compression members rather than relying on one solid beam resisting bending — for this span and load, a truss is likely lighter for the same strength, precisely because triangles don't deform the way Lesson 9 explained.
Applying Unit 4: material selection Steel, treated timber, and aluminum are compared using the "eliminate then rank" method from Lesson 12: timber is eliminated first (flood exposure makes long-term rot and corrosion, from Lesson 13, a serious concern for a park budget that won't cover frequent replacement); steel and aluminum are then ranked on strength-to-weight, cost, and maintenance — with the final choice depending on exactly which of those the park's budget weighs most heavily.
Applying Unit 3 again: stress and equilibrium Once a truss geometry and material are chosen, Lesson 8's equilibrium conditions (ΣF = 0, ΣM = 0) and Lesson 10's stress calculations (σ = F/A) confirm that each member is sized adequately for the estimated load — connecting the rough Fermi estimate from Stage 2 to an actual, checkable number.

Stage 4 — Apply margin and codes

Applying Unit 7: safety factor and codes The calculated stress from Stage 3 isn't designed to just barely survive the estimated load — a factor of safety (Lesson 21) appropriate for a public pedestrian structure with uncertain, variable loading is applied on top of it. Local building codes (Lesson 20) for pedestrian bridges are consulted directly, since they encode decades of prior public-structure failures this design should not have to relearn firsthand.

Stage 5 — Consider the whole system, not just the structure

Applying Unit 5: systems thinking, energy, and Unit 8's Tacoma Narrows lesson The bridge isn't analyzed in isolation — Lesson 23's case study is a direct warning here: a bridge that's statically strong enough can still fail if wind-driven oscillation isn't considered. For a span this short, that risk is low, but the capstone habit — deliberately asking "what dynamic or systemic effect could I be missing?" — is exactly the habit Tacoma Narrows existed to teach.

Stage 6 — Ethics and final responsibility

Applying Unit 7: engineering ethics If the park's budget pressures the design toward cutting the safety factor or skipping a code requirement, Lesson 22's principle applies directly: public safety outranks budget pressure. A public footbridge is exactly the kind of structure the "public first" principle exists to protect — bystanders and park visitors have no say in the engineering trade-offs being made on their behalf.

What this capstone is really teaching

No single lesson in this course could have prepared you for this brief on its own. Real engineering problems don't arrive labeled by unit — they require pulling in units and estimation, statics, materials, energy and systems thinking, codes, safety margins, and ethics, all at once, in the order the actual problem demands rather than the order a course teaches them. That integration, not any single formula, is the real skill this course was building toward.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does a Fermi-style estimate of the design load happen early in Stage 2, before any detailed structural calculation?

An early order-of-magnitude estimate lets obviously unsuitable options be discarded cheaply, before the more expensive detailed analysis stage.

2. In this brief, why is a truss design considered over a simple solid beam for the 6-meter span?

Triangulated truss members carry load efficiently through pure tension and compression, often achieving the needed strength with less material than a solid beam.

3. Why does the capstone deliberately ask "what dynamic or systemic effect could I be missing?" even for a short, low-risk footbridge span?

The risk itself is low for a short span, but the case study's real lesson is the general habit of checking for effects beyond static strength — not a claim that every bridge shares Tacoma Narrows' specific vulnerability.

4. Fill in the blank: if budget pressure pushes toward cutting a safety factor or skipping a code requirement on a public structure, Unit 7's engineering ___ principle says public safety still outranks that pressure.

Public-first ethics outranks cost or schedule pressure, exactly as Lesson 22 established — a public footbridge is precisely the kind of structure that principle protects.

5. Fill in the blank: this capstone's real lesson is that solving a genuine engineering brief requires ___ of ideas across many units at once, not mastery of any single formula in isolation.

Real problems pull together estimation, statics, materials, systems thinking, codes, safety margins, and ethics simultaneously — integration across the whole course is the actual skill being built.
