Case Study — The Tacoma Narrows Bridge Collapse

A real bridge, torn apart by wind alone in 1940 — and how nearly every idea from this course explains exactly why.

← Unit 7, Lesson 22 Lesson 24 → Contents Glossary →

This lesson is different from the rest of the course: instead of introducing a new concept, it applies nearly everything already covered to one real, well-documented failure. Treat it as a test of how the pieces fit together, not new material to memorize.

What happened

The event The original Tacoma Narrows Bridge, in Washington State, opened in July 1940. On November 7, 1940 — just four months later — it collapsed into Puget Sound during a sustained 40 mph wind, an unremarkable wind speed the bridge was expected to easily withstand. No earthquake, no overloading, no material defect. Wind alone tore the bridge apart, and the collapse was captured on film, making it one of the most thoroughly documented structural failures in engineering history.

Why it happened: aerodynamics meeting an under-constrained design

The bridge's deck was unusually narrow and shallow relative to its length — a solid, plate-like girder rather than an open truss. In steady wind, this shape didn't just resist airflow; it shed wind in alternating vortices above and below the deck, creating a rhythmically alternating force.

Why this connects to systems thinking (Unit 5) The bridge deck, the alternating wind vortices, and the bridge's own twisting motion formed a feedback loop: the twisting motion changed how the deck shed wind, which changed the force on the deck, which fed back into more twisting. This is a positive feedback loop of exactly the kind Lesson 15 warned about — small oscillations reinforcing themselves rather than damping out.
Why this connects to resonance and materials (Units 3-4) The alternating aerodynamic force happened to match a natural twisting mode of the bridge's structure, amplifying the oscillation with each cycle rather than letting it settle — the same underlying idea as the stress-strain and structural-rigidity concepts from Unit 3, pushed into a self-reinforcing regime nobody had designed for. The twisting grew for roughly an hour, reaching an amplitude no steel component was ever expected to survive, until the deck's connections failed and sections fell into the water below.

Why it happened: the process failure underneath the physics

Why this connects to the design process (Unit 1) and codes (Unit 7) At the time, bridge design focused almost entirely on static loads — the bridge's own weight and steady traffic loads — with far less attention paid to dynamic, wind-driven behavior. The design process (Lesson 2) hadn't yet iterated against this failure mode, because nobody had a documented precedent to test against. This is precisely the gap that codes and standards (Lesson 20) exist to close: after Tacoma Narrows, aerodynamic and wind-tunnel testing became a standard, required part of long-span bridge design — a code requirement that traces directly back to this one failure, exactly as Lesson 20 described in general terms.

The aftermath: how the profession actually responded

Nobody died in the collapse (famously, only a dog was lost, left behind in an abandoned car), which meant the full engineering attention could go toward understanding why, rather than toward crisis response. The investigation directly led to routine wind-tunnel testing of scale bridge models, greater attention to a structure's stiffness-to-weight ratio, and a lasting professional case study still taught in nearly every introductory engineering course — including, now, this one.

Why this case study anchors the whole course Every major idea from this course appears in this one failure: units and estimation (how wind load was under-estimated), forces and equilibrium (the twisting motion violating assumed static equilibrium), materials and failure modes (the steel connections' ultimate failure), systems thinking (the feedback loop), and codes/ethics (the standard that didn't yet exist, and the one written afterward). A real engineer rarely reasons about just one of these ideas in isolation — real problems, like this one, require holding several at once.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What ultimately caused the Tacoma Narrows Bridge to collapse?

A moderate, steady wind created alternating aerodynamic forces that fed back into the bridge's own twisting motion, amplifying the oscillation until it exceeded what the structure could survive.

2. Why is the growing oscillation best described using the systems-thinking idea of a feedback loop?

The motion and the aerodynamic force reinforced each other in a loop, exactly the positive-feedback pattern described in Unit 5 — not a one-time push, but a self-amplifying cycle.

3. What lasting change did the Tacoma Narrows collapse produce in bridge engineering practice, connecting directly to Lesson 20's idea about codes?

The investigation directly led to aerodynamic and wind-tunnel testing becoming standard practice — a textbook example of a code or standard tracing back to one specific, documented failure.

4. Fill in the blank: the bridge's growing twisting motion is explained by a positive ___ loop between the structure's motion and the aerodynamic force acting on it.

Each cycle of motion reinforced the next, rather than damping out — the defining feature of a positive feedback loop from Unit 5.

5. Fill in the blank: unlike most bridge failures of its era, Tacoma Narrows collapsed under an ordinary, moderate ___ alone — with no earthquake, overload, or material defect involved.

A routine 40 mph wind, not any dramatic external event, was the entire trigger — which is exactly why the failure was so instructive to the profession.
