Unit 1 — What Engineering Is

Engineering

Designing something that doesn't yet exist, to meet a specific need, within real constraints — distinct from science (discovering how the world works) and math (proving statements within a formal system).

Engineering design process

The cycle of defining a problem, setting constraints, generating candidate solutions, prototyping the most promising ones, testing them against the constraints, and iterating based on what testing reveals.

Mechanical engineering

Designs systems involving motion, forces, and energy — engines, machines, vehicles, robots, HVAC systems.

Electrical engineering

Designs systems involving electricity and electromagnetic signals — circuits, power grids, communications, computer hardware.

Civil engineering

Designs large-scale, fixed infrastructure — bridges, buildings, roads, water systems, dams — meant to stand safely for decades under real-world loads.

Chemical engineering

Designs processes that transform raw materials through chemical or physical change at industrial scale — refineries, pharmaceutical production, water treatment.

Unit 2 — Units, Precision, and Estimation

International System of Units (SI)

Defines seven base units everything else is built from: meter, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela. Every derived unit (newton, joule, watt) is a combination of these.

Dimensional analysis

Tracking the units through every step of a calculation, the same way you'd track the numbers, and confirming the units on both sides of an equation actually match.

Accuracy

How close a measurement is to the true value.

Precision

How consistent repeated measurements are with each other — and how many meaningful digits a single measurement reports.

Significant figures

The digits in a measurement that carry real meaning — every reliably known digit, plus one estimated digit at the end.

Fermi problem

A question with no readily available exact data, answered by breaking it into a chain of roughly-estimated quantities that multiply or divide together.

Unit 3 — Statics: Forces, Equilibrium, and Structures

Force / vector

A force is a push or pull on an object, described by both magnitude and direction. A quantity with both magnitude and direction is a vector; a quantity with magnitude only (like mass) is a scalar.

Free-body diagram

A simplified sketch of a single object, isolated from everything touching it, with every force acting on it drawn as an arrow.

Equilibrium

The condition where the net force acting on an object is zero and the net moment acting on it is zero — it isn't accelerating or starting to rotate.

Moment

The turning effect of a force about a point: M = F × d, the force's magnitude times the perpendicular distance from the point to the force's line of action.

Truss

A structure built entirely from straight members connected at pin joints, designed so every member carries only axial force — pure tension or compression, no bending.

Method of joints

Solves a truss by isolating one pin joint at a time and applying force equilibrium (ΣFₓ = 0, ΣFy = 0) at that joint.

Stress (σ)

Internal force per unit cross-sectional area: σ = F / A. Measured in pascals.

Strain (ε)

The fractional change in a member's length under load: ε = ΔL / L₀ — a unitless ratio.

Hooke's law / modulus of elasticity

σ = E × ε — stress and strain are directly proportional at low enough stress, where E (Young's modulus) is a material property measuring stiffness.

Unit 4 — Materials

Ductile

A material that deforms substantially before it fractures, giving visible warning that failure is coming.

Brittle

A material that fractures suddenly, at or just past its yield point, with little or no visible deformation beforehand.

Eliminate, then rank

A material selection method: first eliminate any material that fails a hard requirement (like melting point), then rank the survivors on the criteria that matter most for the specific application.

Fatigue

Failure caused by repeated cycles of stress well below the material's yield point, slowly growing a microscopic crack until it suddenly propagates.

Fracture

Sudden failure that propagates from an existing flaw or crack, especially in brittle materials, often with little or no warning deformation.

Corrosion

The gradual degradation of a material through chemical reaction with its environment, slowly reducing the effective cross-sectional area carrying load.

Unit 5 — Energy and Systems

Work (W)

The energy transferred when a force acts on an object as it moves through a distance: W = F × d, measured in joules.

Energy

The capacity to do work — kinetic, potential, thermal, chemical, and electrical, among other forms.

Conservation of energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted or transferred — total energy before and after any process is always exactly equal.

Power (P)

The rate at which energy is transferred or work is done: P = W / t, measured in watts.

System

Anything that takes one or more inputs, transforms them according to some internal process, and produces one or more outputs.

Feedback loop

Exists when a system's output is fed back to influence its own future input or behavior. Negative feedback pushes toward stability; positive feedback amplifies deviation.

Efficiency (η)

The ratio of useful energy output to total energy input: η = useful output / total input.

Unit 6 — Circuits

Voltage (V)

The electrical "pressure" pushing charge through a circuit, measured in volts.

Current (I)

The rate at which electric charge flows, measured in amperes.

Resistance (R)

Opposition to current flow, measured in ohms.

Ohm's law

V = I × R — the relationship tying voltage, current, and resistance together in a circuit.

Circuit

A closed loop that current can flow around, connecting a power source to one or more components and back to the source again.

Series circuit

Components connected end-to-end in a single loop, so the same current flows through all of them: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ...

Parallel circuit

Components connected across the same two points, each getting the full source voltage independently: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...

Analog signal

A signal that varies continuously and can take on any value within a range.

Digital signal

A signal that takes on only a fixed set of discrete values, almost always two (binary 0 and 1).

ADC / DAC

Analog-to-digital conversion samples a continuous signal into discrete digital values; digital-to-analog conversion reverses the process.

Unit 7 — Codes, Safety, and Ethics

Code

A legally enforceable set of minimum requirements, typically adopted by a government body.

Standard

A detailed technical specification, usually developed by a professional or industry organization, that a code often references or requires by law.

Factor of safety (FS)

The ratio of a material's failure strength to the maximum stress a part is actually expected to experience: FS = failure stress / expected stress.

Margin of safety (MS)

The same idea as factor of safety, expressed as extra capacity above what's required: MS = FS − 1.

Professional Engineer (PE)

A licensed engineer who has passed the FE and PE exams and gained supervised experience, able to legally stamp designs — a personal, legal statement of responsibility for a design's safety and code compliance.
