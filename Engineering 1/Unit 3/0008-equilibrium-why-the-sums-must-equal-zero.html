Equilibrium — Why the Sums Must Equal Zero

A structure that isn't moving must have its forces and moments perfectly balanced — the single idea underneath all of statics.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Once a free-body diagram exists, there's one condition that lets you actually solve for unknown forces: equilibrium. This lesson covers what that condition is, and why it holds for anything not accelerating.

Newton's first law, applied to structures

Definition An object is in equilibrium when the net force acting on it is zero and the net moment (turning effect) acting on it is zero. Newton's first law guarantees that an object in equilibrium is either at rest or moving at constant velocity — it isn't accelerating in any direction, and it isn't starting to rotate.

Statics — this unit's subject — specifically studies objects at rest: buildings, bridges, and stationary machine parts. For all of these, equilibrium isn't optional; it's the defining condition of "not falling down."

Two equilibrium conditions, not one

1. Force equilibrium The sum of all forces in every direction equals zero: ΣFₓ = 0 and ΣFy = 0 (in two dimensions). If this weren't true, the object would accelerate — Newton's second law, F = ma, guarantees a nonzero net force produces nonzero acceleration.
2. Moment equilibrium The sum of all moments (turning effects) about any point equals zero: ΣM = 0. Even if all the forces balance, an object can still start spinning if those forces aren't also balanced in their turning effect — so this is a genuinely separate condition, not implied by force balance alone.
Why moments need their own equation Picture two equal, opposite forces pushing on opposite ends of a rod, both perpendicular to the rod. The net force is zero — they cancel. But the rod still spins, because the forces don't act along the same line. Force balance alone missed this entirely; that's exactly the situation moment equilibrium is designed to catch.

What a moment actually is

Definition A moment (or torque) is the turning effect of a force about a point, equal to the force's magnitude multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the point to the force's line of action.
M = F × d

This is why a longer wrench makes a bolt easier to turn: the same hand-force F, applied at a larger distance d from the bolt, produces a larger moment M — more turning effect for the same effort.

Worked example: a simple beam

Example A 4-meter beam rests on two supports, one at each end. A 100 N weight sits 1 meter from the left support. Because the beam isn't moving, both equilibrium conditions apply: the two support forces must add up to 100 N (force equilibrium), and taking moments about the left support, the right support's force times 4 m must equal 100 N times 1 m — giving the right support 25 N, and by force equilibrium, the left support 75 N. The weight closer to the left support means the left support carries more of the load, which matches intuition and confirms the math.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What two conditions together define equilibrium?

Both the net force and the net moment must be zero — force balance alone doesn't rule out spinning.

2. Why can an object with zero net force still fail to be in equilibrium?

Two equal, opposite forces on opposite ends of a rod cancel as a net force but still produce a spinning moment — force balance and moment balance are genuinely separate conditions.

3. In the beam example, why does the support closer to the 100 N weight carry more of the load?

Taking moments about one support solves directly for the other's force — the closer the load is to a support, the larger that support's share of the total load.

4. Fill in the blank: the turning effect of a force about a point, equal to force times perpendicular distance, is called a ___.

M = F × d — a longer distance from the pivot produces a larger moment for the same force.

5. Fill in the blank: for an object in equilibrium, the sum of all forces in any direction must equal ___.

ΣF = 0 in every direction is the force half of the equilibrium condition; a nonzero sum would mean acceleration, by Newton's second law.
