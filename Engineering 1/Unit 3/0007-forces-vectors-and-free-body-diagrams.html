Forces, Vectors, and Free-Body Diagrams

One diagram that shows every force acting on an object — the first tool in any statics problem.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Statics — the study of objects that aren't accelerating — starts with correctly identifying every force acting on an object. Get that wrong, and every calculation downstream is wrong too, no matter how carefully you do the math. This lesson is about getting that first step right.

Forces are vectors

Definition A force is a push or pull on an object, fully described by both a magnitude (how strong) and a direction (which way). A quantity with both magnitude and direction is called a vector.

Contrast this with a scalar — a quantity with magnitude only, like mass or temperature. "10 kilograms" is complete on its own. "10 newtons" is not complete until you also say which direction — 10 newtons downward is a completely different force than 10 newtons sideways, even though the magnitude is identical.

Common forces you'll draw over and over

Weight The force of gravity pulling an object straight down, equal to mass × gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s² near Earth's surface).
Normal force The push a surface exerts perpendicular to itself against whatever is resting on it — what keeps you from falling through the floor.
Tension The pulling force transmitted through a rope, cable, or string, directed along its length.
Friction A force resisting relative sliding between two surfaces in contact, directed opposite to the direction of sliding (or potential sliding).

The free-body diagram

Definition A free-body diagram is a simplified sketch of a single object, isolated from everything touching it, with every force acting on it drawn as an arrow — showing direction and (when known) relative magnitude.
Example A book resting on a table has exactly two forces acting on it: weight, pointing straight down, and the table's normal force, pointing straight up. A free-body diagram of the book is just those two arrows — the table itself isn't drawn, because the diagram isolates the book alone.
Why isolate the object? Drawing the whole scene (table, floor, walls, book) makes it easy to accidentally mix up which forces act on the book versus forces the book exerts on something else. Isolating just the object forces you to ask, cleanly, one object at a time: "what is touching this, and what force does that contact exert on it?"

A common mistake: forgetting a force exists

The single most common error in statics isn't bad math — it's an incomplete free-body diagram. If you forget to draw friction, or draw weight in the wrong direction, every equation that follows will be internally consistent but describe the wrong physical situation entirely.

Example A crate sitting on a ramp has three forces, not two: weight (straight down), normal force (perpendicular to the ramp surface, not straight up), and friction (along the ramp surface, resisting sliding). Forgetting friction — or drawing the normal force straight up instead of perpendicular to the incline — produces equations that look fine but solve the wrong problem.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What two properties fully describe a force?

A force is a vector — it isn't fully specified without both how strong it is and which way it points.

2. Why is a free-body diagram drawn for a single isolated object, rather than the whole scene?

Isolating one object at a time keeps the diagram focused strictly on forces acting on it, avoiding a common source of directional mix-ups.

3. For a crate resting on an inclined ramp, which direction does the normal force point?

Normal force is always perpendicular to the contact surface — on an incline, that means it tilts with the ramp, not straight up.

4. Fill in the blank: any quantity with both a magnitude and a direction, like force, is called a ___.

Vectors carry direction information; scalars, like mass or temperature, do not.

5. Fill in the blank: the force that resists relative sliding between two surfaces in contact is called ___.

Friction acts opposite to the direction of sliding or potential sliding, and is easy to accidentally omit from a free-body diagram.
