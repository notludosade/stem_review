Stress and Strain — How Materials Respond to Load

Force alone doesn't tell you if a part will survive — stress and strain normalize for size so materials can be fairly compared.

← Lesson 9 Unit 4, Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

The last two lessons found the force running through a structural member. This lesson asks the next question: given that force, does the member actually survive? The answer depends on stress and strain, not force alone.

Why force alone isn't enough

Example A 1,000 N pull will snap a thin wire instantly, but a thick steel column barely notices it. The same force affects a thin part and a thick part completely differently — so "force" alone can't predict whether a member survives. What matters is force relative to the member's cross-sectional area.

Stress: force normalized by area

Definition Stress (σ) is internal force per unit cross-sectional area: σ = F / A. It has units of pressure — pascals (newtons per square meter) in SI.
σ = F / A

Stress is what lets you compare a thin wire and a thick column on equal footing: the thin wire has far less area to spread the same force across, so it experiences much higher stress — and that's what actually determines failure, not the raw force number.

Strain: deformation normalized by original length

Definition Strain (ε) is the fractional change in a member's length under load: ε = ΔL / L₀, where ΔL is the change in length and L₀ is the original length. Strain has no units — it's a ratio, a pure number (often expressed as a percentage).
ε = ΔL / L₀

Just as stress normalizes force by area, strain normalizes deformation by original length — a 1 mm stretch means something very different on a 10 mm member versus a 10 m cable.

Hooke's law: the relationship between them

Definition For most engineering materials at low enough stress, stress and strain are directly proportional: σ = E × ε, where E is the modulus of elasticity (or Young's modulus) — a property of the material itself, not the part's shape.

E measures how stiff a material is: a high E (like steel, ~200 GPa) means a lot of stress produces only a little strain — the material barely stretches. A low E (like rubber) means a little stress produces a lot of strain — the material stretches easily. This is a material property, meaning it's the same for a thin steel wire and a thick steel beam alike; only the stress each one experiences under a given force differs, because their areas differ.

Elastic vs. plastic deformation

Elastic deformation Below a material's yield point, deformation is temporary — remove the load, and the material springs back to its original shape. This is the region Hooke's law describes.
Plastic deformation Beyond the yield point, deformation becomes permanent — remove the load, and the material stays bent or stretched. Bending a paperclip back and forth past its yield point is plastic deformation; a rubber band snapping back after a light stretch is elastic.
Why this distinction drives design Engineers almost always design structural members to stay in the elastic region under normal, expected loads. A bridge that permanently deforms every time a heavy truck crosses it would accumulate damage with every use — the whole point of staying below yield is that the structure returns to its original shape, load after load, indefinitely.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why isn't raw force alone enough to predict whether a structural member will fail?

A thin wire and a thick column experience the same force very differently — stress (force divided by area) is what actually predicts failure.

2. What does a high modulus of elasticity (E) indicate about a material?

E is the constant of proportionality between stress and strain — a high E means the material resists stretching, i.e., it's stiff.

3. Why do engineers generally design structural members to stay below the yield point under normal loads?

Staying in the elastic region means the structure springs back after every load instead of permanently deforming a little more each time.

4. Fill in the blank: internal force divided by cross-sectional area is called ___, measured in pascals.

σ = F / A, giving units of pressure — a way of comparing force across members of different sizes.

5. Fill in the blank: the fractional change in a member's length under load, with no units, is called ___.

ε = ΔL / L₀ — a ratio of two lengths, so the units cancel and strain is dimensionless.
