Trusses and Simple Structures

Why triangles show up in every bridge and crane you've ever seen, and how to solve one joint at a time.

← Lesson 8 Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

Equilibrium from the last lesson applies to a whole structure — but real structures are built from many connected pieces. A truss is the cleanest way to see equilibrium applied piece by piece, and it explains one of the most recognizable shapes in engineering: the triangle.

What a truss is

Definition A truss is a structure built entirely from straight members connected at joints (called pins), designed so every member carries only axial force — pure tension or pure compression along its length, with no bending.
Tension vs. compression A member in tension is being stretched — pulled apart from both ends, like a rope. A member in compression is being squeezed — pushed together from both ends, like a column holding up a roof.

Why triangles, specifically

A triangle is the only polygon whose shape can't change without changing the length of one of its sides. A rectangle made of four hinged rods can collapse into a parallelogram without any rod changing length — it has no inherent rigidity. A triangle made of three hinged rods can't deform at all unless a rod itself stretches or compresses.

Why it matters Because triangles are geometrically rigid on their own, a truss built entirely from triangles doesn't need rigid (bending-resistant) joints to hold its shape — simple pin joints are enough. That's what keeps each member in pure tension or compression, which is exactly the assumption the whole method below depends on.

The method of joints

Definition The method of joints solves a truss by isolating one pin joint at a time as its own free-body diagram, and applying force equilibrium (ΣFₓ = 0, ΣFy = 0) at that joint. Because each joint only has forces (no moments — forces all pass through the same point), only the two force equations apply at each joint, not the moment equation.
Example A simple triangular truss carries a downward load at its top joint, supported by two angled members running down to supports on the ground. Isolating the top joint: the vertical components of the two member forces must add up to support the downward load (ΣFy = 0), and their horizontal components must cancel each other out (ΣFₓ = 0) since nothing else pushes sideways at that joint. Solving these two equations gives the force in each member directly.

Working joint to joint

A joint with only two unknown member forces can always be solved immediately with its two equilibrium equations. Once solved, those member forces become known at the next joint over, which usually reduces that joint to only two new unknowns — letting you work outward across the whole truss, one solvable joint at a time, rather than solving every unknown simultaneously.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What kind of force does every member in an ideal truss carry?

A well-designed truss keeps every member in pure tension or compression along its length, with no bending — this is what makes the method of joints work.

2. Why are triangles the fundamental shape in truss design?

Unlike a hinged rectangle, which can collapse into a parallelogram, a hinged triangle is geometrically locked in shape — no rigid joints are required to keep it stable.

3. Why does the method of joints use only two equilibrium equations (ΣFₓ = 0, ΣFy = 0) at each joint, and not the moment equation?

Since every force at a joint passes through that same point, there's no perpendicular distance for any of them to create a moment about it — only the two force equations are meaningful there.

4. Fill in the blank: a truss member being pulled apart from both ends, like a rope, is said to be in ___.

Tension members are stretched; compression members are squeezed — the two possible states for an axially-loaded truss member.

5. Fill in the blank: the method of joints can immediately solve any joint that has at most ___ unknown member forces.

With only two equilibrium equations available at each joint, at most two unknowns can be solved for at once — which is why you work outward, joint by joint, across the truss.
