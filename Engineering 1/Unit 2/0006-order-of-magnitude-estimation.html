Order-of-Magnitude Estimation

Fermi problems: getting a usably-close answer in minutes, before spending a day computing an exact one.

← Lesson 5 Unit 3, Lesson 7 → Contents Glossary →

Before an engineer commits real time to an exact calculation, a quick sanity-check estimate tells them roughly what answer to expect — and immediately flags a later result that's wildly off. This lesson covers how to build that estimate.

What a Fermi problem is

Definition A Fermi problem is a question with no readily available exact data, answered by breaking it into a chain of roughly-estimated quantities that multiply or divide together — named for physicist Enrico Fermi, known for producing surprisingly good estimates this way on the spot.
Example How many piano tuners are there in Chicago? You don't know this number, and there's no quick way to look it up. But you can estimate: Chicago's population (~2.7 million) → households (~1 million, at ~2.7 people/household) → pianos (~1 in 20 households, ~50,000 pianos) → tunings per year (~once/year, so ~50,000 tunings/year) → tuner capacity (~4 tunings/day × 250 working days ≈ 1,000 tunings/year per tuner) → tuners needed (50,000 ÷ 1,000 = ~50 tuners). The real number is in this same ballpark — not because any single estimate was exact, but because the chain of reasonable guesses converges near the truth.

Why the errors don't compound the way you'd expect

Each individual guess in that chain could easily be off by a factor of 2 in either direction. You might expect five guesses each off by 2× to compound into an answer off by 32×. In practice, overestimates and underestimates tend to partially cancel, because they're independent guesses rather than all-biased-the-same-direction errors — so the final answer is typically within a factor of 3-10 of the true value, which is exactly the precision an order-of-magnitude estimate is meant to deliver.

Why "order of magnitude" is the right bar An order-of-magnitude estimate only aims to get the right power of 10 — is the answer closer to 50, 500, or 5,000? That's a much lower, much more achievable bar than an exact answer, and it's often all you need to catch a gross error or sanity-check a later precise calculation.

Rounding to one significant figure is a feature, not sloppiness

Fermi estimation deliberately uses rounded, one-significant-figure numbers (like "about 3 million" instead of "2,746,388") at every step. This isn't laziness — carrying false precision through a chain of guesses would be misleading, since the underlying guesses were never that precise in the first place.

Example Estimate how many liters of gasoline a mid-size car uses in a year. Typical distance: ~15,000 km/year. Typical fuel economy: ~10 km/liter. 15,000 ÷ 10 = 1,500 liters/year. Both inputs were rounded to one significant figure — the answer is meant to be "around 1,500," not treated as exact.

Where this fits in the design process

Recall the design process from Unit 1: estimation is a fast, cheap way to sanity-check a design direction before investing in a detailed prototype. If a rough estimate says a proposed battery would need to weigh 200 kg to meet a requirement, that's worth knowing in the first five minutes — not after a week of detailed circuit design.

1. What is a Fermi problem?

Fermi problems break an unknown quantity into a chain of estimable sub-quantities that multiply or divide together to approximate the answer.

2. Why doesn't a chain of five individually-uncertain guesses typically compound into a wildly wrong final answer?

Independent errors in different directions tend to offset one another, keeping the final estimate within a factor of about 3-10 of the true value rather than compounding multiplicatively.

3. Why does Fermi estimation deliberately round every input to one significant figure?

Since the underlying guesses were never precisely known, reporting more digits than that would falsely imply a precision the estimate doesn't have.
