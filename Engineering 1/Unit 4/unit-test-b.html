Materials — common engineering materials, material selection trade-offs, and why things break. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. Which of the four material families is described as strong, stiff, and ductile — giving visible warning before failure — but prone to corrosion and relatively heavy for its strength?

Metals like steel, aluminum, and titanium are strong, stiff, and ductile, but can corrode and are relatively heavy compared to their strength.

2. Fill in the blank: a material that deforms substantially — stretching, bending, or necking down — before it finally fractures, giving visible warning of failure, is described as ___.

Ductility is exactly this visible warning before final fracture — the opposite of a brittle material's sudden break.

3. An inspector notices visible bending in a concrete beam's steel reinforcement bars before any crack appears in the concrete itself. What does this bending demonstrate about why steel reinforces concrete?

Steel's ductile bending is precisely the advance warning concrete alone, being brittle, would not provide before a sudden failure.

4. Fill in the blank: carbon fiber strands embedded in an epoxy resin is an example of a ___ — a material combining two components to get a property profile neither has alone.

Combining a stiff fiber with a lighter matrix gets a property profile, like high stiffness at low weight, that neither material achieves on its own.

5. Steel is stronger than aluminum in absolute terms. Why is aluminum, or a carbon-fiber composite, still generally preferred for something like an aircraft's skin?

Density-normalized strength and stiffness, not raw strength, drive the material choice when every kilogram has a real cost, like in aircraft.

6. A brittle ceramic component and a ductile steel component of the same size are both loaded to failure. Answer both parts:

(a) Which best describes the steel component's stress-strain curve behavior right before failure?

(b) Fill in the blank: the ceramic's curve, in contrast, rises linearly and then ___ almost immediately after leaving that linear region, without a flat warning section.

Steel's long flat region reflects ductile behavior that gives warning before failure; a ceramic's abrupt end reflects brittle behavior with no such warning.

7. A budget racing bike and a professional racing bike both need a lightweight frame, yet reasonably use different materials — aluminum versus carbon fiber. Answer both parts:

(a) Why can this be true, even though both need the same part to do the same job?

(b) Fill in the blank: material selection is a trade-off because criteria like strength, weight, ___, and environmental resistance rarely all favor the same material at once.

"Best" only makes sense once the specific application's priorities are known — that's exactly what makes material selection a trade-off, not a lookup.

8. An engineer is selecting a material for a fastener that will sit in a highly corrosive chemical environment. Answer both parts:

(a) Which step happens first in the "eliminate, then rank" method for this fastener?

(b) Fill in the blank: only after hard requirements ___ unsuitable materials does the engineer rank the survivors against remaining criteria like cost and weight.

Elimination happens before ranking, so remaining trade-offs are only weighed among materials that could actually work in the first place.

9. An aircraft fuselage experiences millions of small pressurization cycles over its service life, each individually far below the material's yield stress. Answer both parts:

(a) What failure mode is the dominant concern for this fuselage, rather than a one-time overload?

(b) Fill in the blank: fatigue is dangerous because a part can pass every static load test and still fail later in service, since static tests apply a load only ___ while fatigue depends on repeated cycles.

Fatigue accumulates from many cycles of stress that would each be individually harmless — a single static load test can miss it entirely.

10. A steel cable used in a marine environment develops surface pitting from rust over several years, then later snaps suddenly under a load it had safely carried many times before. Answer both parts:

(a) What best explains why the same load that was once safe eventually caused failure?

(b) Fill in the blank: the gradual degradation of a material through chemical reaction with its environment, such as rusting, is called ___.

Corrosion both raises effective stress by shrinking area and creates a crack-like flaw — often becoming the very starting point a later fracture propagates from.
