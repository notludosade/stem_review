Common Engineering Materials and Their Properties

Metals, polymers, ceramics, and composites — the property profile that makes each one the right, or wrong, choice.

← Unit 3, Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 3 gave you the tools to calculate stress and strain in a member. This lesson covers what determines a material's E (stiffness) and yield point in the first place — and the broad families of materials engineers choose between.

Four material families

Metals Strong, stiff, and ductile (they deform noticeably before breaking, giving visible warning of failure). Conduct heat and electricity well. Examples: steel, aluminum, titanium. Downsides: can corrode, and are relatively heavy compared to their strength.
Polymers (plastics) Lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and cheap to mold into complex shapes. Generally much less stiff and less heat-resistant than metals. Examples: PVC, nylon, polyethylene.
Ceramics Extremely stiff and hard, and excellent under compression, but brittle — they fail suddenly with little warning, rather than deforming first. Examples: glass, concrete, alumina.
Composites Two or more materials combined to get properties neither has alone — typically a stiff, strong fiber embedded in a lighter matrix. Example: carbon fiber (carbon strands in an epoxy resin) is both stiffer and lighter than most metals, but usually far more expensive.

Ductile vs. brittle: a critical distinction

Definition A ductile material deforms substantially (stretches, bends, necks down) before it fractures — giving visible warning that failure is coming. A brittle material fractures suddenly, at or just past its yield point, with little or no visible deformation beforehand.
Why this matters more than raw strength A brittle material can be stronger than a ductile one by some measures and still be the worse engineering choice, because it fails without warning. Concrete (brittle) is almost always paired with steel reinforcement (ductile) in construction specifically because steel's visible bending gives inspectors and occupants advance warning that something is wrong, before a sudden, catastrophic failure.

Reading a stress-strain curve

Recall from the last lesson: below the yield point, stress and strain are proportional (Hooke's law, slope = E). A material's full stress-strain curve tells you much more than that one number:

Example Mild steel's stress-strain curve rises steeply and linearly (high E) up to a clear yield point, then flattens out over a long region where the material stretches a lot with little additional stress (ductile behavior), before finally breaking. A ceramic's curve, by contrast, rises linearly and then simply stops — it fractures almost immediately after leaving the linear region, with no flat "warning" section at all.

Density-normalized properties: why "strongest" depends on the question

Raw strength or stiffness isn't always what matters — often, strength or stiffness per unit weight matters more, especially in vehicles and aircraft where every kilogram has a cost.

Example Steel is stronger than aluminum in absolute terms, but aluminum has a better strength-to-weight ratio, which is why aircraft skin is usually aluminum (or increasingly, carbon-fiber composite) rather than steel — the weight savings matter more than raw strength for something that has to fly.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What distinguishes a ductile material from a brittle one?

Ductility is about visible warning before failure, not raw strength — a ductile material stretches noticeably before it finally breaks.

2. Why is concrete almost always reinforced with steel in construction?

Pairing brittle concrete with ductile steel means visible bending appears before a catastrophic failure, rather than the sudden fracture concrete alone would exhibit.

3. Why is aluminum, not steel, typically used for aircraft skin, despite steel being stronger in absolute terms?

Absolute strength isn't always the deciding property — strength or stiffness per unit weight often matters more, especially for anything that has to fly.

4. Fill in the blank: a material made of two or more combined materials, like carbon fiber in an epoxy matrix, is called a ___.

Composites combine materials to get a property profile — like high stiffness and low weight — that neither component achieves alone.

5. Fill in the blank: ceramics are extremely stiff and hard under compression, but they are ___ — failing suddenly with little warning.

Ceramics' brittleness is the key trade-off against their impressive stiffness and hardness.
