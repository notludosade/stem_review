Analog vs. Digital Signals

Continuous signals versus discrete ones, and why almost everything electronic converts between the two.

← Lesson 18 Unit 7, Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

The voltage and current from the last two lessons can vary in two fundamentally different ways: continuously, or in discrete steps. This lesson covers that distinction, and why it matters for nearly every modern electronic device.

Analog: continuously varying

Definition An analog signal varies continuously and can take on any value within a range — like a dimmer switch that smoothly adjusts brightness from fully off to fully on, passing through every intermediate level.
Example A microphone converts sound pressure waves directly into a continuously-varying voltage — as the sound gets louder or softer, the voltage smoothly rises and falls to match, with no discrete "steps" between values.

Digital: discrete steps

Definition A digital signal takes on only a fixed set of discrete values — almost always just two, representing binary 0 and 1 (typically as "low voltage" and "high voltage"), with nothing meaningful in between.
Example A light switch has exactly two states: off (0) and on (1) — there's no meaningful "half on" state the way a dimmer has a meaningful "half brightness." That binary, all-or-nothing quality is the defining feature of a digital signal.

Why digital signals resist noise so much better

Why this matters Every real signal picks up some electrical noise as it travels. An analog signal has no way to distinguish "this is the original signal" from "this is noise added on top" — any distortion directly corrupts the value being carried. A digital signal only needs to distinguish "closer to 0" from "closer to 1," so a moderate amount of noise can be added without ever changing which of the two states gets read. This noise resistance is the central reason digital systems dominate modern electronics, communications, and computing.

Where the two meet: sampling and conversion

The physical world — sound, temperature, light — is fundamentally analog. But most modern processing, storage, and transmission is digital. Bridging the two requires conversion in both directions:

Analog-to-digital conversion (ADC) Samples an analog signal's value at regular time intervals and rounds each sample to the nearest available digital level — this is exactly what happens when a microphone's continuous voltage becomes a digital audio file.
Digital-to-analog conversion (DAC) Reverses the process — converting stored digital values back into a continuously-varying voltage, which is exactly what happens when a digital audio file drives a physical speaker.
Why almost every electronic device does both A smartphone captures analog sound (microphone) and light (camera sensor), converts them to digital for processing and storage (where noise resistance and computation are essential), then converts back to analog to drive a speaker or a screen. This same analog-in, digital-processing, analog-out pattern repeats across nearly all modern electronics — it's not a special case, it's closer to the default architecture.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the key defining feature of a digital signal?

Digital signals use discrete levels — usually just two, representing binary 0 and 1 — unlike analog signals, which vary continuously.

2. Why do digital signals resist noise better than analog signals?

An analog signal's exact value directly carries the information, so any noise directly corrupts it — a digital signal only needs a coarse threshold decision, which tolerates much more noise.

3. Why does a smartphone need both analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog conversion?

Sound and light enter as analog signals, get converted to digital for noise-resistant processing and storage, then get converted back to analog to actually drive a speaker or screen.

4. Fill in the blank: converting a continuous analog voltage into discrete digital values by regularly sampling it is called ___ conversion.

ADC (analog-to-digital conversion) samples the analog signal at intervals and rounds each sample to the nearest digital level.

5. Fill in the blank: a dimmer switch, which smoothly adjusts brightness through every intermediate level, produces an ___ signal.

Continuous variation through every intermediate value, with no discrete steps, is the defining feature of an analog signal.
