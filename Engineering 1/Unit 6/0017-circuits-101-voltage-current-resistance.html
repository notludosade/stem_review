Circuits 101 — Voltage, Current, and Resistance

The water-pipe intuition that makes Ohm's law click, and the one equation underneath every circuit you'll ever analyze.

← Unit 5, Lesson 16 Lesson 18 → Contents Glossary →

Electrical engineering (from Unit 1's discipline survey) is built on three core quantities. This lesson introduces all three, plus the single relationship — Ohm's law — that ties them together.

The water-pipe analogy

Electricity is invisible, which makes it genuinely harder to build intuition for than forces or materials. A widely-used analogy: imagine water flowing through a pipe.

Voltage (V) The electrical "pressure" pushing charge through a circuit — like water pressure pushing water through a pipe. Measured in volts. Higher voltage means a stronger push.
Current (I) The rate at which electric charge actually flows — like the rate of water flow through the pipe. Measured in amperes (amps). Higher current means more charge passing a point per second.
Resistance (R) Opposition to current flow — like a narrow or clogged section of pipe restricting water flow. Measured in ohms (Ω). Higher resistance means it's harder for current to flow through that part of the circuit.
Where the analogy holds — and where to be careful The analogy is genuinely useful for building first intuition: more "pressure" (voltage) pushes more "flow" (current) through a given "pipe" (resistance), just as you'd expect. But it's an analogy, not a mechanism — actual electric charge flow involves electron movement in a conductor, which behaves quite differently from literal water molecules at a deeper level. Use the analogy to build intuition, not as a literal physical explanation.

Ohm's law: the one equation that ties them together

Definition Ohm's law states that voltage equals current times resistance: V = I × R. Equivalently, current equals voltage divided by resistance (I = V / R), or resistance equals voltage divided by current (R = V / I).
V = I × R
Example A 12-volt battery connected across a 4-ohm resistor: I = V / R = 12 / 4 = 3 amps of current flow. Double the resistance to 8 ohms with the same 12-volt battery, and the current halves to 1.5 amps — more "pipe restriction" for the same "pressure" means less "flow," matching the water-pipe intuition exactly.

Why this matters: it's the same F = ma of electrical engineering

Why Ohm's law is foundational Just as Newton's second law (F = ma) is the starting point for almost every mechanics problem in Unit 3, Ohm's law is the starting point for almost every basic circuit problem. Nearly every circuit calculation in this unit — and in real electrical engineering practice — traces back to applying V = I × R to some part of a circuit.

A circuit needs a closed loop

Definition A circuit is a closed loop that current can flow around, connecting a power source (like a battery) to one or more components (like resistors) and back to the source again. If the loop is broken anywhere — a disconnected wire, an open switch — no current flows at all, regardless of voltage.
Example A light switch works by physically breaking or completing the circuit loop. "Off" isn't reducing the current to a smaller value — it's breaking the loop entirely, dropping current to exactly zero, because there's no longer a closed path for it to flow around.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In the water-pipe analogy, what does resistance correspond to?

Resistance opposes current flow the same way a narrowed pipe section restricts water flow for a given pressure.

2. A 12-volt battery is connected across an 8-ohm resistor. What is the current?

I = V / R = 12 / 8 = 1.5 amps, directly from Ohm's law.

3. Why does turning a light switch "off" drop current to exactly zero, rather than just reducing it?

A circuit is a closed loop — break the loop anywhere and there's no path for current to flow around, so current is zero regardless of voltage.

4. Fill in the blank: the equation V = I × R, relating voltage, current, and resistance, is called ___.

Ohm's law is the electrical equivalent of Newton's second law for mechanics — the starting point for nearly every basic circuit calculation.

5. Fill in the blank: current is measured in ___, representing the rate at which electric charge flows past a point.

Amperes (amps) measure current, analogous to a flow rate in the water-pipe analogy.
