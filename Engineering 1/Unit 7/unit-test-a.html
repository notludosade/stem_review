1. A local building code requires that structural steel meet ASME standards. Which statement correctly distinguishes the code from the standard in this example?

Codes carry legal force and are typically adopted by government bodies; standards supply the detailed technical content, often incorporated into codes by reference.

2. Fill in the blank: codes and standards are overwhelmingly written or revised in direct response to documented ___, so future engineers don't have to relearn the same lesson the hard way.

A specific, investigated failure typically becomes the basis for a new or revised requirement, protecting every future engineer from repeating that exact mistake.

3. Why isn't "just follow best practice" sufficient on its own, compared to a binding code requirement?

A code requirement is enforced through inspection and permitting, turning a lesson into a floor that doesn't depend on any one engineer remembering it under pressure.

4. Fill in the blank: a code or standard sets a legal ___ that a design must clear — not itself a design goal — leaving engineering judgment to decide how far above it to actually design.

Meeting a code is a legal minimum; exceeding it where warranted is a matter of engineering judgment, not a code requirement.

5. A structural bolt fails at 6,000 N and is expected to experience 1,500 N in service. What is its factor of safety?

FS = failure stress / expected stress = 6,000 / 1,500 = 4.

6. A design team considers using a factor of safety of exactly 1 for a bridge cable, and separately considers a factor of safety of 50. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the main problem with a factor of safety of exactly 1?

(b) Fill in the blank: a very high factor of safety like 50 isn't free — it typically means added material, weight, and ___, which is why choosing a factor of safety is itself a trade-off.

Margin above 1 absorbs real-world uncertainty, but margin isn't free — more of it means more material, weight, and cost, so the choice of factor is a genuine trade-off.

7. Aerospace structural components are often designed with a factor of safety around 1.5, while elevator cables typically use a factor of safety of 10 or more. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does elevator design use such a larger margin than aerospace design?

(b) Fill in the blank: engineers don't use one universal factor of safety — the appropriate factor depends on how well the loads are known and how ___ a failure would be.

The factor of safety choice trades off load certainty and failure consequences against the real cost of extra margin — those trade-offs land very differently for elevators versus aircraft.

8. A component has a factor of safety of 6. Answer both parts:

(a) What is this component's margin of safety, as a ratio (MS = FS − 1)?

(b) Fill in the blank: expressed as a percentage, a margin of safety of 5 means the component can withstand ___% more than its expected load before failing.

MS = FS − 1 = 6 − 1 = 5, which corresponds to 500% extra capacity above the expected load.

9. A structural engineer discovers a building's support system doesn't meet code. The developer, facing a costly delay, orders it built anyway and promises to "fix it later." Answer both parts:

(a) According to the core principle of engineering ethics, what should the engineer do?

(b) Fill in the blank: the engineer cannot ethically point to the developer's instruction as a defense, because the obligation to hold paramount the safety of the ___ outranks obligations to any employer or client.

Because the public-first obligation outranks the client relationship, "the client told me to" is never a valid ethical defense for an unsafe design.

10. A licensed Professional Engineer formally certifies ("stamps") a design, and that design later fails due to negligence. Answer both parts:

(a) What does the PE stamp mean for that specific engineer in this situation?

(b) Fill in the blank: earning a PE license requires passing the FE exam, gaining supervised experience, and passing the ___ exam.

The PE stamp is an individually traceable, personal commitment — the licensure system's way of making "public first" enforceable rather than just aspirational.
