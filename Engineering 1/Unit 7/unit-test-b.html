1. An industry standard developed by IEEE specifies detailed technical requirements that a city's electrical code legally requires builders to follow. Which statement correctly distinguishes the two?

Codes carry legal force and are typically adopted by government bodies; standards supply the detailed technical content, often incorporated into codes by reference.

2. Fill in the blank: rather than being written speculatively, codes and standards are overwhelmingly created or revised after investigating a specific, documented ___.

A specific, investigated failure typically becomes the basis for a new or revised requirement, protecting every future engineer from repeating that exact mistake.

3. A contractor argues that following common industry "best practice," even without a formal code requirement, is just as reliable as a binding code. What is the flaw in this reasoning?

A code requirement is enforced through inspection and permitting, turning a lesson into a floor that doesn't depend on any one person remembering it under pressure.

4. Fill in the blank: meeting a code is a legal minimum, not a design goal in itself — codes set a ___ that good engineering judgment then decides how far to exceed.

Meeting a code is a legal minimum; exceeding it where warranted is a matter of engineering judgment, not a code requirement.

5. A component fails at 15,000 N and is expected to experience 3,000 N in service. What is its factor of safety?

FS = failure stress / expected stress = 15,000 / 3,000 = 5.

6. A design team debates between a factor of safety of 1 and a factor of safety of 30 for a component. Answer both parts:

(a) Why would a factor of safety of exactly 1 be dangerous in practice?

(b) Fill in the blank: choosing a factor of safety of 30 instead would add substantial material, weight, and ___ — showing that more margin isn't free.

Margin above 1 absorbs real-world uncertainty, but margin isn't free — more of it means more material, weight, and cost, so the choice of factor is a genuine trade-off.

7. A pressure vessel for a well-characterized industrial process uses a modest factor of safety, while a component holding back floodwater in an unpredictable natural setting uses a much higher one. Answer both parts:

(a) What best explains this difference?

(b) Fill in the blank: the appropriate factor of safety depends on how well the loads are known and how ___ a failure would be.

The factor of safety choice trades off load certainty and failure consequences against the real cost of extra margin — those trade-offs land very differently across applications.

8. A component has a factor of safety of 3. Answer both parts:

(a) What is its margin of safety, as a ratio (MS = FS − 1)?

(b) Fill in the blank: expressed as a percentage, a margin of safety of 2 means the component can withstand ___% more than its expected load before failing.

MS = FS − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2, which corresponds to 200% extra capacity above the expected load.

9. A component manufacturer instructs an engineer to approve a part the engineer knows fails a critical fatigue test, in order to hit a shipping deadline. Answer both parts:

(a) According to engineering ethics, what should the engineer do?

(b) Fill in the blank: engineering ethics ranks holding paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the ___ above any obligation to an employer or client.

Because the public-first obligation outranks the employer relationship, "the manufacturer told me to" is never a valid ethical defense for approving an unsafe part.

10. An unlicensed engineer at a firm claims that only the licensed PE who stamped a set of drawings can be held personally accountable if those drawings later prove negligent. Answer both parts:

(a) Is this claim accurate, and why?

(b) Fill in the blank: becoming a licensed PE requires passing the FE exam, gaining years of supervised experience, and then passing the ___ exam.

The PE stamp is an individually traceable, personal commitment — the licensure system's way of making "public first" enforceable rather than just aspirational.
