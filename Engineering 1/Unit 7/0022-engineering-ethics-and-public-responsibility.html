Engineering Ethics and Public Responsibility

Why "the client told me to" is never a defense, and what a licensed engineer's signature actually commits them to.

← Lesson 21 Unit 8, Lesson 23 → Contents Glossary →

Codes (Lesson 20) and safety factors (Lesson 21) are technical tools. This lesson covers the ethical foundation underneath them — why engineering carries a distinct kind of professional responsibility, different from most other jobs.

The core ethical principle: the public comes first

Definition Virtually every engineering code of ethics — including the NSPE Code of Ethics that governs licensed U.S. engineers — states some version of the same first principle: engineers must hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public. This obligation outranks obligations to an employer, a client, or even the engineer's own convenience or career.
Why this principle exists and ranks first Most professions primarily serve whoever is paying them. Engineering is different because engineering failures can harm people who never chose to be part of the transaction at all — a bystander under a collapsing structure, a driver on a bridge, a resident near a contaminated water supply. "Public first" exists specifically to protect people who have no seat at the table where the engineering decision gets made.

Why "the client told me to" is not a defense

Because the public-first obligation outranks the client relationship, an engineer cannot ethically excuse an unsafe design by pointing to who directed it. If a client or employer demands a design that the engineer knows to be unsafe, the engineer's ethical (and often legal) obligation is to refuse, or escalate — not to comply and defer responsibility upward.

Example A structural engineer discovers a building's support system doesn't meet code, but the developer, facing a costly delay, orders it built anyway and promises to "fix it later." The engineer who signs off regardless does not get to point to the developer's instruction as a defense — professionally and often legally, the responsibility to refuse rests with the engineer who understood the risk.

What licensure actually commits an engineer to

Definition In the U.S., becoming a Professional Engineer (PE) requires passing the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam, gaining several years of supervised experience, and then passing the Principles and Practice of Engineering (PE) exam. A licensed PE can legally "stamp" (formally certify) engineering designs — and that stamp is a personal, legal statement that the engineer takes responsibility for the design's safety and code compliance.
Why the stamp matters A PE stamp isn't a formality — it's a specific, individually traceable commitment. If a stamped design fails due to negligence, the licensed engineer who stamped it can face professional discipline (including losing their license) and legal liability, personally, distinct from the company they worked for. This is the licensure system's way of making "public first" enforceable rather than just aspirational: someone specific is accountable, by name.

Ethics as an extension of everything in this unit

Codes (Lesson 20) encode collective lessons from past failures. Safety factors (Lesson 21) build in margin against uncertainty. Ethics is the underlying reason both exist and get followed even under pressure — schedule pressure, cost pressure, a client's impatience — instead of being quietly skipped when no one seems to be watching.
