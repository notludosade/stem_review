Energy, Work, and Power

Three quantities every engineering discipline shares, tied together by one law that never breaks.

← Unit 4, Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Statics and materials, the last two units, are core mechanical engineering topics. Energy is different — it's the one vocabulary every discipline from Unit 1 shares: an electrical circuit, a chemical reactor, and a mechanical engine are all, at some level, devices for moving energy from one form to another.

Work: force acting through a distance

Definition Work (W) is the energy transferred when a force acts on an object as it moves through a distance: W = F × d (when the force is aligned with the direction of motion). Measured in joules.
W = F × d

Push a box with 50 N of force across 2 meters of floor, and you've done 100 joules of work on it — regardless of how quickly or slowly you did it. Note the specific requirement: a force that produces no motion (like holding a heavy box perfectly still) does zero work by this definition, even though it certainly feels effortful.

Energy: the capacity to do work

Definition Energy is the capacity to do work, and it exists in many forms: kinetic energy (motion), potential energy (stored, due to position — like height in a gravitational field), thermal energy (heat), chemical energy (stored in bonds), and electrical energy, among others.

Conservation of energy: the law that never breaks

Definition The law of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed — only converted from one form to another, or transferred from one object or system to another. Total energy before and after any process is always exactly equal.
Example A ball dropped from a height starts with potential energy and no kinetic energy. As it falls, potential energy converts into kinetic energy — at the instant just before it hits the ground, all of the original potential energy has become kinetic energy (ignoring air resistance). None of it vanished; it simply changed form.
Why this is a genuine law, not just a useful approximation Every attempt to build a machine that produces more energy than it consumes — a "perpetual motion machine" — has failed, without exception, across centuries of serious attempts by credible inventors. Conservation of energy is one of the most rigorously tested principles in all of physics, which is exactly why it's a safe foundation to build every other engineering calculation on top of.

Power: how fast energy transfers

Definition Power (P) is the rate at which energy is transferred or work is done: P = W / t, measured in watts (joules per second).
P = W / t

Power is what separates "how much energy" from "how fast." Lifting a heavy box slowly and lifting it quickly both do the exact same amount of work (same force, same distance) — but the quick lift requires far more power, because the same energy is delivered in far less time.

Example A 100-watt light bulb left on for 10 seconds uses 1,000 joules of energy (P × t = W). A 1,000-watt hair dryer used for the same 10 seconds uses 10,000 joules — ten times the energy, in the same time, because its power rating is ten times higher.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. According to the definition of work, why does holding a heavy box perfectly still (with no motion) do zero work, even though it feels effortful?

W = F × d requires actual displacement — force alone, without motion, transfers no energy by this definition.

2. What does the law of conservation of energy state?

Energy changes form — potential to kinetic, chemical to thermal — but the total amount before and after is always exactly conserved.

3. Lifting a box slowly and lifting the same box quickly to the same height both do the same amount of work. What's different between the two?

Power is the rate of energy transfer — same total work, done faster, means higher power, even though the work itself is identical.

4. Fill in the blank: power, the rate of energy transfer, is measured in ___ — joules per second.

P = W / t, and one watt equals one joule of work or energy transferred per second.

5. Fill in the blank: a falling ball's potential energy converts into ___ energy, the energy of motion, as it falls.

By the time the ball is just about to land, essentially all of its original potential energy has become kinetic energy.
