1. A warehouse worker holds a heavy box perfectly still for two minutes, exerting a large upward force the entire time. According to the physics definition of work, how much work does the worker do on the box?

W = F × d requires actual displacement — force alone, without motion, transfers no energy by this definition.

2. Fill in the blank: no machine has ever been built that produces more energy than it consumes — a so-called perpetual motion machine — because the law of ___ of energy forbids it.

Conservation of energy is one of the most rigorously tested principles in physics — every attempted perpetual motion machine has failed without exception.

3. Two workers each lift an identical 200 N crate the same 2-meter height — one takes 4 seconds, the other takes 1 second. Which statement correctly compares the work and power involved?

Both lifts do W = F × d = 200 × 2 = 400 J of work; power is the rate of that transfer, so the faster lift needs more power for the identical work.

4. Fill in the blank: energy stored due to an object's position, such as height in a gravitational field, is called ___ energy.

Potential energy is stored due to position; it converts into kinetic energy as an object like a falling ball begins to move.

5. A 500-watt space heater runs for 20 seconds. How much energy does it use?

Energy = P × t = 500 × 20 = 10,000 joules, directly from P = W / t.

6. A jet engine, treated as a system, takes in fuel and air and produces thrust, waste heat, and exhaust. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the main benefit of treating the jet engine this way — as a box with inputs and outputs — when analyzing something like its fuel economy?

(b) Fill in the blank: a system takes one or more inputs, transforms them through some internal process, and produces one or more ___.

Systems thinking lets an engineer zoom to whatever level of detail a specific question needs, treating the whole engine as one box when that's the right level.

7. A cruise control system measures a car's speed and adjusts the throttle to correct any deviation from the driver's set speed. Answer both parts:

(a) What type of feedback loop is this?

(b) Fill in the blank: unlike this cruise-control example, ___ feedback amplifies deviation instead of correcting it — the same mechanism behind a microphone's runaway screech near its own speaker.

Negative feedback is self-correcting and stable, which is exactly why engineers design most control systems around it rather than positive feedback.

8. An electric motor takes in 200 W of electrical power and delivers 170 W of useful mechanical power, the rest lost mostly to heat. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the motor's efficiency?

(b) Fill in the blank: efficiency is defined as useful energy ___ divided by total energy input.

η = useful output / total input = 170 / 200 = 0.85, or 85%.

9. A car engine converts only about 25% of gasoline's chemical energy into useful forward motion. Answer both parts:

(a) What happens to the other 75%, given that conservation of energy forbids it from simply vanishing?

(b) Fill in the blank: friction, electrical resistance, and sound losses in most real systems ultimately end up as ___, a form of energy the system usually can't use.

Conservation of energy means none of it vanishes — "lost" energy is really energy converted into a form the system wasn't built to use, primarily heat.

10. An inventor claims to have designed an engine that converts 100% of its fuel's energy into useful work, with zero losses. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is this claim considered physically impossible, rather than just difficult to achieve?

(b) Fill in the blank: because perfect efficiency is impossible, real engineering design work focuses on ___ losses — through better lubrication, insulation, or conductors — rather than eliminating them entirely.

This is a hard physical limit from the second law of thermodynamics — every real efficiency improvement reduces losses toward, but never reaches, the impossible 100% ceiling.
