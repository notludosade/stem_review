Systems Thinking — Inputs, Outputs, Feedback

Treating any device as a box that transforms inputs into outputs — and why feedback loops explain more than any single component.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Every engineering discipline eventually needs to reason about something too complex to analyze component by component. Systems thinking is the tool for that: a way of abstracting a complicated device into something you can reason about at a higher level.

A system as a box

Definition A system is anything that takes one or more inputs, transforms them according to some internal process, and produces one or more outputs. Drawing a system as a simple box — inputs going in, outputs coming out — lets you reason about its overall behavior without tracking every internal detail at once.
Example A car's engine, treated as a system: input is fuel and air, output is mechanical rotation (and waste heat and exhaust). You don't need to model every internal combustion event to reason about how the whole engine behaves — the box abstraction is often exactly the right level of detail for a given question.
Why abstraction is the point A modern car has thousands of individual parts. No single person reasons about all of them simultaneously. Systems thinking lets an engineer zoom to whatever level of detail a specific question actually needs — the whole car as one box when discussing fuel economy, or the engine's internal combustion process in detail when diagnosing a specific engine problem — without those two levels of analysis interfering with each other.

Feedback: outputs that influence future inputs

Definition A feedback loop exists when a system's output is measured and fed back to influence its own future input or behavior. Negative feedback pushes the system toward a stable target (correcting deviation). Positive feedback amplifies deviation, pushing the system further from where it started.
Negative feedback example A thermostat measures room temperature (output) and turns the heater on or off (adjusting the input) to correct any deviation from the target temperature. Too cold → heater turns on. Too warm → heater turns off. The loop actively works to cancel deviation, keeping the room stable near the target.
Positive feedback example A microphone placed too close to its own speaker picks up the speaker's output as new input, which gets amplified and output again, picked up again, amplified again — producing the runaway screech of audio feedback. Each cycle makes the deviation larger, not smaller.
Why this distinction matters in design Most engineered control systems are deliberately designed around negative feedback, specifically because it's self-correcting and stable. Positive feedback is usually something engineers actively design systems to avoid, precisely because — as with the microphone example — it tends to run away rather than settle down. When positive feedback does show up on purpose (some sensor circuits use it briefly), it's used narrowly and carefully, not as a general control strategy.

Why this generalizes across every discipline from Unit 1

A thermostat is electrical. An engine is mechanical. A chemical reactor with automatic temperature control is chemical engineering. All three are systems with feedback loops, reasoned about with exactly the same box-and-loop framework — this is what makes systems thinking a genuinely cross-disciplinary tool, not a mechanical- or electrical-specific one.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the main benefit of treating a complex device as a "system" — a box with inputs and outputs?

Abstraction lets an engineer zoom in or out to the right level of detail for a specific question, without needing to model everything simultaneously.

2. In a thermostat, why is the feedback loop classified as "negative feedback"?

Negative feedback specifically means the loop works to cancel deviation from a target, which is exactly what a thermostat's heater-control loop does.

3. Why do engineers generally design control systems around negative feedback rather than positive feedback?

The microphone-feedback-screech example shows how positive feedback amplifies deviation each cycle, which is precisely why it's avoided in most engineered control systems.

4. Fill in the blank: when a system's output is measured and fed back to influence its own future input, this is called a ___.

A feedback loop connects a system's output back to its own input, which can either stabilize (negative) or amplify (positive) the system's behavior.

5. Fill in the blank: a microphone squealing when placed too close to its own speaker is an example of ___ feedback.

Positive feedback amplifies the original signal each cycle, producing runaway growth rather than a stable, corrected output.
