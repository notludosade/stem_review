Efficiency and Losses in Real Systems

No real system converts energy perfectly — where the losses actually go, and why 100% efficiency isn't just hard, it's physically impossible.

← Lesson 15 Unit 6, Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

Conservation of energy (Lesson 14) says energy is never destroyed. So where does "lost" energy actually go when a system is described as inefficient? This lesson closes that gap.

Efficiency: useful output over total input

Definition Efficiency (η, the Greek letter eta) is the ratio of useful energy output to total energy input: η = useful output / total input, usually expressed as a percentage.
η = useful output / total input

A car engine converting gasoline's chemical energy into forward motion is typically only about 20-30% efficient. The rest isn't destroyed — conservation of energy forbids that — it's converted into a form the system wasn't built to use.

Where "lost" energy actually goes

Heat from friction Moving parts rubbing against each other (bearings, gears, air resistance) convert some useful mechanical energy into heat — energy that still exists, but is no longer doing the job the system was built for.
Electrical resistance Current flowing through a wire's resistance converts some electrical energy into heat (you'll see this precisely in Unit 6) — this is why phone chargers and laptop power bricks feel warm during use.
Sound Vibration and noise carry away a small amount of energy in essentially every mechanical and electrical system — usually a minor loss, but a real one.
Why "lost" is really "converted to an unwanted form" Conservation of energy guarantees the total energy is unchanged — none of it vanishes. "Efficiency losses" really means energy converted into a form the system has no use for (usually low-grade heat), rather than energy that has disappeared. This is precisely why efficiency and conservation of energy don't contradict each other — they're describing two different things: total quantity (always conserved) versus how much of that quantity ends up useful (rarely all of it).

Why 100% efficiency is physically impossible, not just hard

This isn't a limitation of current engineering — it's a hard physical limit. The second law of thermodynamics states that some energy is always converted to a less useful form (typically heat spreading into the surroundings) in any real energy conversion process. A theoretical 100%-efficient engine isn't merely unbuilt; it's provably impossible, in the same category of certainty as conservation of energy itself.

Example Even a nearly ideal system — a well-designed electric motor — tops out around 85-95% efficient, losing the rest mostly to heat in its windings and bearing friction. Compare that to a gasoline engine's 20-30%: both lose energy to the same kinds of places, just in very different proportions, which is exactly why electric drivetrains are pursued so heavily where efficiency matters.

Efficiency as a design driver

Because losses are unavoidable in principle, real design work focuses on minimizing them, not eliminating them: better lubrication reduces friction losses, better insulation reduces unwanted heat transfer, better conductors reduce electrical resistance losses. Every efficiency improvement in engineering history has been a reduction of losses toward — never all the way to — the impossible 100% ceiling.
