Capacitors and Capacitance

Two conductors, an insulating gap, and everything from Units 1–2 — Gauss's law and potential — combine to explain why a capacitor's ability to store charge depends only on its shape.

← Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Capacitance A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulating gap, storing charge +Q on one and −Q on the other at some potential difference V between them. Capacitance C = Q/V, measured in farads (F = C/V). C is a proportionality constant fixed by the capacitor's geometry — put more charge on the plates and V rises to match, so the ratio Q/V stays the same regardless of how much charge is actually there.
Worked Derivation — The Parallel-Plate Capacitor Two flat plates of area A, separated by distance d, carry charge ±Q. Gauss's law (a pillbox straddling one plate) gives the field between the plates: E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A), uniform between the plates. Integrating E over the gap gives the potential difference: V = Ed = Qd/(ε₀A). Then C = Q/V = Q ÷ [Qd/(ε₀A)] = ε₀A/d — the charge Q cancels out entirely, leaving a result that depends only on plate area and separation.
Why C Depends Only on Geometry C = ε₀A/d never mentions Q or V individually — only the shape of the capacitor. That's the whole point of defining capacitance as a ratio: it isolates the "how good is this container at storing charge per volt" property from however much charge happens to be sitting on it right now, the same way "5 gallons per foot of height" describes a cylindrical tank's shape independent of how full it currently is.
Worked Example A parallel-plate capacitor has A = 0.02 m² and d = 0.001 m. C = ε₀A/d = (8.85×10⁻¹²)(0.02)/(0.001) = (8.85×10⁻¹²)(20) = 1.77×10⁻¹⁰ F = 177 pF.

1. Capacitance C = Q/V is best understood as:

Adding more charge raises V proportionally, so Q/V stays fixed — that fixed ratio is what capacitance measures, and it's set entirely by geometry.

2. Fill in the blank: a parallel-plate capacitor has A = 0.015 m² and d = 0.0005 m. Its capacitance is ___ pF. (ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²))

C = ε₀A/d = (8.85×10⁻¹²)(0.015)/(0.0005) = (8.85×10⁻¹²)(30) = 2.655×10⁻¹⁰ F = 265.5 pF.

3. If a parallel-plate capacitor's separation d is doubled while its area stays fixed, its capacitance:

C = ε₀A/d is inversely proportional to d, so doubling the separation halves the capacitance.

4. In steady state, current does not flow across the gap between a capacitor's two plates because:

Charge accumulates on each plate's surface, but nothing physically crosses the insulating gap between them — that's precisely what makes it an insulator rather than a conductor.
