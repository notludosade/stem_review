Energy Stored in a Capacitor

The voltage across a charging capacitor isn't fixed — it climbs as charge builds up — which is exactly why the stored energy has a factor of ½ that a naive QV guess would miss.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Worked Derivation — Where the ½ Comes From Charging a capacitor means moving charge onto it a little at a time. When charge q has already accumulated, the voltage at that instant is V = q/C — so moving the next bit dq takes work dW = V dq = (q/C) dq. Total work charging from 0 up to final charge Q: W = ∫₀Q (q/C) dq = Q²/(2C). Using Q = CV, this is equivalent to U = ½QV = ½CV² = Q²/(2C) — all three forms are the same energy, just written with different variables eliminated via Q = CV.
Why It Isn't Simply U = QV U = QV would be correct only if every bit of charge were moved across the full final voltage V the whole time — true for, say, lifting a fixed charge across a fixed voltage. But a capacitor's voltage starts at 0 and rises linearly to V as it charges, so the charge added early on crosses almost no voltage, and only the very last bit crosses close to the full V. The integral effectively uses the average voltage during charging, which is V/2 — hence the factor of ½.
Worked Example A 4 μF capacitor is charged to 100 V. U = ½CV² = ½(4×10⁻⁶)(100)² = ½(4×10⁻⁶)(10,000) = ½(0.04) = 0.02 J = 20 mJ.

Try it — energy as area under the Q-V line

Since V = q/C rises linearly as charge builds up, the work integral ∫V dq is literally the shaded triangle's area — calculus and geometry giving the same answer.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which of the following is not a correct expression for the energy stored in a capacitor?

U = CV² is missing the factor of ½ that comes from the voltage rising linearly (not staying constant) as the capacitor charges — the correct form is U = ½CV².

2. Fill in the blank: a capacitor of 8 μF is charged to 50 V. The energy stored is ___ J.

U = ½CV² = ½(8×10⁻⁶)(50)² = ½(8×10⁻⁶)(2500) = ½(0.02) = 0.01 J.

3. The factor of ½ in U = ½QV exists because:

Integrating (q/C) dq from 0 to Q effectively uses the average voltage during charging, V/2, rather than the final voltage V the whole way through.

4. If the voltage across a fixed capacitor is doubled, the stored energy:

U = ½CV² depends on V², so doubling V quadruples U while C stays fixed.
