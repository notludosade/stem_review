1. A point charge of magnitude 4×10⁻⁶ C creates a field at a distance of 0.2 m. What is the field's magnitude? (k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²)

E = kQ/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(4×10⁻⁶)/(0.2)² = 35,960/0.04 = 899,000 ≈ 8.99×10⁵ N/C.

2. Two point charges, both +2 μC, sit 0.4 m apart. What is the electric field at the point exactly midway between them?

By symmetry, each +2 μC charge pushes a test charge away from itself and toward the other — at the exact midpoint the two contributions are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, summing to zero by superposition.

3. A uniform field of 800 N/C strikes a flat 0.1 m² surface at 30° from the surface's normal. What is the flux through the surface?

Φ_E = EA cos θ = (800)(0.1)(cos 30°) = (80)(0.866) ≈ 69.3 N·m²/C.

4. A Gaussian sphere of radius 0.1 m encloses a fixed charge Q. If you replace it with a larger concentric Gaussian sphere of radius 0.5 m, still enclosing that same charge Q and nothing else, the total flux through the surface:

Gauss's law, Φ_E = Q_enc/ε₀, depends only on the enclosed charge — the weaker field at larger radius is exactly offset by the larger surface area, leaving flux unchanged.

5. Outside a uniformly charged solid sphere (r > R), the electric field behaves as if:

Outside a spherically symmetric distribution, Gauss's law gives E = kQ/r² — indistinguishable from a point charge Q at the center.

6. A point charge of 9 μC sits at the origin. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the field magnitude at r = 0.3 m is ___ N/C.

(b) If the distance were instead tripled to 0.9 m, the new field magnitude would be:

(a) E = kQ/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(9×10⁻⁶)/(0.3)² = 80,910/0.09 = 899,000 ≈ 8.99×10⁵ N/C. (b) E is inversely proportional to r², so tripling r scales E by 1/3² = 1/9.

7. A uniform field of 400 N/C strikes a 0.25 m² surface head-on (θ = 0°). Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the flux through the surface is ___ N·m²/C.

(b) If the surface were instead rotated so θ = 90°, the flux would become:

(a) Φ_E = EA cos θ = (400)(0.25)(1) = 100 N·m²/C. (b) At θ = 90° the field grazes the surface edge-on and cos 90° = 0, so flux drops to zero.

8. A Gaussian surface encloses a +4 μC charge and a −1 μC charge; a separate +10 μC charge sits well outside the surface. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the total outward flux through the surface, rounded to 3 significant figures, is ___ N·m²/C. (ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²))

(b) The +10 μC charge sitting outside the surface:

(a) Q_enc = 4 − 1 = 3 μC = 3×10⁻⁶ C; Φ_E = Q_enc/ε₀ = 3×10⁻⁶/8.85×10⁻¹² ≈ 3.39×10⁵ N·m²/C. (b) Only enclosed charge sets the net flux — field lines from outside charges enter and exit the surface in equal measure, contributing zero net flux, though they do still bend the local field at any single point.

9. A uniformly charged solid sphere has R = 0.2 m and total charge Q = 8 μC. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: at r = 0.1 m (inside the sphere), the field magnitude is ___ N/C.

(b) At r = R exactly (the sphere's surface), the "inside" formula and the "outside" formula for E:

(a) Q_enc = Q(r/R)³ = 8(0.5)³ = 1 μC; E = kQ_enc/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(1×10⁻⁶)/(0.1)² = 8990/0.01 = 899,000 ≈ 8.99×10⁵ N/C. (b) Both E = kQr/R³ and E = kQ/r² evaluate to kQ/R² at r = R, so the field is continuous there.

10. An infinitely long, uniformly charged straight wire (linear charge density λ) needs to be analyzed with Gauss's law. Answer both parts:

(a) The correctly matched Gaussian surface for this symmetry is a:

(b) Fill in the blank: for a positively charged wire, the field points radially ___ from the wire's axis.

(a) Cylindrical symmetry needs a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface, on which E is constant in magnitude and radially directed over the curved side. (b) Positive line charge produces a field pointing radially outward, away from the axis, at every point around it.
