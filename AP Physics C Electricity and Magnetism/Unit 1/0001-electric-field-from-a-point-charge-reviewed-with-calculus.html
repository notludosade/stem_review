Electric Field from a Point Charge, Reviewed with Calculus

An algebra-based course hands you E = kQ/r² as a fact to plug numbers into. This course rebuilds electricity and magnetism from calculus instead — starting by treating the field as a vector function of position, not a formula.

Lesson 2 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Electric Field as a Vector Function A point charge Q at the origin creates a vector field E(r) = (kQ/r²) r̂ at every point in space, where r is the distance from the charge and r̂ is the unit vector pointing from the charge to the field point. E(r) is a function you can evaluate anywhere — not a single number computed for one specific pair of charges. Sign convention used throughout this course: a positive Q produces E pointing along +r̂ (away from the charge); a negative Q produces E pointing along −r̂ (toward the charge).
Method — Superposition, and Why It Becomes an Integral For several point charges, the net field at any point is the vector sum of each charge's individual field: E_net = ΣEᵢ (superposition). For a continuous distribution of charge — a rod, ring, disk, or sphere — there's no longer a finite list of charges to sum. Instead you slice the object into infinitesimal charge elements dq, each contributing a field dE = k dq/r² r̂, and superposition becomes an integral: E = ∫dE. Lesson 5 carries this out in full; this lesson's job is just to make E(r) feel like a function first.
Why This Is the Right Starting Point Every remaining idea in Units 1–2 is a consequence of treating E as a vector field: flux (Lesson 2) measures how much of this field pierces a surface, Gauss's law (Lesson 3) relates that flux to enclosed charge, and potential (Unit 2) turns out to be an integral of E along a path. None of that language makes sense if E is just "a number you compute per problem" — it has to be a function of position from the start.
Worked Example — Superposition on an Axis A charge q₁ = +3 μC sits at x = 0, and q₂ = −5 μC sits at x = 0.4 m. Find the field at x = 0.2 m (the midpoint).
E₁ (from q₁, positive ⟹ points away from q₁, i.e. +x): kq₁/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(3×10⁻⁶)/(0.2)² ≈ 6.74×10⁵ N/C, direction +x.
E₂ (from q₂, negative ⟹ points toward q₂, which is also +x from this point): kq₂/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(5×10⁻⁶)/(0.2)² ≈ 1.12×10⁶ N/C, direction +x.
Both fields point the same way here, so they add directly: E_net ≈ 6.74×10⁵ + 1.12×10⁶ ≈ 1.80×10⁶ N/C in the +x direction. (If the point weren't on the line joining the charges, you'd need to add the two vectors component by component instead.)

1. Using this course's sign convention, the electric field produced by a negative point charge at some field point points:

Negative charges produce fields pointing along −r̂ — toward the source — the opposite of a positive charge's outward field.

2. For a continuous object like a charged rod, why is E = kQ/r² not directly usable, forcing an integral instead?

Every infinitesimal charge element dq is at its own distance r and direction r̂ from the field point, so superposition over infinitely many elements becomes an integral, E = ∫k dq/r² r̂.

3. Fill in the blank: a point charge of 1 μC creates a field, at a distance of 0.1 m, of magnitude ___ N/C. (Use k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C².)

E = kQ/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(1×10⁻⁶)/(0.1)² = 8990/0.01 = 899,000 N/C = 8.99×10⁵ N/C.

4. The superposition principle states that the net electric field from several point charges is:

Superposition says fields add like vectors: E_net = E₁ + E₂ + E₃ + …, magnitude and direction both accounted for.
