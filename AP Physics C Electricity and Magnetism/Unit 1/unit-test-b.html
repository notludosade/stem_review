1. A point charge of magnitude 6×10⁻⁶ C creates a field at a distance of 0.3 m. What is the field's magnitude? (k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²)

E = kQ/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(6×10⁻⁶)/(0.3)² = 53,940/0.09 = 599,333 ≈ 5.99×10⁵ N/C.

2. Two point charges, both +3 μC, sit 0.6 m apart. What is the electric field at the point exactly midway between them?

By symmetry, each +3 μC charge pushes a test charge away from itself and toward the other — at the exact midpoint the two contributions are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, summing to zero by superposition.

3. A uniform field of 600 N/C strikes a flat 0.15 m² surface at 45° from the surface's normal. What is the flux through the surface?

Φ_E = EA cos θ = (600)(0.15)(cos 45°) = (90)(0.7071) ≈ 63.6 N·m²/C.

4. A Gaussian cube of edge 0.2 m encloses a fixed charge Q. If you replace it with a larger cube of edge 0.6 m, still enclosing that same charge Q and nothing else, the total flux through the surface:

Gauss's law, Φ_E = Q_enc/ε₀, depends only on the enclosed charge — the weaker field at larger distance is exactly offset by the larger surface area, leaving flux unchanged.

5. Outside a uniformly charged spherical shell (all charge spread over the surface, radius R), the electric field for r > R behaves as if:

Outside a spherically symmetric distribution — whether solid or a thin shell — Gauss's law gives E = kQ/r², indistinguishable from a point charge Q at the center.

6. A point charge of 16 μC sits at the origin. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the field magnitude at r = 0.4 m is ___ N/C.

(b) If the distance were instead quadrupled to 1.6 m, the new field magnitude would be:

(a) E = kQ/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(16×10⁻⁶)/(0.4)² = 143,840/0.16 = 899,000 ≈ 8.99×10⁵ N/C. (b) E is inversely proportional to r², so quadrupling r scales E by 1/4² = 1/16.

7. A uniform field of 300 N/C strikes a 0.3 m² surface head-on (θ = 0°). Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the flux through the surface is ___ N·m²/C.

(b) If the surface were instead rotated so θ = 90°, the flux would become:

(a) Φ_E = EA cos θ = (300)(0.3)(1) = 90 N·m²/C. (b) At θ = 90° the field grazes the surface edge-on and cos 90° = 0, so flux drops to zero.

8. A Gaussian surface encloses a +6 μC charge and a −2 μC charge; a separate +15 μC charge sits well outside the surface. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the total outward flux through the surface, rounded to 3 significant figures, is ___ N·m²/C. (ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²))

(b) The +15 μC charge sitting outside the surface:

(a) Q_enc = 6 − 2 = 4 μC = 4×10⁻⁶ C; Φ_E = Q_enc/ε₀ = 4×10⁻⁶/8.85×10⁻¹² ≈ 4.52×10⁵ N·m²/C. (b) Only enclosed charge sets the net flux — field lines from outside charges enter and exit the surface in equal measure, contributing zero net flux, though they do still bend the local field at any single point.

9. A uniformly charged solid sphere has R = 0.3 m and total charge Q = 27 μC. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: at r = 0.1 m (inside the sphere), the field magnitude is ___ N/C.

(b) At r = R exactly (the sphere's surface), the "inside" formula and the "outside" formula for E:

(a) Q_enc = Q(r/R)³ = 27(1/3)³ = 27/27 = 1 μC; E = kQ_enc/r² = (8.99×10⁹)(1×10⁻⁶)/(0.1)² = 8990/0.01 = 899,000 ≈ 8.99×10⁵ N/C. (b) Both E = kQr/R³ and E = kQ/r² evaluate to kQ/R² at r = R, so the field is continuous there.

10. An infinite, uniformly charged flat sheet needs to be analyzed with Gauss's law. Answer both parts:

(a) The correctly matched Gaussian surface for this symmetry is a:

(b) Fill in the blank: for a positively charged sheet, the field points ___ from the sheet on both sides, perpendicular to it.

(a) Planar symmetry needs a pillbox Gaussian surface, whose flat end caps catch the perpendicular field while its curved side contributes zero flux. (b) Positive sheet charge produces a field pointing outward, perpendicular to the sheet, on each side.
