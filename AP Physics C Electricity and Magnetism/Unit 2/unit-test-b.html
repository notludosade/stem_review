1. A point charge of 3 μC creates a potential at a distance of 0.3 m. What is that potential? (k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²)

V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(3×10⁻⁶)/(0.3) = 26,970/0.3 = 89,900 ≈ 8.99×10⁴ V.

2. Two point charges, both +4 μC, sit 1.2 m apart. What is the potential at the point exactly midway between them (0.6 m from each)?

Potential is a scalar: V₁ = V₂ = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(4×10⁻⁶)/(0.6) ≈ 59,933 V each, and they add directly (unlike the field, which does cancel here by symmetry): V_net ≈ 59,933 + 59,933 ≈ 119,867 ≈ 1.20×10⁵ V.

3. A ring of radius 0.4 m carries total charge 6 μC. What is the potential on its axis at x = 0.3 m from the center? (r = √(x²+R²) = 0.5 m)

V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(6×10⁻⁶)/(0.5) = 53,940/0.5 = 107,880 ≈ 1.08×10⁵ V, using r = √(x²+R²), not x or R alone.

4. The angle between the electric field and the equipotential surface passing through any given point is always:

Since V doesn't change along an equipotential surface, E can have no component along it — the two are always perpendicular.

5. Just outside the surface of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field is oriented:

A surface-parallel component would keep driving charge to move along the surface, contradicting equilibrium — so E must be perpendicular to the surface right at it.

6. A point charge of 2 μC sits at the origin. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the potential at r = 0.1 m is ___ V.

(b) If r were instead tripled (charge unchanged), the new potential would be:

(a) V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(2×10⁻⁶)/(0.1) = 17,980/0.1 = 179,800 V. (b) V falls off as 1/r, one power of r slower than E's 1/r² — tripling r scales V by 1/3, not 1/9.

7. Two point charges, both +9 μC, sit 1.0 m apart. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the potential at the point exactly midway between them (0.5 m from each) is ___ V.

(b) Compared to the electric field at that same midpoint, this potential:

(a) V₁ = V₂ = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(9×10⁻⁶)/(0.5) = 80,910/0.5 = 161,820 V; V_net = 161,820 + 161,820 = 323,640 V. (b) The field vectors from two equal charges point opposite ways at the midpoint and cancel to zero, but potential is scalar and both contributions add — a direct illustration of why V and E don't have to agree in behavior.

8. A ring of radius 0.4 m carries total charge 5 μC. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: the potential on its axis at x = 0.3 m (r = √(x²+R²) = 0.5 m) is ___ V.

(b) For x much larger than R, V(x) approaches:

(a) V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(5×10⁻⁶)/(0.5) = 44,950/0.5 = 89,900 V. (b) When x ≫ R, √(x²+R²) ≈ x, so V(x) ≈ kQ/x — any localized charge looks like a point charge from far enough away.

9. An isolated conductor is in electrostatic equilibrium. Answer both parts:

(a) Fill in the blank: one point on its surface is measured at V = 620 V. At the conductor's interior center, V = ___ V.

(b) Any excess charge on this conductor must reside entirely on its outer surface because:

(a) A conductor in equilibrium is one single equipotential object — every point, interior or surface, shares the same V = 620 V. (b) Since E = 0 on any Gaussian surface drawn just inside the conductor, Gauss's law forces Q_enc = 0 there, stranding all excess charge on the outer surface.

10. Moving a charge along an equipotential surface. Answer both parts:

(a) Moving a positive test charge along an equipotential surface requires:

(b) Fill in the blank: if E had a nonzero component along a surface, V would have to ___ as you moved along that surface — contradicting what "equipotential" means.

(a) W = qΔV = q(0) = 0 anywhere on a single equipotential surface, regardless of path. (b) A surface-parallel field component would mean V changes along the surface — but "equipotential" means V is constant there, so E can only point perpendicular to it.
