Electric Potential as an Integral of the Field

Potential isn't a separate idea from the field — it's what you get by integrating the field along a path. Differentiate it and you get the field right back.

← Lesson 3 Lesson 5 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Electric Potential Taking V(∞) = 0 as the reference, the electric potential at a point r is V(r) = −∫∞r E·dl — the negative of the field integrated along any path from infinity in to r (the result doesn't depend on which path, since the electric field is conservative). Equivalently, E = −dV/dr along that same direction: field is potential's derivative, potential is field's integral.
Worked Derivation — Recovering V = kQ/r For a point charge Q, integrate radially inward from infinity: V(r) = −∫∞r (kQ/r'²) dr' = −kQ[−1/r']∞r = −kQ(−1/r − 0) = kQ/r. This is the same V = kQ/r formula from an algebra-based course — except now it's a derived result of integrating E(r), not a fact handed to you.
Why Potential Is Often Easier Than Field E is a vector at every point; combining several charges' fields means adding vectors component by component. V is a scalar (just a number) at every point; combining several charges' potentials is ordinary addition: V_net = ΣVᵢ, no angles or components required. Many problems are far easier solved by finding V first (scalar superposition) and only then differentiating to get E, than by adding field vectors directly.
Worked Example — Potential from Two Charges (Scalar Superposition) q₁ = +4 μC at x = 0, q₂ = −2 μC at x = 0.5 m. Find V at x = 0.2 m.
V₁ = kq₁/r₁ = (8.99×10⁹)(4×10⁻⁶)/(0.2) = 179,800 V.
V₂ = kq₂/r₂ = (8.99×10⁹)(−2×10⁻⁶)/(0.3) ≈ −59,933 V.
V_net = V₁ + V₂ ≈ 179,800 − 59,933 ≈ 119,867 V — plain addition, no vector components needed.

1. Taking V(∞) = 0, the electric potential at a point is defined as:

V(r) = −∫E·dl from infinity to r; the field is conservative, so the result is path-independent.

2. Fill in the blank: a point charge of 1 μC sits at the origin. The potential at r = 0.1 m is ___ V. (k = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C²)

V = kQ/r = (8.99×10⁹)(1×10⁻⁶)/(0.1) = 8990/0.1 = 89,900 V.

3. For a positive point charge, V(r) = kQ/r decreases as r increases. Since E = −dV/dr, this means E is:

dV/dr = −kQ/r² (negative, since V falls with r), so E = −dV/dr = +kQ/r² — positive, pointing outward, exactly matching the point-charge field from Lesson 1.

4. Combining the potentials of several point charges at one location requires:

Potential is a scalar field, so superposition is plain arithmetic addition — one of the main reasons it's often easier to work with than E directly.
