Series and Parallel Resistor Networks

Resistors in series simply add. Resistors in parallel add reciprocally — the exact opposite pattern from how capacitors combine, so keep the two straight.

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Definition — Series Resistors Resistors connected end-to-end along one path share the same current (nowhere for charge to branch off), and each drops its own share of the total voltage. R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + … — resistances add directly. Adding a resistor in series always increases total resistance.
Definition — Parallel Resistors Resistors connected across the same two nodes share the same voltage, and current splits between the branches according to each branch's own resistance. 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + … — reciprocals add. Adding a resistor in parallel always decreases total resistance (it opens up another path, so R_eq always ends up smaller than the smallest individual resistor in the group).
Why This Is the Opposite Pattern From Capacitors Resistors in series add directly; resistors in parallel add reciprocally. Capacitors do the exact opposite: capacitors in series add reciprocally (1/C_eq = Σ1/Cᵢ), and capacitors in parallel add directly (C_eq = ΣCᵢ). The physical reason: putting resistors in series lengthens the path charge has to push through (R = ρL/A grows with L), while putting capacitors in series effectively increases the total gap between the outermost plates (harder to store charge per volt, so C shrinks like a reciprocal sum). Don't carry the resistor pattern over to capacitors, or vice versa — memorize them as a contrasting pair.
Worked Example R₁ = 4 Ω and R₂ = 12 Ω in parallel: 1/R_eq = 1/4 + 1/12 = 3/12 + 1/12 = 4/12 = 1/3, so R_eq = 3 Ω — smaller than either individual resistor, exactly as expected for a parallel combination.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Three resistors, 2 Ω, 5 Ω, and 3 Ω, are connected in series. Their equivalent resistance is:

Series resistances add directly: R_eq = 2 + 5 + 3 = 10 Ω.

2. Fill in the blank: R₁ = 6 Ω and R₂ = 3 Ω are connected in parallel. R_eq = ___ Ω.

1/R_eq = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2, so R_eq = 2 Ω.

3. Unlike resistors, capacitors connected in series combine by:

Series capacitors add reciprocally and series resistors add directly — the two rules are exact opposites, which is exactly why it's worth double-checking which component you're combining before applying either rule.

4. Two resistors, 8 Ω and 40 Ω, are connected in parallel. Which of the following must be true of R_eq?

Adding a parallel path always opens up more room for current to flow, so R_eq always ends up smaller than the smallest individual branch resistance — here, R_eq = 1/(1/8 + 1/40) ≈ 6.67 Ω, indeed less than 8 Ω.
