Identity and Access Management

Every managed service in this unit assumes one thing is already solved: the system knows exactly who's asking, and exactly what they're allowed to do. That system is IAM, and it's the single most consequential piece of "security in the cloud" from Lesson 2.

← Lesson 14 Unit 6, Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 2 drew a line: the provider secures the cloud, the customer secures what they build in it. Nowhere is that customer-side responsibility more consequential than access control — deciding exactly who, or what, is allowed to touch each of the compute, storage, and managed service resources this course has covered. Get this wrong, and every other lesson's careful architecture is one leaked credential away from a very bad day.

What IAM actually manages

Definition Identity and Access Management (IAM) is a system for defining identities (a specific human user, or a non-human identity like an application or service) and permissions (exactly which actions that identity is allowed to take on which resources), and enforcing that rule set on every single request, automatically. A request to delete a storage bucket, deploy new code, or read a database isn't just "does this credential work" — it's checked against IAM's rules for whether this specific identity is allowed to do this specific action on this specific resource, every time.

Roles and policies: bundling permissions sensibly

Definition A policy is the actual rule set defining which actions are allowed or denied. A role is a named bundle of policies assigned to an identity — instead of granting permissions one at a time to every individual user, an administrator defines roles like "Developer" or "Billing Viewer" once, each with an appropriate bundle of permissions, and then assigns identities to whichever role fits their job.

A stolen credential can only do what its role was ever allowed to do — that's the whole point of the next section.

The principle of least privilege

Definition The principle of least privilege means granting every identity only the minimum permissions it actually needs to do its job, and nothing more. A "Read-Only Analyst" role that genuinely cannot delete anything, deploy code, or view billing isn't being overly cautious for no reason — it's a deliberate limit on how much damage a single mistake, or a single stolen credential, can possibly cause.
Why IAM misconfiguration is the most common real-world cloud breach Lesson 2's worked example — a database left open with no password — is a shared responsibility failure, but the deeper, more common version of that same mistake is an IAM policy granting far more access than a role actually needs: a marketing analytics tool with permission to delete production databases, or every employee's laptop holding admin-level credentials "just in case." When (not if) a credential eventually leaks — through a phishing email, a misplaced API key, or a compromised laptop — least privilege is what limits the blast radius to whatever that one over-permissioned identity could reach, instead of the entire cloud account.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does IAM check on every single request, not just once at login?

IAM evaluates identity, action, and resource together on every request, not just credentials at login.

2. What is the relationship between a role and a policy?

Roles bundle policies together so permissions can be assigned by job function instead of one at a time.

3. Why does the principle of least privilege reduce the damage from a single stolen credential?

Least privilege doesn't prevent theft — it limits what a stolen credential can actually do once compromised.

4. Fill in the blank: the system for defining identities and permissions, and enforcing them on every resource request, is called ___.

Identity and Access Management (IAM) governs who can do what to which resource.

5. Fill in the blank: the principle of granting every identity only the minimum permissions it actually needs, and nothing more, is called the principle of ___.

Least privilege limits how much damage any single mistake or compromised credential can cause.
