Managing Risk

Identifying what could go wrong before it does, and why a risk register is a decision tool, not paperwork.

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 11 covered planning the work you know about. This lesson covers the work you don't yet know about — the risks that could disrupt a plan — and the standard tool for managing them.

What counts as a risk

Why "risk" means something specific here A risk is a potential future event that hasn't happened yet — a supplier might miss a delivery date, a key team member might leave, a test might reveal a design flaw. This is different from an issue, which is a problem that has already occurred. Risk management is specifically about the "might," before it becomes a "did."

Two dimensions: likelihood and impact

Why both dimensions matter A risk that's very likely but low-impact (a minor part shipping a day late) usually deserves less attention than one that's unlikely but severe (a single-source supplier going out of business entirely). Rating each identified risk on both likelihood and impact — often as a simple high/medium/low grid — is what lets a team prioritize which risks actually deserve a response plan.

The risk register

What it contains A risk register lists each identified risk along with its likelihood, its impact, a planned response if it occurs, and who owns watching for it. It's reviewed and updated regularly throughout a project, not written once and filed away.
Why it's a decision tool, not paperwork Treating a risk register as a compliance formality — filled out once to satisfy a process requirement — defeats its entire purpose. Its real value is forcing an explicit answer, in advance, to "what would we actually do if this happens?" so that when a risk does materialize, the response is a decision that was already made calmly, not one improvised under pressure.

Four standard responses to an identified risk

Avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept Avoid: change the plan so the risk can't occur at all. Mitigate: take action now to reduce its likelihood or impact. Transfer: shift the risk to someone else, such as through insurance or a contract clause. Accept: acknowledge the risk and decide, deliberately, to proceed without further action — appropriate for risks that are unlikely, low-impact, or too costly to address relative to the exposure.
Example A single-source supplier risk might be mitigated by qualifying a second supplier in advance, or accepted deliberately if switching costs would exceed the actual risk of disruption — either way, the choice is made explicitly and recorded, rather than left undecided until the supplier actually fails to deliver.

1. How does a "risk" differ from an "issue" in project management terms?

Risk management is specifically about things that might happen, addressed before they turn into things that did happen.

2. Why does a risk register need to rate both likelihood and impact, rather than just one or the other?

Both dimensions together are what let a team correctly prioritize which risks actually deserve a planned response.

3. Why does treating a risk register as a one-time compliance formality defeat its purpose?

A risk register's value comes from the advance thinking it forces, not from the document's existence as a compliance artifact.
