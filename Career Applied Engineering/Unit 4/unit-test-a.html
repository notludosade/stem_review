1. A stakeholder asks a project lead to add a major new feature without moving the deadline or adding budget. According to the iron triangle, what must happen?

Scope, schedule, and budget are structurally linked — the iron triangle says at most two can be held fixed; the third (or quality) has to move.

2. A new project lead is handed the assignment "build the footbridge" and needs to turn it into tasks that can actually be estimated and assigned to individual team members. What tool is built for exactly this?

A WBS decomposes large, unestimatable work like "build the bridge" into small tasks like load calculations or peer review, each assignable to one person or small team.

3. A project manager notices Task X can be finished two days faster by adding another engineer, but Task X isn't on the critical path. What effect will speeding up Task X have on the project's finish date?

Only tasks on the critical path determine the earliest possible finish date; speeding up an off-path task doesn't shorten the timeline.

4. A supplier has already missed a delivery date, and the team is now dealing with the fallout. Is this best described as a "risk" or an "issue" in project management terms?

A risk is a potential future event; once it has actually happened, it's an issue, not a risk.

5. Fill in the blank: a risk register's real value is that it functions as a ___ tool — forcing an explicit answer, in advance, to what a team would actually do if each risk occurred.

Treating the register as a one-time compliance formality defeats its purpose; its value is the calm, advance decision-making it forces.

6. A team lead is asked to add a feature with no schedule or budget change. Answer both parts about how the iron triangle actually plays out in a case like this:

(a) Why is the trade-off described as "iron" rather than just a general tendency?

(b) Fill in the blank: if scope, schedule, and budget are all held fixed under this pressure, ___ is usually the constraint that silently gives instead.

(a) The relationship is a structural limit, not something skill can work around. (b) Quality is the fourth, often-invisible variable that absorbs pressure when the other three are locked.

7. A schedule was built at kickoff, and three months in, one task has taken three times longer than planned. Answer both parts:

(a) What does a Gantt chart make visible that a simple task list doesn't?

(b) Fill in the blank: because the task overran so badly, the schedule should be updated immediately rather than left as-is — a project plan is meant to be treated as a ___ document, not a one-time forecast.

(a) A Gantt chart reveals parallel work and the critical path at a glance. (b) Treating the plan as fixed regardless of new information produces false confidence; it must be updated as reality changes.

8. A team is comparing two risks: a minor part that might ship one day late, and a single-source supplier that could go out of business entirely. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does a risk register rate each risk on both likelihood and impact, rather than either alone?

(b) Fill in the blank: even though the single-source supplier failing is unlikely, it still deserves serious attention because its ___ would be severe.

(a) Both dimensions together let a team correctly prioritize which risks deserve a planned response. (b) A rare but high-impact risk can outweigh a common but minor one — impact matters as much as likelihood.

9. A firm relies on a single supplier for a critical part. Answer both parts about how the team could respond to that risk:

(a) The team decides to qualify a second supplier in advance, specifically to lower the odds of a disruption. Which risk response is this?

(b) Fill in the blank: if the team instead purchases insurance to cover the cost of a supply disruption, that response is called ___ — shifting the risk to someone else.

(a) Proactively reducing likelihood or impact before a risk occurs is mitigation. (b) Shifting the risk itself, such as through insurance or a contract clause, is called transfer.

10. A stakeholder casually asks for "one more feature, real quick" without offering to move the deadline or add staff. Answer both parts about how to handle this conversation well:

(a) What is the best way to respond, based on how the iron triangle should be used in a real conversation?

(b) Fill in the blank: naming the trade-off explicitly turns an ambiguous request into a concrete ___ that the requester can actually make.

(a) Making the cost of a scope change visible, instead of absorbing it invisibly, is the whole point of naming the trade-off. (b) This turns vague pressure into an explicit decision someone can consciously choose.
