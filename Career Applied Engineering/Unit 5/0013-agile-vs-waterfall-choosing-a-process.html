Agile vs. Waterfall: Choosing a Development Process

Two different answers to "how much do we plan before we start," and when each one actually fits.

← Unit 4, Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 4 covered the tools for planning and tracking work. This lesson covers a higher-level choice that shapes how those tools get used: how much of a project should be planned up front, versus discovered as work proceeds.

Waterfall: plan fully, then execute

The waterfall approach Waterfall moves through distinct phases in sequence — requirements, design, implementation, testing, delivery — completing each fully before the next begins. Requirements are locked in early, and the whole project is planned in detail up front.
Why waterfall fits some projects well When requirements are genuinely stable and well understood in advance — a bridge whose loads and span are fixed by physical site conditions, for instance — planning fully before building avoids costly rework, since changing physical construction mid-project is far more expensive than changing a plan on paper.

Agile: plan a little, build, learn, repeat

The agile approach Agile breaks work into short, repeated cycles (often called sprints) — each one planning a small, achievable slice, building it, and reviewing the result before planning the next slice. Requirements are expected to change as real feedback comes in, and the process is built around adapting to that rather than resisting it.
Why agile fits some projects well When requirements are genuinely uncertain up front — building software where user needs only become clear once people try an early version — committing to a fully detailed plan before any real feedback exists risks building the wrong thing carefully and on schedule. Short cycles let a team correct course based on what's actually been learned, rather than what was guessed at the start.

The real choice: how much can you know in advance?

Why this is the deciding question Neither approach is universally better — the deciding factor is how much of the real requirements can genuinely be known before work starts. Physical, safety-critical, or highly regulated projects tend to have requirements fixed by physics, code, or contract, which favors waterfall's up-front planning. Projects where the right answer only emerges through real use and feedback favor agile's short, adaptive cycles.
Example A structural engineer designing a permanent bridge cannot "iterate" on the number of support piers after concrete is poured — the physical constraints demand waterfall-style upfront rigor. A software team building a new mobile app feature can ship a small version, watch how people actually use it, and adjust — a case where iterating cheaply is not just possible but usually produces a better outcome than guessing correctly up front.

Hybrid approaches are common in practice

Many real projects mix the two — an engineering firm might use waterfall for a fixed physical structure but agile for the accompanying software or firmware, or use waterfall for overall project phases while running agile-style short cycles within a single phase. The underlying question — how much can genuinely be known in advance for this particular piece of work — matters more than picking one label and applying it uniformly.
