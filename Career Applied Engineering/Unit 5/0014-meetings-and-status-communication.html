Meetings and Status Communication

Why most meetings fail before they start, and how to write a status update someone will actually read.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Whatever process a team uses (Lesson 13), it still runs on two everyday communication habits: meetings and status updates. Both are easy to do badly and cheap to do well.

Why most meetings fail before anyone speaks

Why the failure happens this early A meeting without a clear, stated purpose fails at the moment it's scheduled, not during the discussion — attendees show up unsure what decision or output the meeting is even for, so the conversation drifts. The single highest-leverage fix is stating, in the invitation itself, exactly what needs to happen by the end: a decision made, a specific question answered, information shared that requires real-time discussion.
Example "Weekly sync" as a meeting title gives attendees no way to prepare or know if they're even needed. "Decide which of two vendor proposals to accept — bring your cost comparison" tells every attendee exactly what's expected of them and lets people who aren't needed skip it entirely.

The three things every meeting needs

Purpose, the right people, a written outcome A clear purpose (stated above), only the people who are actually needed for that purpose (not everyone tangentially related), and a brief written record of what was decided and who owns each resulting action. Without the third, a meeting's outcome exists only in attendees' memory — which fades and diverges within days.

Information that doesn't need a meeting at all

Why a status update often replaces a meeting A lot of what gets shared in status meetings — what's done, what's in progress, what's blocked — doesn't require real-time discussion at all, only real-time availability to read it. A well-written status update lets each reader absorb that information on their own schedule, which is strictly cheaper than requiring a group of people to be in the same room or call at the same time for information that doesn't actually need a live conversation.

Writing a status update people actually read

A structure that works Three short sections cover almost every situation: what shipped or got done since the last update, what's actively in progress, and what's blocked (with what's needed to unblock it, and who could help). Leading with the most decision-relevant information — especially anything blocked — respects the fact that most readers skim rather than read every word in order.
Why "blocked" deserves top billing A blocker is the one status a reader can actually act on — everything else is just information. Burying it at the bottom of a long update, under a wall of completed-task detail nobody needs to act on, delays exactly the kind of help that a status update is supposed to make possible.
