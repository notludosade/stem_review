Unit 1 — Resumes, Portfolios, and Applications

Claim-evidence-result

A resume-writing structure: state what you did, the specific evidence or method behind it, and the quantified result — instead of vague duty-language like "responsible for."

Engineering portfolio

A collection of real project write-ups showing the problem, constraints, design decisions and trade-offs, and — most valuably — what went wrong and what was learned, not just a polished final result.

Required vs. preferred qualifications

Required qualifications are near-mandatory for a role; preferred qualifications are a wish list. Tailoring an application means matching the required qualifications precisely without fabricating experience.

Unit 2 — The Technical Interview

Interview pipeline

The typical multi-stage sequence an engineering candidate moves through: recruiter screen, technical screen, technical or design interview, behavioral interview, onsite or final round — each testing something different.

Whiteboard / design problem

An open-ended design prompt worked through live, structured by clarifying the problem, stating assumptions out loud, sketching a rough approach before refining it, narrating trade-offs, and stating what to validate next.

STAR method

A four-part structure for behavioral interview answers: Situation (context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you specifically did — the longest part), Result (the quantified outcome and what you learned).

Unit 3 — Working as a Professional Engineer

Documentation's real audience

A future reader — often the same author much later, or a teammate — who has real engineering background but no memory of the current context in the writer's head. Good documentation is written for that reader specifically.

Translating a trade-off

Communicating a technical trade-off to a non-engineer in terms relevant to their actual decision (options, costs, risks) rather than explaining the underlying technical mechanism in depth.

Work-directed feedback

Feedback phrased to critique the work itself ("this bracket might not hold the load") rather than the person ("you didn't think this through") — the phrasing that invites engagement instead of defensiveness.

Unit 4 — Project Management Basics

Iron triangle

The linked project constraints of scope, schedule, and budget — fixing any two forces the third to move; quality is the quieter fourth variable that often silently absorbs pressure instead.

Work breakdown structure (WBS)

Decomposing a large project into progressively smaller phases, tasks, and subtasks, until each piece is small enough to estimate and assign with confidence.

Gantt chart

A chart showing each task as a bar on a timeline, making parallel work and task dependencies visible at a glance.

Critical path

The single chain of dependent tasks that determines a project's earliest possible finish date — speeding up tasks off this path doesn't shorten the project.

Risk register

A document listing each identified risk with its likelihood, impact, planned response, and owner — reviewed and updated throughout a project, not filed away once.

Avoid / mitigate / transfer / accept

The four standard responses to an identified risk: change the plan to eliminate it, act now to reduce it, shift it to someone else (e.g. insurance), or deliberately proceed without further action.

Unit 5 — Process and Practice

Waterfall

A development process that moves through distinct phases in sequence, completing each fully before the next begins, based on requirements planned and locked in up front — fits work with genuinely stable, well-known requirements.

Agile / sprint

A development process built around short, repeated cycles (sprints) that plan a small slice of work, build it, and review before planning the next — fits work whose requirements are genuinely uncertain up front.

Status update structure

A written update covering what shipped, what's in progress, and what's blocked — with blocked items leading, since they're the one category a reader can actually act on.

Conflict of interest

Arises whenever a personal interest could influence a professional judgment, even without changing the outcome. The professional obligation is to disclose it, not to guarantee it never exists.

Whistleblowing

Reporting a serious safety or ethical concern outside the organization — typically a last resort, appropriate only after internal channels have been tried and failed to address a genuine, serious risk.

Unit 6 — Licensure and Career Growth

Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam

Typically taken near graduation, tests broad undergraduate engineering knowledge. Passing it earns "Engineer in Training" status, not a full license.

Principles and Practice of Engineering (PE) exam

Taken after several years of qualifying supervised experience under a licensed PE, tests discipline-specific applied judgment. Passing it, plus the experience requirement, earns the full Professional Engineer license.

Specialist

A career path built on deep, hard-to-replicate expertise in one narrow technical area.

Generalist

A career path built on working competence across many areas, often connecting systems or disciplines that specialists don't cross as often.

Management path

A career path shifting the primary work from producing technical output directly to enabling other people's technical output — a genuinely different job, not simply a promotion from strong technical work.
