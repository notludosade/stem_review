Working with Non-Engineers

Translating technical trade-offs for managers, clients, and other departments without dumbing them down.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

Almost no engineer works only with other engineers. Managers, clients, salespeople, and other departments all make decisions that depend on understanding engineering trade-offs — without having the technical background to evaluate the details themselves. This lesson covers how to communicate with them effectively.

The core skill: translate the trade-off, not the mechanism

Why mechanism-first explanations fail with this audience Explaining how a technical system works, in depth, to someone who needs to make a business or scheduling decision usually buries the one thing they actually need: what are the options, and what does each one cost or risk? A non-engineer stakeholder needs the trade-off translated into terms relevant to their decision — not a simplified engineering lecture.
Example: the same fact, two audiences To another engineer: "We could use a lookup table instead of recomputing the value each time, trading memory for speed." To a product manager: "We can make this feature respond noticeably faster, but it'll use more of the device's memory — on older phones that could cause other apps to run slower. I'd recommend it, but wanted you to know the trade-off." Same underlying fact, translated to what each audience can actually act on.

"Dumbing down" versus translating

Why translating isn't the same as oversimplifying Removing the trade-off itself — "don't worry about it, it'll just be faster" — isn't simplification, it's withholding information the stakeholder needs to make an informed call, particularly if that trade-off could affect their own decisions later. Good translation keeps every decision-relevant fact intact and only removes the technical mechanism the listener doesn't need in order to decide.

Framing trade-offs as decisions, not just information

Example Instead of just describing a risk, framing it as an explicit choice — "we can ship on the original date with this known limitation, or take two extra weeks to fix it properly; here's my recommendation and why" — gives a non-engineer stakeholder something concrete to actually decide, rather than a fact they don't know what to do with.

Why credibility compounds over many of these conversations

Why this matters over time A non-technical stakeholder can't independently verify most engineering claims — they're relying on you being honest about real trade-offs, including inconvenient ones. An engineer who reliably surfaces real risks (not just good news) earns a level of trust that makes every future conversation easier; one who consistently downplays problems to seem more agreeable erodes it, often permanently, the first time a downplayed risk actually materializes.

1. Why does an in-depth, mechanism-first explanation typically fail when talking to a non-technical stakeholder?

A stakeholder making a business or scheduling decision needs the trade-off, not a deep dive into how the underlying mechanism works.
