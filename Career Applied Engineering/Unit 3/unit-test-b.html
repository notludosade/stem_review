Unit 3 Test — Version B

Working as a Professional Engineer. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. Six months after shipping a feature, an engineer opens their own old code and can't remember why a particular timeout value was chosen, since no comment or document explains it. What does this situation illustrate about documentation's real audience?

Documentation is written for a reader without the current context — and that reader is frequently a future version of the very person who wrote the code.

2. Fill in the blank: a comment that just restates what the code already shows adds little value — the more useful comment explains ___ a particular approach or value was chosen.

Documentation's real value is usually explaining why a decision was made — reasoning that disappears once its author is no longer around to ask.

3. A new engineer joins a project and opens the README hoping to get the code running quickly, but instead finds pages of design trade-off discussion with the actual setup steps mentioned only briefly near the end. What went wrong here?

A README needs to get a reader running quickly; deep design trade-offs belong in a separate document meant for a rarer, deeper read.

4. A teammate's test suite has a gap that let a bug through. Which response is most likely to keep the conversation productive rather than defensive?

Naming the specific gap in the work, rather than characterizing the person, raises the same concern while inviting engagement instead of defensiveness.

5. Fill in the blank: instead of telling a teammate "this needs work," pointing at a ___ issue — "the retry logic doesn't handle a timeout on the third attempt" — actually gives them something to fix.

Specific feedback names an exact, fixable issue; a vague impression gives the recipient nothing concrete to act on.

6. A design decision that took a team two weeks to work out was never written down anywhere, and the only engineer who remembers the reasoning is on extended leave. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the practical risk this situation creates for the team right now?

(b) Fill in the blank: work that functions but was never documented quietly becomes a ___ the moment the person who understood it is unavailable.

(a) Without the documented reasoning, the team risks changing the system in a way that breaks an assumption nobody wrote down. (b) That's exactly what makes undocumented work a liability — its safety depends entirely on one person staying available.

7. An engineer explains a proposed database change to a sales director using a detailed walkthrough of indexing internals and query execution plans. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is this explanation likely to fail with this particular audience?

(b) Fill in the blank: the core skill in this kind of conversation is translating the ___ — not the underlying mechanism — into terms the listener can actually act on.

(a) A stakeholder making a decision needs the trade-off itself, not a deep technical walkthrough of the mechanism. (b) That trade-off — the real options, costs, and risks — is exactly what needs to be translated, not removed.

8. An engineer tells a client, "this update will just make things smoother," without mentioning that it will also delay another planned feature by two weeks. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does this count as "dumbing down" rather than a good translation?

(b) Fill in the blank: presenting the choice as an explicit ___ — "we can ship the update now with the delay, or wait and avoid it" — gives the client something concrete to decide.

(a) Good translation keeps every decision-relevant fact intact while removing only the unneeded mechanism — this example strips out a fact the client needed instead. (b) Framing the choice as a named decision, rather than a vague reassurance, is what gives the client something to actually act on.

9. Answer both parts about two related professional habits:

(a) An engineer tells a client "everything's on track" for months, then reveals a major risk only once it can no longer be hidden. Why does this damage trust more than raising the risk earlier would have?

(b) A different engineer responds to nearly every code review comment by explaining why the reviewer is wrong, even when the comment is valid. What is the long-term effect of this pattern on the team?

(a) Since a stakeholder can't independently verify engineering claims, trust is the entire basis of the relationship — and it breaks hard the first time a downplayed risk turns out real. (b) The same dynamic applies to feedback: defensiveness makes giving it feel not worth the friction, so a team quietly stops catching mistakes early.

10. A colleague snaps, "this whole approach is wrong," during a design review, offering no further detail. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the recommended professional response to feedback delivered this way?

(b) Fill in the blank: an engineer who habitually responds ___ to critique — explaining why they were right all along — trains their team to stop offering feedback at all.

(a) Reacting to the delivery instead of the substance risks dismissing a real, valid catch. (b) Habitual defensiveness is what most reliably makes giving feedback feel not worth the friction, which is why it's the single biggest failure mode here.
