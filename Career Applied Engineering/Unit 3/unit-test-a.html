Unit 3 Test — Version A

Working as a Professional Engineer. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. An engineer writes a setup guide using shorthand and unexplained internal jargon, reasoning that "anyone on the team would already know this." Why is this a risky assumption?

Documentation matters most for a reader — often a future version of yourself, or a teammate — who lacks the context the writer currently holds in their head.

2. Fill in the blank: code can usually show how something works to a careful reader, but documentation's real value is explaining ___ — why this approach was chosen over an alternative.

The reasoning behind a decision is exactly the information that disappears once the person who knew it moves on — that's what documentation is best positioned to preserve.

3. An engineer writes a project's README file as a long document covering every design alternative that was considered and why each was rejected, with the actual setup instructions buried on page four. What's the problem with this document?

A README needs to get a new reader running quickly; deep design reasoning belongs in a separate design document meant for a rarer, deeper read.

4. A reviewer notices a flaw in a teammate's circuit design. Which phrasing is more likely to lead to a productive technical conversation?

Critiquing the work rather than the person raises the same concern while inviting engagement with the problem instead of defensiveness.

5. Fill in the blank: "this could be better" gives a recipient nothing to act on — good feedback needs to be ___ and actionable, pointing at an exact, fixable issue.

Vague feedback gives a recipient nothing concrete to change — specificity is what turns feedback into an actual next step.

6. An engineer who wrote a critical piece of firmware leaves the company without ever documenting why several key constants were set the way they were. Answer both parts:

(a) What happens to that undocumented knowledge once the engineer is gone?

(b) Fill in the blank: undocumented but functioning work quietly becomes a ___ the moment its original author is unavailable.

(a) The "why" behind a decision disappears once the person who knew it is gone, leaving only code that shows how, not why. (b) That's precisely what makes undocumented work a liability — it's fragile the instant the one person who understood it leaves.

7. An engineer explains a proposed change to a product manager by walking through, in full technical depth, exactly how a new caching layer would be implemented at the code level. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is this explanation likely to fail with this particular audience?

(b) Fill in the blank: the core skill in communicating with non-engineers is translating the ___ — not the technical mechanism — into terms relevant to the listener's actual decision.

(a) A stakeholder making a business or scheduling decision needs the trade-off, not a deep dive into the underlying mechanism. (b) That's the trade-off itself — options, costs, risks — which is what actually needs translating.

8. An engineer tells a client, "don't worry about the technical details, it'll just work better," omitting the fact that the change also increases memory use on older devices. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does this qualify as "dumbing down" rather than good translation?

(b) Fill in the blank: framing a trade-off as an explicit ___ — "we can do A, or B, with these consequences" — gives a non-engineer stakeholder something concrete to act on.

(a) Good translation keeps every decision-relevant fact intact and only removes the mechanism the listener doesn't need — this example removes a fact instead. (b) Framing the trade-off as a named decision, rather than inert information, is what gives the stakeholder something to actually respond to.

9. Answer both parts about two related professional habits:

(a) Why does an engineer who consistently downplays real risks to stakeholders tend to lose credibility over time?

(b) Why does an engineer who habitually reacts defensively to feedback end up hurting their own work over time?

(a) A non-technical stakeholder can't independently verify engineering claims, so trust is the basis of the relationship — and it erodes sharply once a downplayed risk turns out real. (b) In the same way, a team stops bothering to flag issues once giving feedback stops feeling worth the friction, so problems surface later and more expensively instead of being caught early.

10. A senior engineer bluntly tells a teammate, "this retry logic is broken," with no further explanation or softening. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the recommended professional response to feedback delivered this bluntly?

(b) Fill in the blank: an engineer who habitually responds ___ to critique trains their team to stop offering feedback at all, even when the critique is valid.

(a) Reacting to poor delivery instead of evaluating the substance risks missing a real, valid technical catch. (b) Consistent defensiveness is the habit that most reliably shuts down a team's willingness to keep giving feedback.
