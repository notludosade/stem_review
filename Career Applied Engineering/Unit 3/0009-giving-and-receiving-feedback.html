Giving and Receiving Feedback

Why technical feedback is a professional skill in itself, not just a byproduct of being right.

← Lesson 8 Unit 4, Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

Being technically correct is necessary but not sufficient for engineering feedback to actually improve anything. This lesson covers how to give feedback that gets acted on, and how to receive it in a way that actually helps you improve.

Giving feedback: critique the work, not the person

Why this distinction matters "This design has a weak point at the mounting bracket" invites a technical conversation. "You didn't think this through" invites defensiveness instead — even when the underlying technical observation is identical. The distinction isn't about being gentler for its own sake; it's about which phrasing actually gets the recipient to engage with the problem rather than defend themselves.
Example: the same observation, two ways Person-directed: "You clearly didn't consider the load case here." Work-directed: "I think this bracket might not hold up under the maximum load case — have you checked it against that?" The second version raises the exact same concern while leaving room for the possibility that it was considered and there's a good reason it's fine — an important possibility to leave open, since you might simply be missing context.

Being specific and actionable

Why vague feedback doesn't help "This could be better" gives the recipient nothing to actually change. Specific feedback ("the retry logic doesn't handle a timeout on the third attempt") points at an exact, fixable issue. The goal of feedback is a concrete next step for the recipient, not a general impression of your opinion.

Receiving feedback: separate the signal from the delivery

Why this matters Feedback is sometimes delivered poorly — bluntly, or in a way that feels personal — even when the underlying technical point is valid. Reacting to the delivery instead of evaluating the substance means potentially missing a real, useful catch. The professional habit is to extract the technical claim first ("is this actually a problem?") and address the delivery, if it needs addressing, as a separate conversation.

Why defensiveness is the single biggest failure mode

Example An engineer who responds to every critique with an explanation for why they were right all along trains their team to stop offering feedback at all — not because the feedback was wrong, but because giving it stopped being worth the friction. The habit that keeps feedback flowing is responding to a valid critique with something closer to "good catch, let me fix that," even when it stings.

Why this compounds the same way non-engineer trust does

Why this connects to Lesson 8 Just as consistently downplaying risk to stakeholders erodes trust over time (Lesson 8), consistently reacting defensively to feedback erodes a team's willingness to catch your mistakes early — when they're still cheap to fix — rather than after they've shipped.

The technical content can be identical, but work-directed phrasing gets a recipient to engage with the actual issue rather than defend themselves.

2. When receiving feedback that's delivered bluntly, what is the recommended professional habit?

Reacting to poor delivery instead of evaluating the substance risks missing a real, valid technical catch.

3. Why does an engineer who habitually responds defensively to critique end up hurting themselves in the long run?

Teammates stop bothering to flag issues once giving feedback stops feeling worth the friction — meaning problems surface later and more expensively instead of being caught early.
