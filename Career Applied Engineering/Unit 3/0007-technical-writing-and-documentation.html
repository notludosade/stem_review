Technical Writing and Documentation

Why engineering documentation exists for a reader who isn't you, six months from now — and how that changes what you write.

← Unit 2, Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

Units 1 and 2 covered getting hired. This unit covers what the job actually looks like day to day, starting with the skill most engineers underrate: writing documentation other people — including a future version of yourself — will actually depend on.

The real audience: not you, right now

Why this reframing matters While writing documentation, everything feels obvious — you just built the thing. Six months later, or to a teammate who never touched this part of the system, none of it is obvious. Good documentation is written for that future reader specifically: someone with real engineering background, but zero memory of the specific decisions and context you currently hold in your head.

What documentation should actually answer

What, not just how Code or a design can usually show how something works to someone willing to read it closely. Documentation's real value is explaining why — why this approach was chosen over an alternative, why a particular constant has the value it does, why an edge case is handled the way it is. The "why" is exactly the information that disappears once the person who knew it moves to another project or leaves the team.
Example A comment reading "retry count is 3" restates what the code already shows. A comment reading "retry count is 3 because the vendor's API times out under load roughly 1 in 4 calls, and testing showed 3 attempts clears it 99% of the time" preserves the reasoning nobody could recover just by reading the code itself.

Matching the format to the reader's actual need

Quick reference vs. full explanation A README or setup guide needs to get a new reader running quickly — short, scannable, task-oriented. A design document needs to preserve reasoning for a much rarer, deeper read — the trade-offs considered, not just the final choice (the same trade-off-first structure Lesson 2's portfolio write-ups use). Writing a design document's worth of context into a README buries the one thing a new reader actually needs: how to get started.

Why documentation is a professional obligation, not an afterthought

Why this matters Undocumented but functioning work quietly becomes a liability the moment its author is unavailable — on vacation, moved to another team, or gone. Documentation is what lets engineering knowledge outlive the specific person who created it, the same underlying idea Unit 7 of Engineering 1 covered for codes and standards: making a hard-won lesson available to people who weren't there when it was learned.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Who is the real intended audience for most engineering documentation?

Documentation matters most for a reader — often a future version of yourself — who no longer holds the same context the writer currently has.

2. What does documentation typically provide that code alone usually cannot?

Code can usually show how something works to a careful reader; the "why" behind a decision is the information documentation is best positioned to preserve.

3. Why does writing a design document's worth of context directly into a README cause a problem?

Different documents serve different reading needs — a README should be scannable and task-oriented, not loaded with deep design reasoning that belongs elsewhere.

4. Fill in the blank: documentation's real value is often explaining ___ a decision was made, since that reasoning disappears once the person who knew it is no longer around.

The "why" behind a decision — not just what the decision was — is the specific knowledge that's otherwise lost when its author becomes unavailable.

5. Fill in the blank: undocumented but functioning work quietly becomes a ___ the moment its original author is unavailable, since the reasoning behind it leaves with them.

Work that only one person understands is fragile the instant that person is gone — documentation is what makes engineering knowledge outlive any one individual.
