Unit 1 Test — Version B

Resumes, Portfolios, and Applications. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. A resume bullet reads: "Responsible for maintaining the lab's 3D printers." A reviewer flags it as weak. What's the most accurate diagnosis?

Duty-language bullets describe a role, not a result — a hiring manager can't tell strong performance from minimal upkeep from a sentence like this.

2. Fill in the blank: "cut assembly defects by an ___ 15%" is a more convincing resume claim than "improved assembly quality," because it signals the change was actually measured.

A defensible estimate — even without a lab-verified exact number — shows that impact was actually measured, which is what makes a technical reader trust the claim.

3. A mentor tells a student, "write your bullets like a lab report, not like a job description." Answer both parts:

(a) Why does this advice work especially well for an engineering hiring manager audience?

(b) Fill in the blank: claim, ___, and result is the structure a resume bullet borrows from a lab report to earn a technical reader's trust.

(a) A resume that provides evidence for its claims, the way a lab report does, mirrors a standard engineers already hold each other to. (b) Evidence is the piece that turns a bare claim into something a hiring manager can actually evaluate.

4. Fill in the blank: a class project that once anchored a resume can usually move into a detailed ___ entry once real project experience exists to take its place on the resume itself.

A portfolio has room to carry that same material in depth, freeing the resume for the strongest, most verifiable results available.

5. A hiring manager reviews a portfolio page showing a single glossy photo of a completed drone, captioned "Built a quadcopter." What is missing that would make this entry actually convincing?

A finished photo proves an object exists; it doesn't show the reasoning process a hiring manager is actually trying to evaluate.

6. A student has time to either add three more shallow one-line project entries to their portfolio, or expand on one existing project with its trade-offs and failure points. Which serves the portfolio's actual purpose better?

A portfolio is trying to prove you can reason well about the work you've done, not that you've simply done a large number of things.

7. A student's write-up for a solar-charger project jumps straight from "the goal" to "the final circuit," skipping everything in between. Answer both parts:

(a) What should be added right after the goal is stated, before describing the final design?

(b) Fill in the blank: explaining the ___ between the alternatives that were considered, and why one won, is what makes a write-up demonstrate real design reasoning.

(a) Constraints give the design decisions real context — without them, "why this design" can't be evaluated. (b) Naming what was gained and given up between alternatives is what shows genuine reasoning, not just a final result.

8. A candidate with strong experience in both structural analysis and manufacturing process design is applying to a role focused almost entirely on structural analysis. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the appropriate way to handle the manufacturing experience on this specific application?

(b) Fill in the blank: this kind of reordering is what tailoring actually means — ___ on true accomplishments, not invention of new ones.

(a) The experience stays real and present, just reordered so the reader's first priority is answered fastest. (b) Emphasis — not fabrication — is the entire mechanism of honest tailoring.

9. A posting lists "proficiency in Python" as required and "experience with embedded Linux" as preferred, and the phrase "cross-functional collaboration" shows up three separate times in the text. Answer both parts:

(a) How should an applicant treat the "preferred" qualification compared to the "required" one?

(b) Fill in the blank: a phrase like "cross-functional collaboration" that is ___ multiple times across a posting is a signal of what the role actually emphasizes day to day.

(a) Required qualifications filter the initial pool; preferred ones differentiate within it, so a strong application still addresses preferred items directly. (b) Repetition across a posting usually reflects genuine priority, not incidental phrasing.

10. The same candidate applies to a research-focused engineering role and a production-floor engineering role, using the identical unmodified resume for both. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the likely consequence of using one unmodified resume for both roles?

(b) Fill in the blank: a strong cover letter answers "why this role, and why you" directly, instead of just paragraph-form ___ of what the resume already lists.

(a) The same true resume can look like a strong or weak fit purely based on which accomplishments it foregrounds for a given reader's priorities. (b) A cover letter's distinct job is making that specific connection explicit — merely restating the resume wastes that opportunity.
