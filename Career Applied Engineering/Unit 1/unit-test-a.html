Unit 1 Test — Version A

Resumes, Portfolios, and Applications. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

1. Two engineering students write resume bullets for the same internship task. Student A writes "Responsible for testing prototype units." Student B writes "Tested 40 prototype units against spec, flagging a wiring defect that affected roughly 1 in 5 units before shipment." Why is Student B's bullet stronger?

A strong bullet names the action, the target, and a verifiable result — duty language like "responsible for" only describes that a role existed, not how well it was performed.

2. Fill in the blank: even without an exact figure, a defensible ___ — such as "reduced setup time by roughly 30%" — is more convincing on a resume than an unquantified claim like "improved setup time."

A reasonable estimate signals that impact was actually measured, not just asserted — that's the distinction a hiring manager is really evaluating.

3. An applicant is deciding how to structure a resume bullet for an engineering hiring manager. Answer both parts:

(a) Why do engineering hiring managers respond especially well to a bullet structured as claim, evidence, and result?

(b) Fill in the blank: a resume bullet structured like a lab report — claim, ___, result — earns more trust from an engineering hiring manager than a duty description does.

(a) Engineers are trained to distrust unverifiable claims, so a resume that provides evidence for its claims earns more trust from this specific audience. (b) The middle term of that structure — evidence — is what turns an assertion into something a technical reader can actually trust.

4. Fill in the blank: once real project experience exists, a strong ___ can carry the kind of depth that a generic coursework listing no longer needs to occupy limited resume space with.

A portfolio has room for the depth a one-page resume doesn't, freeing the resume to focus on strongest, most verifiable results.

5. A student's engineering portfolio consists of ten polished photos of finished projects, each with a one-line caption. What is the main weakness of this portfolio, according to best practice?

A hiring manager evaluating engineering ability is looking for evidence of reasoning and judgment — a finished-result photo with a caption doesn't provide that on its own.

6. A candidate is deciding between writing up ten projects in a sentence each, or three projects in full depth (problem, trade-offs, failure, result). Which approach does best practice favor, and why?

Depth demonstrates the reasoning process a hiring manager actually wants evidence of; breadth without depth doesn't provide that evidence no matter how many projects are listed.

7. A student is drafting a portfolio write-up for a robotics project. Answer both parts:

(a) Which piece of context should the write-up establish before describing the final design?

(b) Fill in the blank: describing the alternatives considered and the ___ that led to the final design choice demonstrates real design reasoning, not just a reported result.

(a) Constraints define what the design actually had to satisfy — without them, "why this design" has no real context. (b) Naming the trade-offs between alternatives is what shows genuine design reasoning rather than a bare outcome.

8. A candidate has real experience in both embedded firmware and mechanical CAD, and is applying to a firmware-focused role that lists no CAD requirement. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the honest, appropriate way to handle the CAD experience on this application?

(b) Fill in the blank: tailoring an application is fundamentally about ___ — choosing which true accomplishments to lead with — not inventing experience that doesn't exist.

(a) Tailoring means reordering true, existing accomplishments so the reader's first question gets answered fastest — not deleting real experience or fabricating new experience. (b) That reordering is what "emphasis" means here, as distinct from invention.

9. A job posting lists five "required" qualifications and three "preferred" qualifications, and the phrase "regulatory compliance" appears in the title, summary, and two bullet points. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the practical difference between the posting's required and preferred qualifications?

(b) Fill in the blank: language like "regulatory compliance" that is ___ across a posting's title, summary, and requirements usually signals what the role actually emphasizes day to day.

(a) Required items filter the initial pool; preferred items differentiate within it, so both deserve an explicit response. (b) Recurring language across a posting reflects genuine priority, worth mirroring honestly where it truly applies.

10. A candidate is applying to two different "mechanical engineer" postings — one emphasizing CAD design, the other emphasizing hands-on manufacturing and testing. Answer both parts:

(a) Why can the same true resume read as a strong fit for one posting and a weak fit for the other?

(b) Fill in the blank: a cover letter that merely ___ the resume's bullet points in paragraph form wastes its one distinct purpose — connecting specific experience to this specific posting's need.

(a) The same underlying candidate can look like very different fits depending on which real accomplishments are emphasized for a given posting's priorities. (b) A cover letter that only restates the resume skips its actual job: making an explicit, specific case for this particular fit.
