Unit 2 Test — Version B

The Technical Interview. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

← Course Contents Version A →

1. During a first-round call, a recruiter asks about a candidate's availability, salary expectations, and willingness to relocate, but asks no technical questions at all. What is this call most likely testing?

The recruiter screen filters out basic mismatches early, before a more expensive technical interviewer's time is spent on them.

2. Fill in the blank: given a prompt like "design a system to do X," the recommended first move is to ___ the problem — asking about scale, constraints, and what success actually looks like — before proposing anything.

Real prompts are usually deliberately underspecified, and clarifying questions are themselves part of what's being evaluated.

3. A candidate hears a design prompt and immediately starts sketching a detailed final architecture on the whiteboard without asking any questions first. What risk does this create?

The problem is testing reasoning process, not a lucky first guess — jumping straight to a solution skips the part that's actually being evaluated.

4. In response to "tell me about a time you missed a deadline," a candidate says only, "It happens sometimes, and I just try to learn from it and move on." Why is this a weak behavioral answer?

A behavioral question is looking for evidence of a real, specific situation and how it was handled — a generality like this gives none.

5. Fill in the blank: the four-part structure of Situation, Task, Action, and Result is commonly known as the ___ method for answering behavioral interview questions.

STAR gives a repeatable shape to a real story so it contains concrete, evaluable substance instead of a vague generality.

6. A hiring process includes a 30-minute call where an engineer asks a candidate detailed questions about a past firmware project, followed later by a separate session where the candidate works through a brand-new, open-ended sensor-design prompt live. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the earlier call (the technical screen) mainly checking?

(b) Fill in the blank: the later design-problem stage tests how the candidate actually ___ through an open-ended problem, not just whether they know isolated facts.

(a) The technical screen checks that background claims hold up, often surfacing quickly whether a candidate can explain their own past work in depth. (b) The design stage tests live reasoning, which is why it's usually the highest-weighted stage in the final decision.

7. A candidate, unsure what to expect, spends all their preparation time drilling whiteboard design problems, then walks into a round that turns out to be entirely behavioral questions about past teamwork. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this outcome illustrate about interview preparation?

(b) Fill in the blank: the ___ interview stage assesses collaboration, communication, and how a candidate actually handles failure, through questions about past experience.

(a) Preparing the same way for every stage wastes effort — identifying the stage ahead of time (or asking the recruiter) focuses preparation on what's actually being tested. (b) That stage is specifically the behavioral interview, which whiteboard drilling doesn't prepare a candidate for.

8. A candidate working a design prompt says: "I'll assume this needs to run on battery power most of the day, since you mentioned it's a portable device — let me know if that's wrong. Given that, a simpler always-on sensor loop is easier to build but drains the battery faster, while a polling approach saves power but adds some response delay — given what you said about battery life mattering most, I'd lean toward polling." Answer both parts:

(a) What does stating the battery-power assumption out loud accomplish?

(b) Fill in the blank: naming the ___ between the always-on loop and the polling approach — what each gains and costs — demonstrates both awareness of alternatives and a real decision criterion.

(a) A spoken assumption can be corrected immediately, avoiding a design built on an unconfirmed premise. (b) Naming what's gained and lost between approaches, tied to a stated constraint, packs several signals of good reasoning into one sentence.

9. An interviewer gives two candidates the identical open-ended prompt. One works quietly and reveals a finished design only at the very end; the other narrates each step and closes by naming what she'd want to verify next. Answer both parts:

(a) Why does the second candidate's approach tend to be evaluated more favorably?

(b) Fill in the blank: many whiteboard problems are deliberately open-ended, with no single correct answer, so closing with what you'd ___ next shows you understand the design is a starting point.

(a) The interviewer is evaluating reasoning on display, not just checking a final design against one fixed key — narrating gives them something to evaluate. (b) Naming a concrete next validation step is an honest, accurate close that leaves a strong final impression.

10. A candidate's answer to "tell me about a time you improved a process" spends four sentences on background and team context, then says "and we ended up fixing it," without ever describing what the candidate personally did. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the main weakness in this answer?

(b) Fill in the blank: the ___ section of a STAR answer — specifically what the candidate did, not what "the team" did — should typically get the most airtime.

(a) An interviewer evaluating individual judgment can't extract much when the candidate's specific role stays hidden behind "we." (b) Action is where the evidence of individual contribution lives, which is why it deserves the most airtime.
