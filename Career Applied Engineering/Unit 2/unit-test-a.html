Unit 2 Test — Version A

The Technical Interview. 10 points across every lesson in this unit — 5 questions worth 1 point, 5 worth 1 point split across two parts. Score 90% or higher to clear this unit.

← Course Contents Version B →

1. A candidate spends a 20-minute recruiter screen call rehearsing a detailed technical explanation of their senior project. What does this suggest about their preparation?

The recruiter screen exists to catch basic mismatches early, before an engineer's interview time is spent — deep technical depth belongs in a later stage.

2. Fill in the blank: because real whiteboard prompts are usually deliberately underspecified, the first step is to ___ the actual problem — scale, constraints, and the specific goal — before proposing anything.

Clarifying questions mirror the design process's "define" stage, and asking good ones is itself part of what's being evaluated.

3. In a design interview, a candidate immediately states a specific solution before asking any clarifying questions. The solution turns out to be a poor fit for the actual constraints. What is the main cost of this approach?

A whiteboard problem is testing reasoning process, not a lucky first guess — jumping to an answer skips the part that's actually being evaluated.

4. Asked "tell me about a time you handled conflict on a team," a candidate answers, "I always try to stay calm and professional." Why does this fall short as a behavioral interview answer?

Behavioral interviews want evidence of a real, specific situation and how it was actually handled — a generality provides no such evidence.

5. Fill in the blank: Situation, Task, Action, and Result together form the ___ method, the standard structure for answering behavioral interview questions with real substance.

STAR gives a repeatable four-part shape to a real story, ensuring it contains concrete, evaluable substance instead of a vague generality.

6. A candidate advances from a 45-minute call with an engineer discussing their past projects, to a separate 60-minute session working a new, open-ended design problem live. Answer both parts:

(a) What does the first stage (the technical screen) primarily check, distinct from the second?

(b) Fill in the blank: the deeper technical or design interview stage tests how a candidate actually ___ through an open-ended problem, not just whether they know isolated facts.

(a) The technical screen checks that background claims hold up in conversation, often surfacing quickly if a candidate can't explain their own work in depth. (b) The design stage is usually the highest-weighted stage precisely because it tests live reasoning process, not memorized facts.

7. A candidate spends every free hour before their interview process rehearsing STAR-format behavioral stories, regardless of which stage is coming up next. Answer both parts:

(a) Why might this preparation strategy be a mistake?

(b) Fill in the blank: the ___ interview stage specifically assesses collaboration, communication, and how a candidate handles failure through questions about past experience.

(a) Preparing the same way for every stage wastes effort — knowing what a given stage tests focuses preparation correctly. (b) The behavioral stage is the one that STAR-style preparation is actually built for.

8. Midway through a whiteboard problem, a candidate says: "I'll assume we need to support about 5x current traffic, based on what you mentioned about growth — let me know if that's off. Given that, I could use a simpler caching approach, which is easier to build but doesn't handle a certain edge case well, versus a more complex approach that does — given the timeline you mentioned, I'd lean toward the simpler one for now." Answer both parts:

(a) Why is stating the traffic assumption out loud better than silently designing around it?

(b) Fill in the blank: naming the ___ between the simpler and more complex caching approaches — what each gains and costs — in one clear statement demonstrates real design reasoning.

(a) A spoken assumption can be corrected right away, avoiding a design built on a premise nobody actually agreed to. (b) Naming what's gained and lost between two approaches, tied back to a stated constraint, is what packs several signals of good reasoning into one sentence.

9. Two candidates each produce a workable design for the same open-ended whiteboard prompt. Candidate A works silently and presents a finished design at the end. Candidate B talks through each step, states what she'd test next, and finishes with a comparable design. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is Candidate B's approach generally considered stronger, even though both produced a workable design?

(b) Fill in the blank: closing a design answer with "the next thing I'd want to ___ is..." signals that the design is a starting point, not a finished, certain answer.

(a) Many whiteboard problems are deliberately open-ended, so the interviewer is grading the reasoning on display, not just checking against one fixed correct output. (b) Naming what you'd validate next is an honest, accurate close that leaves a strong final impression.

10. A candidate answers a behavioral question by spending nearly the entire response describing the project's background and saying "we decided to fix the bug" without ever specifying their own role. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the main problem with this answer?

(b) Fill in the blank: in STAR, the ___ section — what the candidate specifically did — should usually get the most airtime, since it's what demonstrates individual judgment.

(a) An interviewer evaluating individual judgment can't extract much from a story where the candidate's own specific role stays unclear behind "we." (b) Action is where the real evidence of individual contribution lives, which is why it deserves the most airtime.
