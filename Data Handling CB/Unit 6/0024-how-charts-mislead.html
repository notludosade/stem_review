How Charts Mislead

A chart can use the exact right type from Lesson 23, contain no factually wrong numbers, and still leave its audience with the wrong impression. This lesson covers the most common ways that happens — including one rooted in decisions made two units ago.

← Lesson 23 Lesson 25 → Contents Glossary →

Every technique below produces a chart that is, in a narrow technical sense, accurate — no number on it is fabricated. The distortion happens in what the visual choice implies to a reader who isn't scrutinizing the axis labels.

Axis manipulation

Truncated y-axis Starting a bar or line chart's y-axis somewhere above zero exaggerates differences: two bars of height 98 and 100 look nearly equal on a 0-to-100 axis, but the same two values on an axis starting at 95 make the difference look enormous, even though the underlying values (a 2% difference) haven't changed at all. Bar charts in particular should almost always start at zero, since bar length itself is the visual cue readers use for magnitude.
Inconsistent scales and dual axes Two lines plotted on different y-axes (one scaled 0-10, the other 0-1000) can be made to visually "cross" or "diverge dramatically" purely by choosing where each axis starts and ends, independent of whether the underlying quantities have any meaningful relationship at that moment. A reader who doesn't check both axis labels sees an apparent relationship that the axis choice manufactured.
Why Unit 5's cleaning decisions can mislead just as much as a bad axis, without anyone intending it to A chart is only as honest as the data behind it, and Unit 5's cleaning choices are exactly where that honesty is decided before a single pixel is drawn. Silently dropping a Lesson 20 outlier because it "looked bad" on a chart, rather than because it was judged to be a genuine error, changes the story the chart tells — and unlike a truncated axis, a reader has no way to see this decision at all, since the removed point simply isn't there. The same applies to Lesson 19's missing-data choices: filling gaps with an imputed mean, then charting the result without noting which points were real and which were filled, can make an otherwise incomplete dataset look more solid than it is. A visualization inherits every judgment call made two units earlier — which is exactly why Lesson 22 argued for keeping a cleaning log: the log is what lets a chart's audience, or its author months later, check whether the version shown is a fair one.
Worked example A monthly-sales line chart shows a dramatic uptick starting in March. The y-axis turns out to start at 40,000 rather than 0, exaggerating a modest real increase — and separately, February's data point had been excluded from the chart because it contained an outlier that was never actually verified as an error (Lesson 20's judgment call, skipped). Fixing the axis to start at zero and restoring the February point (once genuinely confirmed as valid, not an error) together turn a dramatic-looking spike into a much more modest, and more honest, trend line.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why does truncating a bar chart's y-axis (starting above zero) distort how readers perceive the data?

Truncation distorts the visual proportion readers rely on, even though the underlying numbers are unchanged.

2. How can a Unit 5 data-cleaning decision, such as dropping an unverified outlier, mislead a chart's audience in a way an axis trick does not?

An axis choice is at least visible and checkable; a silently dropped data point leaves no visual trace at all.

3. Why did Lesson 22 argue for keeping a cleaning log, in terms directly relevant to this lesson's chart-honesty discussion?

The cleaning log is the audit trail that connects a chart back to the judgment calls that shaped its underlying data.

4. Fill in the blank: starting a bar chart's y-axis above zero to exaggerate a visual difference is called a ___.

A truncated (non-zero-start) axis is the standard term for this distortion.

5. Fill in the blank: plotting two lines on independently scaled y-axes, which can manufacture an apparent crossing or divergence unrelated to the real relationship between the variables, uses ___.

Dual axes let each line's scale be chosen independently, which can create a misleading visual relationship.
