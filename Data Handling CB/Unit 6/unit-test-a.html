1. A retailer wants to show how its total monthly revenue changed over the past two years. Which chart type fits this question best?

A line chart's continuous path is well suited to showing a trend over a time axis.

2. Fill in the blank: an analyst wants to show the relationship between hours of sunlight and daily ice cream sales across many days. The chart type best suited to this is a ___.

A scatter plot directly shows how two numeric variables relate, point by point.

3. Fill in the blank: human vision is reliably bad at comparing pie-slice ___ and areas precisely — two slices of 23% and 19% look nearly identical, while the same two values as bar heights are trivially easy to compare.

This perceptual weakness is exactly why a sorted bar chart is usually the more accurate comparison tool once there are more than a handful of categories.

4. A bar chart comparing two products' sales (98 units and 100 units) uses a y-axis starting at 95 instead of 0. What effect does this have?

Bar length is the visual cue readers use for magnitude, so starting the axis above zero exaggerates a difference that, measured from zero, may be small.

5. Fill in the blank: the design principle of maximizing the proportion of a chart's visual content devoted to actual data, rather than gridlines and decoration, is called maximizing the ___.

A high data-ink ratio means most of a chart's visual content is the actual data, not decoration or clutter.

6. A chart plots weekly sales (scaled 0-1000) and weekly customer satisfaction score (scaled 0-10) on two separate y-axes, and the two lines appear to "cross" dramatically one month. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this dual-axis chart risk implying to a reader who doesn't check both axis labels?

(b) What would a more honest presentation of this same data require?

Two lines on independently scaled axes can be made to visually converge or diverge purely by choosing where each axis starts and ends — a reader who trusts the visual without checking both axes sees a manufactured relationship.

7. A monthly-sales chart shows a dramatic uptick starting in March. It turns out February's data point was excluded because it looked like an outlier — but it was never actually verified as a genuine error. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is this kind of silent exclusion more dangerous to a chart's honesty than a truncated axis?

(b) What should have happened with the February data point instead?

A visualization inherits every judgment call made during cleaning — the cleaning log is what lets a chart's audience check whether the version shown is a fair one.

8. The same quarterly revenue-by-region dataset needs to be presented both at a board meeting and to the regional analysts who did the work. Answer both parts:

(a) What design fits the board-meeting audience best?

(b) What design fits the analysts reviewing the underlying work best?

Detail level should match the audience's actual need — a quick-decision audience needs a clear headline; an analytical audience needs enough detail to verify the reasoning.

9. A data story follows the structure Context → Finding → Implication. Answer both parts:

(a) What does the "Context" step establish?

(b) What is the most common way a data story goes wrong?

Each step should follow directly from the one before it — the gap between a finding and an overreaching implication is exactly where a data story's biggest risk lives.

10. A scatter plot shows a positive correlation (R-squared 0.55) between weekly social media posts and weekly sales. Answer both parts:

(a) Which implication overreaches what this chart actually supports?

(b) Which implication is honest given only this correlational finding?

Prose narration pulls toward confident, causal-sounding language even when the chart only demonstrates an association — the honest version names the possible confounder and proposes a test rather than asserting causation.
