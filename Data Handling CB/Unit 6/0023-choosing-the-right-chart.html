Choosing the Right Chart

Unit 5 made data trustworthy. This unit makes it legible — starting with the single decision that shapes everything else about a visualization: which chart type actually fits the question being asked.

← Lesson 22 Lesson 24 → Contents Glossary →

A chart is chosen for the relationship it's meant to show, not for how it looks. Getting this wrong is the most common — and most avoidable — data visualization mistake, and it happens before a single color or label is chosen.

Match the chart to the question

Chart types by task Comparison (how do categories differ?) → bar chart. Trend over time (how does a value change?) → line chart. Part-to-whole (what share does each category hold?) → stacked bar or (for very few categories) pie chart. Relationship between two numeric variables (Lesson 16's correlation question) → scatter plot. Distribution (Lesson 15's shape of the data) → histogram. Each of these answers a different question, and a chart chosen for the wrong question actively works against the reader even when every number in it is accurate.
Why a pie chart is the most commonly misused chart type A pie chart works only when there are few categories (roughly five or fewer) and the parts genuinely sum to a meaningful whole. Beyond that, human vision is reliably bad at comparing angles and areas precisely — two pie slices of 23% and 19% look nearly identical, while the same two values as bar heights are trivially easy to compare. A bar chart, ordered by value, communicates a part-to-whole comparison more accurately than a pie chart in almost every case except the very simplest — which is why experienced analysts default to bars and reach for pie only rarely.
Worked example A regional sales dataset with 8 regions, each region's share of total revenue: an 8-slice pie chart forces readers to compare subtly different wedge angles; the same data as a horizontal bar chart, sorted from largest region to smallest, lets a reader immediately see the ranking and the actual magnitude of the gaps between regions — the same numbers, but only one chart type actually serves the "which region matters most, and by how much" question.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Which chart type is generally the best fit for showing the relationship between two numeric variables, the same question Lesson 16 addressed with a correlation coefficient?

A scatter plot directly shows how two numeric variables relate, point by point.

2. Why do experienced analysts generally prefer a sorted bar chart over a pie chart for comparing many categories' share of a whole?

The core issue is perceptual: angle/area comparisons are less precise for the human eye than length comparisons.

3. What is the defining principle behind choosing a chart type, according to this lesson?

Matching chart type to the underlying question is the organizing principle of this lesson.

4. Fill in the blank: the chart type best suited to showing a distribution's shape (Lesson 15), such as whether data is right-skewed or roughly normal, is a ___.

A histogram displays frequency across value ranges, directly revealing shape and skew.

5. Fill in the blank: the chart type generally used to show how a value changes over time is a ___.

A line chart's continuous path is well suited to showing trend over a time axis.
