Telling a Data Story

This closes Unit 6: assembling chart choice (Lesson 23), honesty (Lesson 24), and audience design (Lesson 25) into a complete narrative — and confronting the exact trap Lesson 16 warned about, now that you're the one telling the story.

← Lesson 25 Lesson 27 → Contents Glossary →

A data story is a sequence of charts and framing that leads an audience from a question to a conclusion. It's more than any single chart — it's the connective narrative between them, and narrative is exactly where the correlation-vs-causation trap resurfaces.

Structure of a data story

A reliable shape Context — what question or business situation motivates this analysis? Finding — what does the data actually show, presented with the right chart type (Lesson 23) and honestly (Lesson 24)? Implication — what should the audience do or conclude as a result? Each step should follow directly from the one before it — a story that jumps from finding straight to a strong implication, skipping over what the finding can and can't support, is where the most damage happens.
Why "telling a story" is exactly where correlation gets misread as causation Lesson 16 warned that correlation is cheap to observe and causation is hard to establish, and that the gap between them is easy to paper over with confident language. A data story is prose wrapped around a finding — and prose has a strong pull toward causal-sounding verbs ("X drives Y," "Y is because of X") even when the underlying chart only shows two lines moving together. The ice cream/drowning example from Lesson 16 works precisely as an example of bad *causal* narration around a *correlational* chart — a scatter plot of ice cream sales against drownings is a completely honest chart; the failure happens the moment someone writes "ice cream sales cause drownings" underneath it. The same risk applies to a regression line from Lesson 18: a well-fit line is real information about association, and the moment a data story's narration claims it as proof of a driver, it has made a claim the chart itself never supported. The discipline is the same one from Lesson 16: before writing an implication, ask whether a confounding variable could explain the same finding, and word the implication no more strongly than the evidence (correlational vs. experimental) actually allows.
Worked example: honest vs. dishonest narration of the same chart Context: "Marketing asked whether social media activity relates to sales." Finding: a scatter plot shows a positive correlation between weekly social posts and weekly sales, R-squared 0.62 (Lesson 18). Dishonest implication: "Posting more on social media increases sales — we should triple our posting frequency." Honest implication: "Social media activity and sales move together; a likely confounder is that both rise during planned promotional periods. Before committing budget to more posts, a controlled test — increasing posting frequency for some products but not others — would tell us whether the relationship is causal." The chart didn't change between the two versions. Only the story did.
