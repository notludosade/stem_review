1. A retailer wants to compare this quarter's total revenue across six distinct product categories. Which chart type fits this question best?

A bar chart is the standard fit for comparing how discrete categories differ from one another.

2. Fill in the blank: an analyst wants to show the relationship between hours studied and exam score across many students. The chart type best suited to this is a ___.

A scatter plot directly shows how two numeric variables relate, point by point.

3. Fill in the blank: an 8-slice pie chart forces readers to compare subtly different wedge ___, which human vision does far less accurately than comparing bar heights for the same data.

This perceptual weakness is exactly why a sorted bar chart is usually the more accurate comparison tool once there are more than a handful of categories.

4. A bar chart comparing two quarters' revenue ($420,000 and $430,000) uses a y-axis starting at $400,000 instead of $0. What effect does this have?

Bar length is the visual cue readers use for magnitude, so starting the axis above zero exaggerates a difference that, measured from zero, may be small.

5. Fill in the blank: removing 3D effects, heavy background shading, and excessive gridlines from a chart, since they add visual noise without adding information, is a way of maximizing the ___.

A high data-ink ratio means most of a chart's visual content is the actual data, not decoration or clutter.

6. A chart plots daily temperature (scaled 0-100°F) and daily ice cream sales (scaled 0-5,000 units) on two separate y-axes, and the two lines appear to sharply diverge one week. Answer both parts:

(a) What does this dual-axis chart risk implying to a reader who doesn't check both axis labels?

(b) What would a more honest presentation of this same data require?

Two lines on independently scaled axes can be made to visually converge or diverge purely by choosing where each axis starts and ends — a reader who trusts the visual without checking both axes sees a manufactured relationship.

7. A chart of weekly attendance figures fills several missing weeks with the column's average value, then displays the result with no indication of which points are real and which were filled in. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is this kind of undisclosed imputation dangerous to a chart's honesty?

(b) What should have happened with the imputed weeks instead?

A visualization inherits every judgment call made during cleaning — the cleaning log is what lets a chart's audience check whether the version shown is a fair one.

8. The same customer-satisfaction dataset needs to be presented both to company executives and to the analytics team that produced it. Answer both parts:

(a) What design fits the executive audience best?

(b) What design fits the analytics team reviewing the underlying work best?

Detail level should match the audience's actual need — a quick-decision audience needs a clear headline; an analytical audience needs enough detail to verify the reasoning.

9. A data story follows the structure Context → Finding → Implication. Answer both parts:

(a) What is the most common way a data story goes wrong?

(b) What does the "Finding" step present?

Each step should follow directly from the one before it — the gap between a finding and an overreaching implication is exactly where a data story's biggest risk lives.

10. A scatter plot shows a positive correlation (R-squared 0.70) between weekly gym-membership signups and local news mentions of "New Year's resolutions." Answer both parts:

(a) Which implication overreaches what this chart actually supports?

(b) Which implication is honest given only this correlational finding?

Prose narration pulls toward confident, causal-sounding language even when the chart only demonstrates an association — the honest version names the possible confounder and proposes a test rather than asserting causation.
