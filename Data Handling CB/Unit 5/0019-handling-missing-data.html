Handling Missing Data

Lesson 3 named completeness as a data quality dimension. This lesson is the practical follow-through: how to recognize missing data, why it's missing matters, and what your real options are once you find it.

← Lesson 18 Lesson 20 → Contents Glossary →

Recall Lesson 3's $0 revenue example: a zero could mean genuinely no revenue, an unrecorded value, or an error. Before deciding what to do about missing data, you first have to reliably recognize it — which sounds simpler than it is.

Recognizing missing data

Forms missing data takes True blanks (an empty cell or NULL) are the easy case. Harder: sentinel values standing in for "missing" — a "9999" age, a "N/A" typed as text into a numeric column, a "01/01/1900" placeholder date, or (Lesson 3's example) a "0" used where the real answer is "not recorded." Sentinel values are dangerous specifically because they look like real data to any tool that isn't told otherwise — an average that includes a "9999" placeholder age is badly wrong, with no error raised.

Why missing data is missing: MCAR, MAR, MNAR

Definitions Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) — the chance of being missing has nothing to do with any value, observed or not (a sensor randomly dropped a reading). Missing At Random (MAR) — missingness relates to something else you did observe, but not to the missing value itself (older respondents in a survey are more likely to skip an optional digital-literacy question, but conditional on age, skipping isn't related to their actual answer). Missing Not At Random (MNAR) — the missingness is related to the value itself (people with very high incomes are more likely to decline to state their income) — the most dangerous case, since the missing values differ systematically from the ones you can see.
Why the reason for missingness changes what's safe to do about it If data is MCAR, simply dropping the incomplete rows introduces no systematic distortion — you lose sample size, nothing else. If data is MNAR, dropping incomplete rows (or filling them with an average) actively biases the remaining data, because the missing values weren't a random subset — they were disproportionately the high (or low) end of the real distribution. This is structurally the same warning as Lesson 2's sampling bias: a subset that isn't random doesn't represent the whole, whether that subset is who happened to answer a survey or which rows happened to have complete data.

What to actually do about it

Options Delete the row (safe under MCAR, riskier otherwise, and only reasonable when missing rows are a small share of the data). Delete the column (when a field is missing too often to be usable at all). Impute — fill in a reasoned estimate, e.g. the column's mean or median (Lesson 14) for numeric data, or the most common category (mode) for categorical data — cheap, but risks understating the data's true variability. Flag it — keep the value as missing but add a separate column marking that it was missing, preserving the information that something was unknown rather than hiding it inside a filled-in guess.
Worked example A customer database has 3% of rows missing a "region" value. If those blanks arose because a form field was optional and skipped essentially at random (MCAR), dropping those rows for a regional analysis is defensible. If instead the blanks are concentrated among customers who signed up through a specific channel that never asked for region (a pattern related to something observable — MAR), dropping them would silently underrepresent that channel's customers in every regional breakdown that follows.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. Why are sentinel values (like "9999" for age) more dangerous than a true blank cell?

A blank is at least recognizable as missing; a sentinel value hides in plain sight as a plausible-looking number or entry.

2. Why is Missing Not At Random (MNAR) data the most dangerous case to simply drop or fill with an average?

MNAR's defining danger is that the missing values aren't a random subset — they differ systematically from what's observed, echoing the sampling-bias concern from Lesson 2.

3. What does "flagging" missing data (adding a separate column marking a value as missing) preserve that simple imputation does not?

Flagging keeps the "this was missing" fact visible and analyzable, rather than burying it inside an imputed value that looks just like real data.

4. Fill in the blank: filling in a missing value with a reasoned estimate, such as a column's mean or mode, is called ___.

Imputation is the general term for filling in missing values with an estimated substitute.

5. Fill in the blank: the category of missingness where the chance of a value being missing is related to the value itself (e.g. high earners declining to state income) is abbreviated ___.

MNAR (Missing Not At Random) is the case where missingness itself correlates with the unseen value.
