Deduplication & Standardization

Lesson 3 flagged consistency as a data quality dimension; Lesson 8 showed dropdowns preventing new inconsistency going forward. This lesson is about cleaning up the inconsistency and duplication that already exists in data you didn't collect that carefully.

← Lesson 20 Lesson 22 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 3's inconsistency example was "USA" vs. "United States" vs. "U.S." all meaning the same country. Lesson 8 showed how a dropdown list stops that from happening in newly entered data. This lesson is about the data that already has the problem — often merged from multiple sources that each had their own conventions.

Duplicate records

Exact vs. fuzzy duplicates An exact duplicate is a row that is a byte-for-byte match of another — the easy case, found by checking whether every field matches (or checking a unique ID column, when one exists). A fuzzy duplicate is a row referring to the same real-world entity but recorded slightly differently — "Jon Smith, 555-0142" and "Jonathan Smith, (555) 012-0142" might be the same customer entered twice, with a typo and a formatting difference. Fuzzy duplicates can't be found with a simple equality check; they require deciding on a matching rule (e.g. same phone number after removing formatting, or same name plus same address) and accepting that some matches will be judgment calls.
Why duplicates quietly break more than they look like they should A duplicated customer row doesn't just take up extra space — it double-counts that customer in every count, sum, and average computed afterward (Lesson 14's mean, Lesson 12's SQL aggregation), and if the duplicate has slightly different values in some fields (an updated address in one copy but not the other), a JOIN (Lesson 11) against that table can silently produce two rows of output for what should be one match. The two problems compound: duplicate detection has to happen before aggregation and joining, not after, or the downstream numbers are wrong in a way that no formula will flag as an error.

Standardization

Common standardization tasks Case (forcing "Smith" and "smith" to match), whitespace (trimming leading/trailing spaces that make "Ohio" and "Ohio " look different to an exact match), formatting (unifying phone numbers, dates, and currency into one consistent format), and category values (mapping "USA"/"United States"/"U.S." to one canonical value — precisely the Lesson 3 example, resolved after the fact rather than prevented at entry like Lesson 8's dropdown).
Worked example A merged customer list from two systems has "NY", "N.Y.", and "New York" all appearing in a state column, plus two rows for "Maria Garcia" at the same address with phone numbers "555-201-4488" and "5552014488" (identical once formatting is stripped). Standardization maps all three state spellings to one canonical code ("NY"); deduplication treats the two Maria Garcia rows as a fuzzy duplicate once phone-number formatting is stripped and compared, and merges them into one record, keeping whichever field values are most complete or most recent.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What distinguishes a fuzzy duplicate from an exact duplicate?

Fuzzy duplicates require a matching rule and judgment, unlike exact duplicates, which a plain equality check finds.

2. Why should duplicate detection happen before aggregation (Lesson 12) or joining (Lesson 11), not after?

Duplicates distort every calculation performed afterward, silently and without any error being raised.

3. How does resolving "USA" / "United States" / "U.S." into one canonical value after the fact (standardization) differ from Lesson 8's dropdown-list approach?

Both address the same inconsistency problem from Lesson 3, at two different points — before entry (Lesson 8) and after the fact (this lesson).

4. Fill in the blank: unifying case, whitespace, formatting, and category values into one consistent form across a dataset is called ___.

Standardization is the umbrella term for these consistency-fixing tasks.

5. Fill in the blank: two records referring to the same real-world entity but recorded with typos or formatting differences (e.g. "Jon Smith" vs. "Jonathan Smith" at the same phone number) are an example of a ___.

Fuzzy duplicates require a matching rule to detect, unlike exact byte-for-byte duplicates.
