Where Data Comes From

Every dataset was collected somehow — and the collection method can bake in a distortion no amount of clever analysis later can undo. Before analyzing data, ask where it came from.

← Lesson 1 Lesson 3 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 1 gave you a vocabulary for what a dataset looks like. This lesson asks a question that has to come before any analysis: how was this data actually collected, and what does that collection method already assume or exclude?

Primary vs. secondary data

Definitions Primary data is collected directly for the question at hand — a survey you ran, an experiment you designed, sensor readings from your own equipment. Secondary data was collected by someone else, for some other purpose, and you're reusing it — a government census dataset, a public API, a previous department's spreadsheet. Secondary data is usually cheaper and faster to get, but it was shaped by someone else's questions, definitions, and collection choices, which may not match yours.

Common collection methods

Sources Surveys and questionnaires (self-reported, subject to memory and honesty limits) · Sensors and logs (automatically recorded, e.g. website clickstreams or IoT devices — accurate about what they measure, but only measure what they were built to measure) · Experiments (data generated under deliberately controlled conditions, e.g. an A/B test) · Public and administrative datasets (census data, government records, open data portals) · Transactional records (orders, payments, logins — a byproduct of normal business operation, not designed as a research dataset).

Sampling

Definitions Almost no dataset contains an entire population — it contains a sample, a subset meant to represent the whole. Random sampling gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection. Stratified sampling divides the population into subgroups (strata) first, then samples within each, to guarantee smaller subgroups aren't accidentally missed. Convenience sampling just takes whoever's easiest to reach (people walking by, an email list you already have) — the cheapest method, and the least reliable.
Why convenience sampling quietly breaks conclusions A convenience sample only represents the population if being "convenient to reach" is unrelated to the thing you're measuring — and it usually isn't. A survey emailed only to existing customers can't tell you why non-customers aren't buying. A poll conducted only during business hours misses anyone working a different schedule. The distortion this creates is called sampling bias: a systematic difference between the sample and the population it's meant to represent, caused by the sampling method itself rather than by chance. Sampling bias doesn't show up in the data as an obvious error — the numbers you collected are completely real — it shows up as a mismatch between what you concluded and what's actually true of the whole population, and it cannot be fixed by collecting more data using the same biased method. This distinction — bias from the collection method itself, versus ordinary sampling variability — is exactly what Unit 4's confidence intervals lesson will come back to when it asks what a margin of error can and can't account for.
Spotting the collection method behind a claim A headline reads: "73% of developers prefer Language X." Before trusting it: who was surveyed (a specific forum's readers? a random sample of all developers?), how were they reached (an email blast to a mailing list is convenience sampling), and does the survey's population actually match the population the headline implies ("developers" in general, vs. "developers who read this particular site").

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the key difference between primary and secondary data?

The distinction is about who collected the data and why, not its format or cost.

2. What distinguishes stratified sampling from simple random sampling?

Stratification's whole purpose is guaranteeing subgroup representation that a plain random draw might miss by chance.

3. Why can't sampling bias be fixed just by collecting more data using the same method?

Random variability shrinks with more data; a systematic bias in who or what gets sampled does not.

4. Fill in the blank: sampling that simply takes whoever is easiest to reach, rather than deliberately representing the population, is called ___ sampling.

Convenience sampling is the cheapest but least reliable method, since "easy to reach" is rarely unrelated to what's being measured.

5. Fill in the blank: a systematic difference between a sample and the population it's meant to represent, caused by the collection method itself, is called ___.

Sampling bias is a property of the collection method, distinct from ordinary chance variability, and it doesn't shrink with more data collected the same way.
