Data Governance & Regulation, Briefly

This closes Unit 7: PII (Lesson 27) and bias (Lesson 28) aren't only ethical concerns — in many places, they're legal ones. This is a brief map of that landscape, not a substitute for legal advice.

← Lesson 28 Lesson 30 → Contents Glossary →

This lesson is intentionally brief and general — data regulation is a genuinely specialized legal field, varies by jurisdiction and industry, and changes over time. The goal here is orientation: knowing these frameworks exist and roughly what they require, so you know when to ask someone whose job it is to know the specifics.

Major regulatory frameworks

A brief map, not a legal reference GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation, European Union) — governs personal data of EU residents; grants individuals rights including access to their data, correction, and deletion ("right to be forgotten"); requires a lawful basis for processing personal data. CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act) — grants California residents rights to know what personal data is collected about them, and to opt out of its sale. HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, United States) — governs health information specifically, including the de-identification standard referenced in Lesson 27. These are three examples among many others worldwide (Brazil's LGPD, Canada's PIPEDA) — the specific rules that apply depend on whose data it is, where they live, and what industry the data pertains to.
Data governance, as a practice Data governance is the set of internal policies an organization uses to manage data responsibly — who can access which data, how long it's retained, how it's classified by sensitivity, and how compliance with external regulation is actually implemented day to day. Regulation sets the external legal floor; governance is how an organization actually operationalizes meeting (or exceeding) that floor internally.
Why "briefly" is the right scope for this lesson, and not a shortcut Data regulation genuinely requires specialized legal expertise to apply correctly to a specific real situation — jurisdiction, industry, and the exact nature of the data all change the answer, and getting it wrong can carry serious consequences (fines, legal liability, harm to real people). The purpose of this lesson isn't to make you capable of making a compliance judgment call yourself; it's to make sure you recognize the situations where one is needed at all — handling health data, EU residents' data, or anything that looks like PII from Lesson 27 — so you know to loop in someone qualified rather than guessing.
Worked example A small e-commerce company expanding to sell in the EU and California now has customers whose data falls under both GDPR and CCPA. In practice, this means the company needs a data governance policy covering: what customer data is collected, a documented lawful basis for processing it (GDPR), a process for handling a customer's request to delete their data (both frameworks, worded slightly differently), and a record of who internally has access to that data. None of this is something one analyst decides alone — it's a governance function, usually involving legal and compliance staff.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the relationship between "regulation" and "data governance" as described in this lesson?

Regulation is the external legal requirement; governance is how an organization operationalizes meeting it.

2. What right does GDPR's "right to be forgotten" grant to individuals?

The right to be forgotten specifically covers deletion of an individual's already-collected personal data.

3. What is this lesson's actual goal in introducing GDPR, CCPA, and HIPAA only briefly, rather than in legal depth?

This lesson is explicitly scoped as orientation, not a substitute for actual legal expertise.

4. Fill in the blank: the European Union regulation granting individuals rights including data access, correction, and deletion is abbreviated ___.

GDPR is the EU's General Data Protection Regulation.

5. Fill in the blank: the set of internal policies an organization uses to manage who can access data, how long it's retained, and how compliance is implemented day to day is called ___.

Data governance is the internal practice that operationalizes meeting external regulation.
