PII & Anonymization

Every unit so far has treated data as neutral rows and columns. Behind many of those rows are real people, and this unit is about the responsibilities that come with that — starting with recognizing what actually identifies someone.

← Lesson 26 Lesson 28 → Contents Glossary →

Personally Identifiable Information (PII) is any data that can identify a specific individual, either on its own or combined with other data. Handling it responsibly starts with recognizing that "combined with other data" clause — it's where most real-world anonymization mistakes happen.

Direct vs. indirect identifiers

Two categories A direct identifier points to one specific person on its own: name, Social Security number, email address, phone number. An indirect identifier (sometimes called a quasi-identifier) doesn't identify anyone alone, but can narrow a large population down to one person when combined with a few others: ZIP code, birth date, and gender together, for instance, have been shown to uniquely identify the majority of the U.S. population, even though none of the three alone identifies anyone.

Anonymization techniques

Common approaches Removal — deleting direct identifiers entirely. Masking — replacing part of a value (showing only the last four digits of an account number). Aggregation — reporting only group-level statistics (Lesson 12's GROUP BY) rather than individual rows, so no single row is exposed at all. Generalization — reducing precision, e.g. replacing an exact birth date with a birth year, or an exact address with a ZIP code, specifically to weaken the indirect-identifier combinations above.
Why removing the obvious direct identifiers is often not enough A dataset with names removed but ZIP code, birth date, and gender left intact can still be re-identified, sometimes with high accuracy, by cross-referencing against other public records (like voter registration lists) — a well-documented finding from real re-identification research. This is the anonymization equivalent of a JOIN (Lesson 11): if the "anonymized" data still shares indirect identifiers with some other dataset, joining the two can reconstruct exactly the identity that removal was meant to protect. True anonymization has to consider what other data sources exist in the world, not just what was deleted from this one file.
Worked example A hospital releases a "de-identified" research dataset with patient names removed, but retains exact birth date, ZIP code, and gender for each patient. A researcher cross-references this against a publicly available voter registration database (which does include names alongside those same three fields) and re-identifies a majority of the patients. The fix: generalize birth date to birth year and ZIP code to the first three digits only, which sharply reduces how many people any one combination of values could match.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What distinguishes an indirect identifier (quasi-identifier) from a direct identifier?

ZIP code, birth date, and gender are each individually non-identifying, but combined can uniquely identify a person.

2. Why can a dataset with names removed still fail to protect patient identities, as in the hospital worked example?

Re-identification through cross-referencing indirect identifiers is exactly why removal of direct identifiers alone is often insufficient.

3. How does generalization (e.g. replacing an exact birth date with a birth year) help prevent re-identification?

Generalization keeps the field usable for analysis while making it match far more people, reducing identification risk.

4. Fill in the blank: any data that can identify a specific individual, alone or combined with other data, is called ___.

PII is the umbrella term this whole lesson is organized around.

5. Fill in the blank: the anonymization technique of reducing precision, such as replacing an exact address with only a ZIP code, is called ___.

Generalization specifically targets the precision of a field, distinct from removal or masking.
