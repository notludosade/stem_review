Correlation vs. Causation

Two variables moving together is one of the easiest true facts about data to misread as one causing the other. This lesson gives you the concept and the discipline to tell the two apart.

← Lesson 15 Lesson 17 → Contents Glossary →

"Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer" is a classic example: the two are correlated, but ice cream doesn't cause drowning. This lesson formalizes why that reasoning fails, and how to catch it in your own analysis.

Correlation

Definition Correlation measures how strongly two quantitative variables move together, typically summarized by a correlation coefficient ranging from -1 to +1. A value near +1 means the variables tend to rise and fall together; near -1 means one tends to rise as the other falls; near 0 means little to no linear relationship. Correlation says nothing about which variable, if either, is driving the other.

Causation

Definition Causation means a change in one variable actually produces a change in the other. Establishing causation typically requires a controlled experiment (deliberately changing one variable while holding others fixed, and observing the effect) — something a correlation observed in existing, uncontrolled data cannot substitute for on its own.

The confounding variable

Definition A confounding variable is a third factor that influences both variables being studied, creating a correlation between them without either causing the other. In the ice cream/drowning example, the confounder is warm weather: it drives both ice cream sales and swimming (and therefore drowning risk) upward independently.
Why this mistake is so easy to make and so consequential Correlation is cheap to compute from data you already have; establishing causation usually requires deliberately designed experimentation, which is expensive, slow, or sometimes impossible (you can't ethically randomly assign some people to smoke and others not to, so smoking-and-cancer causation had to be argued from converging non-experimental evidence over decades, not a single clean experiment). Because correlation is so much easier to obtain, there's a constant pull toward treating it as if it answers the causal question — a pull that produces confidently wrong headlines and, in a business context, decisions based on a driver that was never actually a driver. Unit 6's storytelling lesson will come back to this exact confusion, since it's one of the most common ways a data visualization ends up misleading its audience even when every number in it is accurate.
Spotting a likely confounder "Cities with more firefighters have more fire damage" — sounds like firefighters cause damage, but the confounder is fire size/severity: bigger fires get more firefighters dispatched and cause more damage. The number of firefighters didn't cause the damage; the severity of the fire drove both numbers upward together.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What does a correlation coefficient near 0 indicate about two variables?

A coefficient near 0 signals weak or no linear association, independent of any causal question.

2. In the ice cream sales / drowning deaths example, what role does warm weather play?

Warm weather is the classic confounder here — it's what actually drives both quantities, not either one driving the other.

3. Why is correlation from existing, uncontrolled data generally insufficient on its own to establish causation?

Ruling out confounders is exactly what a controlled experiment is designed to do, which a bare correlation from existing data cannot.

4. Fill in the blank: a third factor that influences both variables being studied, creating a correlation between them without either causing the other, is called a ___.

The confounding variable is the hidden driver behind an apparent but non-causal relationship between two measured variables.

5. Fill in the blank: the relationship in which a change in one variable actually produces a change in another, typically established through a controlled experiment, is ___.

Causation requires demonstrating that one variable actually drives changes in the other, not just that the two move together.
