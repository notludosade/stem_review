Descriptive Statistics

A thousand numbers can't be held in your head at once. Descriptive statistics compress a dataset down to a handful of numbers that summarize its center and spread — the starting point for every later lesson in this unit.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Units 2 and 3 taught you to compute AVERAGE and AVG. This lesson explains what those numbers mean, why they can mislead on their own, and what else you need alongside them.

Measures of center

Definitions The mean is the arithmetic average — sum divided by count. The median is the middle value when all values are sorted (or the average of the two middle values, for an even count). The mode is the most frequently occurring value.
Why the mean can be badly misleading The mean is sensitive to extreme values (outliers); the median isn't. Nine employees earning $50,000 and one CEO earning $2,000,000 gives a mean salary of about $245,000 — a number that describes literally none of the ten employees well. The median in that same set is $50,000, a far more representative "typical" value. Recall this is a ratio-level variable (Lesson 1) being averaged correctly in the arithmetic sense — the mean isn't computed wrong, it's just not always the right summary to reach for. As a rule of thumb: heavily skewed data (like income, home prices, or response times with a long tail) is usually better summarized by the median; roughly symmetric data is usually fine to summarize with the mean.

Measures of spread

Definitions The range is simply max minus min. The standard deviation measures the typical distance of values from the mean — a small standard deviation means values cluster tightly around the mean; a large one means they're spread widely. The interquartile range (IQR) is the range of the middle 50% of the data (the 75th percentile value minus the 25th percentile value), and — like the median — is resistant to outliers, unlike the range or standard deviation.
Worked example: two datasets, same mean, different story Dataset A: 48, 49, 50, 51, 52 (mean 50, small spread). Dataset B: 10, 30, 50, 70, 90 (mean 50, large spread). Reporting "the mean is 50" for both completely hides how differently these two datasets actually behave — which is exactly why a measure of center is never reported alone in careful analysis; it needs a measure of spread alongside it.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In a company with nine employees earning $50,000 and one CEO earning $2,000,000, why is the median a more representative "typical" salary than the mean?

The mean is correctly computed but sensitive to outliers; the median resists exactly this kind of distortion.

2. Why is reporting only a mean or median, without any measure of spread, potentially misleading?

Center and spread answer different questions — a shared mean can hide wildly different underlying distributions.

3. Why is the interquartile range (IQR) often preferred over the plain range as a measure of spread?

The range is fully determined by the two most extreme values in the dataset; the IQR deliberately ignores them.

4. Fill in the blank: the measure of center found by sorting all values and taking the middle one (or the average of the two middle values), which resists distortion by outliers, is the ___.

The median's position-based definition, rather than an arithmetic sum, is what makes it resistant to a single extreme value.

5. Fill in the blank: the measure of spread that describes the typical distance of values from the mean is the ___.

Standard deviation directly measures how far, typically, values sit from the mean.
