Distributions

The same mean and standard deviation can come from very differently shaped data. This lesson gives you a vocabulary for the shape itself — the distribution — and introduces the one shape most of applied statistics leans on.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

A distribution describes how often each value (or range of values) occurs across a dataset — visualized as a histogram, it's the shape you'd see if you plotted every value's frequency.

The normal distribution

Definition The normal distribution ("bell curve") is symmetric, with values clustering densely around the mean and thinning out gradually in both directions. Many real-world quantities — heights, measurement errors, test scores across a large population — approximate this shape closely enough that it's the default assumption behind a large share of standard statistical methods, including the confidence intervals in Lesson 17.

Skew

Definition A distribution is skewed when it isn't symmetric — one side has a longer "tail" than the other. Right-skewed (positive skew) data has a long tail toward high values, e.g. income or home prices, where most values cluster at the lower end but a few very high values stretch the tail rightward. Left-skewed (negative skew) is the mirror image, e.g. age at death in a population with modern healthcare, where most values cluster near an upper limit with a longer tail toward lower ages.
Why skew is exactly why Lesson 14 recommended the median for income data Right-skewed data pulls the mean toward the tail — a few very high incomes drag the mean upward, away from where most of the data actually sits — while the median, defined by position rather than magnitude, stays anchored near where most values genuinely are. Recognizing skew visually (or checking whether mean and median differ substantially) is how you decide, case by case, whether Lesson 14's "mean vs. median" guidance actually applies to the dataset in front of you.
Reading a histogram for shape A histogram of exam scores clustered symmetrically around 75 with a shrinking tail on both sides is roughly normal. A histogram of household incomes with a tall bar near $40,000-$60,000 and a long, thinning bar extending out to $500,000+ is right-skewed — and reporting "the average household income is $95,000" for that data, without mentioning the skew, would overstate what a "typical" household actually earns.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What defines a normal distribution?

Symmetry and the specific bell-shaped clustering around the mean are the defining features of a normal distribution.

2. In right-skewed data like income, why does the mean tend to sit higher than the median?

The mean's sensitivity to magnitude, versus the median's sensitivity only to position, is exactly what separates them under skew.

3. Why does recognizing skew matter when deciding whether to summarize a dataset with a mean or a median?

Skew is precisely the condition under which mean and median diverge and the choice between them starts to matter practically.

4. Fill in the blank: the symmetric, bell-shaped distribution that many standard statistical methods (including confidence intervals) assume by default is the ___.

The normal distribution's symmetric, bell-curve shape underlies a large share of standard statistical methods.

5. Fill in the blank: a distribution with a long tail toward high values, such as income or home prices, is described as ___.

Right-skewed (positive skew) data has its longer tail stretching toward high values.
