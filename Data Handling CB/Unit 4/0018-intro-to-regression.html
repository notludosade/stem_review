Intro to Regression

Lesson 16 measured how strongly two variables move together. Regression goes one step further: fitting a line that lets you predict one variable from the other — closing out this unit's statistics toolkit.

← Lesson 17 Lesson 19 → Contents Glossary →

This lesson covers simple linear regression only — one predictor variable, one outcome. More advanced regression and predictive modeling belong to AI Developer's territory, not this course.

Simple linear regression

Definition Simple linear regression fits a straight line, y = mx + b, through a scatter of (x, y) points, chosen so the line is the best overall fit to the data (specifically, the line minimizing the total squared vertical distance between each point and the line — "least squares"). x is the predictor (independent variable); y is the outcome being predicted (dependent variable); m is the slope (how much y changes per unit of x); b is the intercept (the predicted y when x is 0).

R-squared

Definition R-squared (ranging from 0 to 1) measures how much of the variation in y is explained by the fitted line's relationship with x. An R-squared of 0.81 means 81% of the variation in y is accounted for by the linear relationship with x — the remaining 19% is due to other factors the model doesn't capture. A low R-squared means the line is a poor fit, even if the slope itself is statistically meaningful.
Why regression doesn't escape Lesson 16's correlation-vs-causation warning Fitting a line that predicts y from x, and even confirming it fits well (high R-squared), still only describes an association — it doesn't establish that x causes y. A regression line predicting ice cream-sales from drowning-deaths (or the reverse) could fit reasonably well precisely because both are driven by the same confounding variable, warm weather, from Lesson 16. Regression is a more precise, quantified way of describing a relationship between variables — it is not a different, causation-proof technique. The same question always applies: is there a plausible confounder, or was this relationship established through a controlled experiment?
Worked example A regression of ice cream sales (x) against monthly temperature (y needn't fit either direction cleanly — but suppose we fit temperature predicting sales) gives sales = 40(temperature) + 200, R-squared 0.78. Interpretation: for every 1-degree increase in temperature, predicted sales rise by 40 units, and temperature explains about 78% of the variation in sales — but this says nothing about whether raising the temperature in a room would cause more ice cream to sell; both are driven by the season.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. In simple linear regression's equation y = mx + b, what does m represent?

The slope m is specifically the rate of change of y per unit of x; b (the intercept) is the value at x=0.

2. What does an R-squared value of 0.81 mean?

R-squared measures explained variation in y, not prediction accuracy on individual points or anything about causation.

3. Why doesn't a well-fitting regression line (high R-squared) establish that x causes y?

Regression quantifies association more precisely than a plain correlation coefficient, but it inherits the exact same correlation-vs-causation limitation from Lesson 16.

4. Fill in the blank: the statistic, ranging from 0 to 1, measuring how much of the variation in y is explained by its linear relationship with x is ___.

R-squared quantifies explained variation, distinct from the slope or intercept themselves.

5. Fill in the blank: the method of fitting a regression line by minimizing the total squared vertical distance between each data point and the line is called ___.

Least squares is the standard fitting criterion for simple linear regression.
