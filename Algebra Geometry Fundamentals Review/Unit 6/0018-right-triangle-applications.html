Right-Triangle Applications

The capstone of this course: the Pythagorean Theorem, algebra's equation-solving, and the coordinate plane all meet in one formula.

← Lesson 17 Contents Glossary →

This whole course has moved between two subjects — algebra and geometry — treating them as separate toolkits. This last lesson shows they were never really separate: the distance formula, one of the most useful tools in coordinate geometry, is nothing but the Pythagorean Theorem wearing algebra notation.

Deriving the distance formula

Derivation Take any two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂). Draw the horizontal and vertical legs connecting them — a right triangle, with legs of length |x₂ − x₁| and |y₂ − y₁| and hypotenuse equal to the straight-line distance d between the points. By the Pythagorean Theorem: d² = (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)², so d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²] — the distance formula, and nothing more than a² + b² = c² with coordinates plugged in.

Drag either point. Watch the right triangle connecting them, and confirm the distance always matches √(dx² + dy²).

Try It — The Distance Formula IS the Pythagorean Theorem

Why this closes out the course Every unit so far handed you a formula and a method. This one shows those aren't separate stories: Unit 1's coordinate points, Unit 6's right triangle, and simple algebraic substitution combine into a single new tool. That's the real payoff of a fundamentals course — not just each piece on its own, but seeing how they snap together.

Worked example

Find the distance between (1, 2) and (4, 6) d = √[(4 − 1)² + (6 − 2)²] = √[3² + 4²] = √[9 + 16] = √25 = 5.
Notice the legs (3 and 4) form the familiar (3, 4, 5) Pythagorean triple from Lesson 17 — recognizing it skips the final square root entirely.

A second application: the height of a leaning ladder

Worked example A 13-foot ladder leans against a wall, its base 5 feet from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach? The ladder is the hypotenuse (13), the ground distance is one leg (5), and the height is the other leg: h² = 13² − 5² = 169 − 25 = 144, so h = 12 feet.

Check your recall

No peeking back up — answer from memory first, then check.

1. What is the distance between (2, 3) and (7, 15)?

d = √[(7−2)² + (15−3)²] = √[5² + 12²] = √[25 + 144] = √169 = 13.

2. A 10-foot ladder leans against a wall with its base 6 feet from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?

h² = 10² − 6² = 100 − 36 = 64, so h = √64 = 8 feet.

3. What is the distance formula's relationship to the Pythagorean Theorem?

d² = (x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² is exactly a² + b² = c² with the legs written as coordinate differences.

4. Fill in the blank: d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²] is called the ___.

This is the distance formula — the Pythagorean Theorem rewritten with coordinate differences as the two legs.

5. Fill in the blank: the distance between (0, 0) and (6, 8) is ___.

d = √[6² + 8²] = √[36 + 64] = √100 = 10 — the (6, 8, 10) Pythagorean triple.
