Solving by Factoring

One property turns "a product equals zero" into "one of the pieces must be zero" — the fastest route to a quadratic's solutions when it factors cleanly.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 4 covers factoring itself in depth. This lesson assumes you can already factor a quadratic and focuses on what to do with that factored form: turn it into two easy linear equations using one key property.

The zero-product property

Definition The zero-product property: if two factors multiply to zero, at least one of them must be zero — there is no other way for a product of real numbers to equal 0. So if (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0, then either x − 2 = 0 or x − 3 = 0 (or both). This only works when one side of the equation is exactly 0 — it's why solving by factoring always starts by moving everything to standard form first.

A quadratic can have two, one, or zero real solutions

Why "two" isn't guaranteed x² − 5x + 6 = 0 has two different solutions because it factors into two different linear factors. But x² − 6x + 9 = 0 factors as (x − 3)(x − 3) = 0 — the same factor twice, giving only one solution, x = 3 (called a repeated root). And some quadratics, like x² + 4 = 0, don't factor over the real numbers at all — there's no real x whose square is negative. Factoring only finds solutions when they exist and are rational; Lesson 6 covers a method that always works.

Watch for this common mistake

A tempting shortcut that doesn't work Given (x − 2)(x − 3) = 6, it's tempting to set each factor equal to 6. Don't — the zero-product property only applies when the product equals zero. This equation must first be expanded and rearranged into standard form (x² − 5x + 6 = 6, then x² − 5x = 0) before factoring again.

1. According to the zero-product property, if (x + 4)(x − 7) = 0, then:

A product of zero means at least one factor is zero — so either x + 4 = 0 or x − 7 = 0 (giving x = −4 or x = 7).

2. Solve by factoring: x² − x − 12 = 0. What are the solutions?

x² − x − 12 factors as (x − 4)(x + 3) = 0, since −4 × 3 = −12 and −4 + 3 = −1, giving x = 4 or x = −3.

3. Why can't you solve (x − 1)(x + 5) = 7 by setting each factor equal to 7?

The zero-product property is only true for a product equal to zero — a product equal to 7 could come from countless factor pairs, not just "each factor equals 7."

4. Fill in the blank: when a quadratic factors as (x − 3)(x − 3) = 0, x = 3 is called a ___, since the same factor appears twice.

A repeated (or double) root happens when both factors are identical, giving only one distinct solution instead of two.

5. Fill in the blank: the rule that a product of factors equal to zero means at least one factor must be zero is called the ___.

The zero-product property is the entire justification for turning a factored quadratic into two simple linear equations.
