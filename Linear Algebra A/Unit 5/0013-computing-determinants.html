Computing Determinants

One number, computed from a square matrix, that already secretly appeared twice — as an area-scaling factor and as an invertibility test.

← Lesson 12 Lesson 14 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 6's ad − bc and Lesson 12's area-scaling factor were the same quantity in disguise: the determinant. This lesson gives it a formal definition and, crucially, extends it past 2×2 to any square matrix.

1. The 2×2 determinant

Definition — 2×2 Determinant For A = [[a, b], [c, d]], det(A) = |A| = ad − bc.

2. The 3×3 determinant, by cofactor expansion

Definition — Cofactor Expansion (along row 1) For a 3×3 matrix A, det(A) = a₁₁M₁₁ − a₁₂M₁₂ + a₁₃M₁₃, where Mᵢⱼ (the minor) is the determinant of the 2×2 matrix left after deleting row i and column j from A. The alternating signs (+, −, +) come from each term's position, not from the sign of the entry itself.
Why expand along a row at all Cofactor expansion breaks an n×n determinant down into n smaller (n−1)×(n−1) determinants, each one computable by the same rule one size down. It's recursive: a 3×3 reduces to three 2×2's, a 4×4 would reduce to four 3×3's, and so on.

Check your recall

1. For A = [[4, 3], [2, 5]], what is det(A)?

ad − bc = (4)(5) − (3)(2) = 20 − 6 = 14.

2. In cofactor expansion along the first row of a 3×3 matrix, what sign pattern applies to the three terms?

The signs alternate starting from +, based purely on position: (+, −, +) for columns 1, 2, 3 of row 1.

3. Fill in the blank: for [[1, 3], [2, 4]], the determinant is ___.

(1)(4) − (3)(2) = 4 − 6 = −2.

4. For A = [[2, 1, 0], [1, 3, 2], [0, 1, 4]], what is the minor M₁₁ (delete row 1, column 1)?

Deleting row 1 and column 1 leaves [[3, 2], [1, 4]], with determinant (3)(4) − (2)(1) = 12 − 2 = 10.

5. Fill in the blank: for B = [[1, 0, 2], [0, 1, 0], [3, 0, 4]], det(B) = ___ (expand along row 1: 1·M₁₁ − 0·M₁₂ + 2·M₁₃).

M₁₁ = det[[1,0],[0,4]] = 4. M₁₃ = det[[0,1],[3,0]] = 0 − 3 = −3. det(B) = 1(4) − 0(M₁₂) + 2(−3) = 4 − 6 = −2.
