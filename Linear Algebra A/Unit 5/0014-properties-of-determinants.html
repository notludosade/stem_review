Properties of Determinants

How each row operation from Lesson 8 changes the determinant — and a shortcut that turns elimination into the fastest way to compute one.

← Lesson 13 Lesson 15 → Contents Glossary →

Cofactor expansion works, but it gets slow fast — a 5×5 determinant by cofactor expansion means computing five 4×4 determinants. The properties in this lesson connect determinants back to Lesson 8's row operations, and hand you a far faster route.

1. What each row operation does to the determinant

Properties — Row Operations and det Swapping two rows negates the determinant. Scaling one row by a nonzero constant k multiplies the determinant by k. Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged.
Why this makes elimination a shortcut The third property is the important one: the row operation elimination relies on most (adding a multiple of one row to another) doesn't change the determinant at all. That means you can run Gaussian elimination down to a triangular matrix, tracking only swaps and scalings along the way, and the determinant falls out almost for free.
Shortcut — Triangular Matrices For an upper triangular, lower triangular, or diagonal matrix, det(A) is just the product of the diagonal entries — every cofactor-expansion term besides that one path down the diagonal turns out to multiply by a zero.

Click through each property with the same starting matrix, A = [[2, 3], [1, 4]], det(A) = 5.

Try It — What Changes, What Doesn't

A = [[2, 3], [1, 4]], det(A) = (2)(4) − (3)(1) = 5.

Swap R1 and R2: [[1, 4], [2, 3]], det = (1)(3) − (4)(2) = 3 − 8 = −5.

−5 = −(5) — the swap negated the determinant, exactly as predicted.

A = [[2, 3], [1, 4]], det(A) = 5.

Scale R1 by 3: [[6, 9], [1, 4]], det = (6)(4) − (9)(1) = 24 − 9 = 15.

15 = 3 × 5 — scaling a row by 3 scaled the determinant by 3.

A = [[2, 3], [1, 4]], det(A) = 5.

R2 = R2 + 2R1: [[2, 3], [5, 10]], det = (2)(10) − (3)(5) = 20 − 15 = 5.

Still 5 — adding a multiple of one row to another never changes the determinant.

For the upper triangular matrix [[2, 5, 1], [0, 3, 7], [0, 0, 4]]:

det = 2 × 3 × 4 = 24 — just the diagonal entries multiplied together, no cofactor expansion needed.

Cofactor expansion would confirm the same 24, far more slowly.

Check your recall

1. Swapping two rows of a matrix does what to its determinant?

A single row swap always flips the sign of the determinant, regardless of the matrix's entries.

2. If det(A) = 6 and you scale one row of A by 4, what is the new determinant?

Scaling one row by k multiplies the whole determinant by that same k: 4 × 6 = 24.

3. If det(A) = −3 and you add 5 times row 1 to row 2, what is the new determinant?

Adding a multiple of one row to another never changes the determinant, no matter the multiple used.

4. Fill in the blank: the determinant of a triangular matrix equals the ___ of its diagonal entries.

Every other term in the cofactor expansion of a triangular matrix contains a zero factor, leaving only the diagonal's product.

5. A 3×3 matrix has an entire row of zeros. What is its determinant?

Expanding along a zero row makes every term in the cofactor expansion multiply by 0 — the determinant is always 0, regardless of the other rows.
