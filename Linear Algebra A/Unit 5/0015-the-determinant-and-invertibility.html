The Determinant and Invertibility

Every thread from Units 2 through 5 — inverses, independence, rank, area — ties together at exactly one number.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 6 introduced ad − bc as the thing you divide by to build an inverse. Lesson 12 showed the same quantity measuring area-scaling. This lesson makes the connection precise, for square matrices of any size — and collects several other equivalent conditions along with it.

1. The theorem

Theorem — Determinant and Invertibility A square matrix A is invertible if and only if det(A) ≠ 0. Equivalently, for a square A, all of the following are either simultaneously true or simultaneously false: A is invertible · det(A) ≠ 0 · the columns of A are linearly independent · Ax = b has a unique solution for every b · rank(A) equals the size of A.
Why det(A) = 0 breaks invertibility Lesson 12 showed a linear transformation's area-scaling factor is |det(A)|. When det(A) = 0, the transformation squashes the entire plane down onto a line (or a point) — genuinely different input vectors get flattened onto the same output. An inverse would need to know which input produced a given output, and once information like that is destroyed, there's no way to recover it. This is precisely why the 2×2 inverse formula divides by ad − bc: dividing by 0 is the algebra's way of refusing to invert something that can't be inverted.

Check your recall

1. A square matrix A is invertible exactly when:

A nonzero determinant is the exact if-and-only-if condition for invertibility of a square matrix.

2. Is A = [[3, 6], [1, 2]] invertible?

Row 2 is exactly half of row 1, so det(A) = 6 − 6 = 0 — A is singular, not invertible.

3. Fill in the blank: if det(A) = 0 for a square matrix A, the columns of A are linearly ___.

det(A) = 0 is one of several equivalent ways of saying A's columns aren't independent — one column is a combination of the others.

4. Geometrically, what happens to the plane under a transformation whose matrix has determinant 0?

An area-scaling factor of |det(A)| = 0 means every region's area becomes 0 — the whole plane flattens onto something with less than two dimensions.

5. Fill in the blank (yes/no): if det(A) = 0 for a 5×5 matrix A, is A invertible?

The theorem holds for square matrices of any size, not just 2×2 or 3×3 — det(A) = 0 always rules out an inverse.
