Complex Zeros & the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Every zero the Rational Root Theorem can find is rational. Here's what's guaranteed to exist beyond it.

Take f(x) = x³ − 4x² + 6x − 4. Graphed below, it crosses the x-axis exactly once — yet it's degree 3. Lesson 9 said a cubic can have up to 2 turning points and up to 3 real zeros. This one has neither more than one of. Where did the other two zeros go?

One Crossing, Degree 3 — What's Missing?

f keeps rising the entire time — no wiggle, no second crossing. A cubic doesn't have to use up all its turning points or all its real zeros.

1. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Theorem Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: every polynomial of degree n ≥ 1 has at least one zero among the complex numbers. Applied repeatedly (dividing out each zero one at a time), it follows that a degree-n polynomial has exactly n zeros, counting multiplicity — some of which may be complex rather than real.

So this cubic has exactly 3 zeros total; the graph only shows the real ones. Let's find all three the same way Lesson 10 did — one candidate at a time.

Finishing the job Dividing out (x − 2) leaves the quotient x² − 2x + 2, remainder 0. That's only degree 2 — solvable directly by the quadratic formula: x = (2 ± √(4 − 8)) / 2 = (2 ± 2i) / 2 = 1 ± i. All three zeros: 2, 1 + i, 1 − i — exactly n = 3, exactly as the theorem promised.

2. Complex zeros never travel alone

Theorem Complex Conjugate Root Theorem: if a polynomial has real coefficients and a + bi (b ≠ 0) is a zero, then its conjugate a − bi is guaranteed to be a zero too. Complex zeros of a real-coefficient polynomial always show up in mirrored pairs — never alone.

That's exactly what happened above: 1 + i came paired with 1 − i, unprompted. Drag the point anywhere in the upper half below — its conjugate mirrors automatically underneath, for any real-coefficient polynomial whatsoever.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantees exactly n zeros for a degree-n polynomial, counting multiplicity — but says nothing about how many are real versus complex.

2. If 3 − 2i is a zero of a polynomial with real coefficients, then:

The Complex Conjugate Root Theorem pairs a + bi with its conjugate a − bi — for 3 − 2i, that partner is 3 + 2i, not any other rearrangement of the same digits.

3. Complex zeros are only guaranteed to pair up as conjugates when the polynomial has ___ coefficients.

The pairing depends on the coefficients being real numbers — a complex-coefficient polynomial can have a lone complex zero with no conjugate partner required.
