Dividing Polynomials & Finding Zeros

Lesson 9 assumed a factored form handed to you. Here's how to actually get there.

← Lesson 9 Lesson 11 → Contents Glossary →

Given a polynomial in expanded form, how do you find its zeros? The tool is synthetic division — a compact shortcut for dividing a polynomial by (x − r) — paired with two theorems that turn a division result directly into information about zeros.

1. Synthetic division

Definition To divide f(x) by (x − r): write f's coefficients in a row, bring down the first one, then repeatedly multiply the last result by r and add it into the next coefficient. The final row gives the quotient's coefficients (one degree lower than f) followed by the remainder.

Step through it live below, dividing P(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 by (x − 3).

Try It — Synthetic Division, Step by Step

P(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 ÷ (x − 3):

2 −3 −11 6

2. Two theorems that make division useful

Theorem Remainder Theorem: dividing f(x) by (x − r) always leaves a remainder equal to f(r) — no separate evaluation needed. The widget above found remainder 0, so f(3) = 0 without ever substituting 3 into the original expression.
Theorem Factor Theorem: r is a zero of f exactly when (x − r) divides f with remainder 0 — equivalently, exactly when (x − r) is a factor of f. Zero remainder and "r is a zero" are the same fact stated two ways.

Together: synthetic division by a candidate r checks, in one pass, whether r is a zero and hands you the reduced polynomial to keep factoring if it is.

3. Which candidates to even try?

Theorem Rational Root Theorem: if a polynomial has integer coefficients, every rational zero p/q (fully reduced) has p dividing the constant term and q dividing the leading coefficient. For P(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6: p divides 6 (±1, ±2, ±3, ±6), q divides 2 (±1, ±2) — so the only candidates worth testing are ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6, ±½, ±3⁄2.

That's a short, finite list instead of infinitely many real numbers — but the theorem doesn't say which candidates work, only which are worth testing. Synthetic division is how you check. Below, x = 1 and x = −2 are two of the candidates from that list — flip between them.

Try It — Test Two Candidates

P(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 ÷ (x − 1):

2 −3 −11 6

Click "Next Step" to begin.

P(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 ÷ (x + 2):

2 −3 −11 6

Click "Next Step" to begin.

Putting it together x = 1 leaves remainder −6 (not a zero); x = −2 leaves remainder 0 (a zero, matching the Factor Theorem). Dividing out both (x − 3) and (x + 2) reduces P down to the quadratic 2x − 1, giving the third zero x = ½ directly. All three zeros of this cubic — 3, −2, and ½ — came from nothing but synthetic division and these two theorems, with no cubic formula in sight.
