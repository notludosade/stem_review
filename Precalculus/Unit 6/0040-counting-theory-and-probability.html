Counting Theory, the Binomial Theorem, and Probability

The final lesson of the course: how to count arrangements without listing them, expand a binomial without multiplying it out, and turn both into the odds of something happening.

← Lesson 39 Contents Glossary →

1. Permutations and combinations

Definition — Permutation vs. Combination A permutation counts arrangements where order matters: P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)! ways to arrange r items chosen from n. A combination counts selections where order doesn't matter: C(n, r) = n! / (r!(n − r)!) — the permutation count divided by the r! ways those r items could themselves be ordered.
Worked Example Nine swimmers race; first, second, and third place matters. P(9,3) = 9 · 8 · 7 = 504 ways. Choosing 3 of 4 paintings to hang, with no ranking among them: C(4,3) = 4!/(3!·1!) = 4 ways — far fewer, since order no longer creates new outcomes.

2. The Binomial Theorem

Formula — Binomial Theorem (x + y)ⁿ = Σ (k=0 to n) C(n,k) · x(n−k) · yk. The coefficients are exactly the combinations C(n,k) — and arranged by row, they form Pascal's Triangle, symmetric left to right, each entry the sum of the two above it.

4. Probability

Definition — Probability The probability of an event is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), equal to the count of favorable outcomes divided by the count of total outcomes — and counting theory is exactly the tool for finding both counts.
Worked Example A store has 8 phones, 3 defective. Buying 2 at random, the probability both are non-defective is: (ways to choose 2 of the 5 good phones) / (ways to choose any 2 of the 8) = C(5,2) / C(8,2) = 10 / 28 = 5/14.
