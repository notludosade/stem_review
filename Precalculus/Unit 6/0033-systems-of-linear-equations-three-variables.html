Systems of Linear Equations in Three Variables

Three equations, three unknowns — the same elimination idea as Lesson 32, just applied twice in a row to work a variable down at a time.

← Lesson 32 Lesson 34 → Contents Glossary →

A solution to a system in x, y, and z is an ordered triple satisfying all three equations at once — geometrically, the single point where three planes all intersect. The strategy is to eliminate one variable between two pairs of equations, collapsing three equations in three unknowns down to two equations in two — exactly Lesson 32's problem.

1. The elimination strategy

Strategy — Solving a 3×3 system (1) Pick a variable and eliminate it from two different pairs of equations, producing two new equations in the remaining two variables. (2) Solve that 2×2 system (Lesson 32's methods). (3) Substitute both found values back into any original equation to get the third variable.
Worked Example x + y + z = 6, 2x − y + z = 3, x + 2y − z = 2. Adding equations 1 and 2 eliminates y: 3x + 2z = 9. Adding equations 2 and 3 eliminates z: 3x + y = 5. Adding equations 1 and 3 also eliminates z: 2x + 3y = 8. Solving the last two together gives x = 1, then y = 5 − 3(1) = 2, then z = 6 − 1 − 2 = 3 — the triple (1, 2, 3). Check: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 ✓, 2(1) − 2 + 3 = 3 ✓, 1 + 2(2) − 3 = 2 ✓.

2. Classifying 3×3 systems

Classification Just as with two variables: a system with exactly one solution (one shared point among all three planes) is consistent and independent. One with infinitely many solutions (the planes share a whole line, or coincide) is consistent and dependent. One where the elimination steps produce a false statement like 0 = 5 is inconsistent — no solution exists.

1. The strategy for a 3×3 system reduces it, step by step, to a system with:

Eliminating one variable from two different pairs of the original three equations leaves exactly two equations in the remaining two unknowns — Lesson 32's problem, solvable with substitution or elimination.

2. If the elimination steps for a 3×3 system produce a false statement like 0 = 5, the system is ___.

A false numerical statement means no values of x, y, and z can satisfy every equation at once — exactly what "inconsistent" describes, the same as two parallel lines in the two-variable case.

3. For x+y+z=6, 2x−y+z=3, x+2y−z=2, the solution is:

Substituting x=1, y=2, z=3 into all three original equations checks out: 1+2+3=6, 2(1)−2+3=3, and 1+2(2)−3=2 — all three hold, confirming (1,2,3) as the unique solution.

Keeping track of which equations have already been combined — and not accidentally eliminating the same variable the same way twice — is the most common bookkeeping slip here. Ask for a messier 3×3 system to practice organizing the steps if that tracking still feels error-prone. Lesson 34 turns to systems that aren't linear at all.
