Systems of Linear Equations in Two Variables

One equation gives a whole line of solutions. Two equations, considered together, usually pin that down to a single point.

← Lesson 31 Lesson 33 → Contents Glossary →

This starts the final unit of the course. A system of linear equations asks for every (x, y) that satisfies more than one equation simultaneously — not each equation's own line separately, but the point (or points) they share.

1. Solving by substitution and elimination

Method — Substitution Solve one equation for one variable, then substitute that expression into the other equation — reducing two equations in two unknowns to one equation in one unknown.
Method — Elimination (Addition) Multiply one or both equations by a constant so that a variable's coefficients become opposites, then add the equations — that variable cancels, again leaving one equation in one unknown.
Worked Example y = 2x − 1 and y = −x + 5. Substituting: 2x − 1 = −x + 5 → 3x = 6 → x = 2. Then y = 2(2) − 1 = 3. The solution is the single point (2, 3) — the one place both lines agree.

2. Three kinds of systems

Classification Consistent, independent: different slopes, exactly one solution (the lines cross once). Consistent, dependent: identical slope and intercept, infinitely many solutions (the lines coincide). Inconsistent: same slope, different intercepts, no solution (the lines are parallel and never meet).

3. Finding the shared point live

The dashed line is fixed at y = −x + 5. Drag the point along the solid line y = 2x − 1 and find where it also lands on the dashed line — that's the system's solution.

Check your recall

1. A system with two parallel, non-identical lines is:

Parallel lines with different intercepts never cross, so no ordered pair satisfies both equations — that's exactly what "inconsistent" means. "Independent" describes a consistent system with one solution, and "dependent" describes coinciding lines.

2. Making one variable's coefficients opposites, then adding the equations, is called the method of ___.

Adding the two equations after matching opposite coefficients cancels one variable entirely — the defining move of the elimination (addition) method, as distinct from substitution.

3. The solution to y = 2x − 1 and y = −x + 5 is:

Setting 2x − 1 = −x + 5 gives x = 2, and substituting back gives y = 2(2) − 1 = 3 — the point (2, 3) satisfies both equations at once.
