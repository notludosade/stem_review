Transformations of Functions

Shifting, stretching, and flipping a graph — without touching the underlying shape.

← Lesson 4 Lesson 6 → Contents Glossary →

Once you know one function's graph, you know the graph of an entire family of related functions — because a few small edits to the equation move, stretch, or flip the shape in completely predictable ways.

Why it works Slide h positive and the graph moves right, even though you're subtracting. That's because y = f(x − h) asks "what did f output h steps earlier?" — to get the same output you now have to plug in a bigger x. k is the opposite and easier: it's added directly to the output, so positive k moves the whole graph straight up.

Notice a does two jobs at once: its size stretches (|a| > 1) or compresses (|a| < 1) the graph vertically, and its sign flips it upside down when negative. That second part — flipping — deserves its own closer look, because there are actually two different kinds of flip.

2. Two different reflections

Using f(x) = √x, toggle each button below independently and compare.

Try It — Vertical vs. Horizontal Reflection

y = √x

Vertical reflection negates the output — flip over the x-axis. Horizontal reflection negates the input — flip over the y-axis. They look similar in name but move completely different parts of the equation, and (as you can see by toggling both) they're independent of each other.

Check your recall

1. The graph of y = f(x) + 5 compared to y = f(x) is shifted:

Adding outside the function acts directly on the output — every y-value goes up by exactly 5.

2. The graph of y = f(x − 3) compared to y = f(x) is shifted:

Subtracting inside the function shifts right, not left — the classic reversed-sign gotcha you just tested with the h slider.

3. y = −f(x) reflects the graph over the ___ axis.

Negating the output flips the graph vertically, over the x-axis — reflecting over the y-axis instead is what negating the input (f(−x)) does.
