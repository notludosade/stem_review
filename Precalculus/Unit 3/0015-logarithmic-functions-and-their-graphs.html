Logarithmic Functions and Their Graphs

The question "what power gives me this?" — and the mirror-image graph that answers it.

← Lesson 14 Lesson 16 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 14's exponential function answers "what do I get if I raise b to the x power?" A logarithm asks the reverse question: "what power do I need to raise b to, to get this number?" Reverse questions come from inverse functions — and that's exactly the relationship between the two.

1. The logarithm, defined

Definition y = logb(x) is equivalent to bʸ = x, for x > 0, b > 0, b ≠ 1. In words: logb(x) is the exponent b must be raised to in order to get x.

Because a logarithm is the inverse of an exponential function, its graph is the exponential's graph reflected across the line y = x. Everything about the log's shape — its domain, its asymptote, even its direction of increase — is the exponential's properties, mirrored.

2. Domain, range, and asymptote

Rules For y = logb(x): domain is (0, ∞) — logs of zero or negative numbers are undefined. Range is all real numbers, (−∞, ∞). The vertical asymptote is x = 0. This is exactly the exponential's domain and range swapped — inevitable, since reflecting across y = x always swaps a function's domain and range with its inverse's.

3. Two special bases: common and natural logs

Definitions Common logarithm: log(x) means log10(x). Natural logarithm: ln(x) means loge(x), where e ≈ 2.71828 is the same constant Lesson 19 will meet again as the base whose exponential is its own rate of change.

1. y = logb(x) is equivalent to:

The logarithm y is defined as the exponent that b must be raised to in order to produce x — so raising b to the y power must give back x exactly.

2. The domain of y = logb(x) is:

Logs of zero or negative numbers are undefined — there's no real power you can raise a positive base to that produces zero or a negative result. That restriction is also the exponential function's range, swapped in by the inverse relationship.

3. ln(x) is log base ___ of x.

The natural logarithm always uses base e ≈ 2.71828 — the same constant that will reappear as the exponential function that is its own derivative.
