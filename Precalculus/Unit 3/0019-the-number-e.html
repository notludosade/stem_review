The Number e

Compound interest, pushed to its limit, lands on the single most important constant in Calculus.

Lessons 15 and 18 have already been using e without deriving it. It's time to see where it actually comes from — and it comes from a very ordinary question about money.

1. Compound interest

Formula A(t) = P(1 + r/n)nt: P is the principal, r is the annual rate (as a decimal), n is the number of times interest compounds per year, and t is years.

Fix $1 invested at 100% annual interest for one year, and compound it more and more often. What happens to the payout?

2. e as a limit

Definition e = limn→∞ (1 + 1/n)ⁿ ≈ 2.718281828... — an irrational number, named after Leonhard Euler, that the compounding widget above is closing in on as n grows without bound.

Type in larger and larger values of n below to watch (1 + 1/n)ⁿ keep closing the gap to e — it never quite reaches it, but gets arbitrarily close.

3. Continuous compounding and continuous growth

Formulas As n → ∞, the compound-interest formula becomes A(t) = Pert — continuous compounding. It's the same shape as Lesson 18's growth model A(t) = A₀ekt, with P playing the role of A₀ and r playing the role of k.

This is exactly why e keeps showing up: any process compounding continuously — money, population, radioactive decay run in reverse — is described by the same base-e formula, not because of a coincidence, but because e is what "compounding without pause" converges to.

3. The constant e is named after the mathematician ___.

Leonhard Euler is the mathematician the constant e is named for, though he did not discover it first — it had appeared earlier in work on compound interest.
