Angles, Degrees, and Radians

A fresh function family starts with a fresh way to measure the thing it takes as input: rotation.

← Lesson 19 Lesson 21 → Contents Glossary →

Every function family so far has taken a plain number as input. Trigonometric functions take an angle — an amount of rotation — which means the first job is pinning down exactly how angles get measured and named before any function can be built on top of them.

1. Standard position and two units of measurement

Definition An angle in standard position has its vertex at the origin and its initial side along the positive x-axis. The terminal side is wherever the initial side lands after rotating — counterclockwise for a positive angle, clockwise for a negative one.

Degrees split a full rotation into 360 equal pieces, a convention going back to Babylonian astronomy. Radians measure rotation a completely different way: by arc length.

Definition One radian is the angle swept when the arc length traveled equals the radius: for a circle of radius r, an angle of θ radians traces an arc of length s = rθ. A full rotation traces the whole circumference, 2πr — so a full rotation measures 2π radians, matching 360°.

Setting 2π radians = 360° gives the conversion factor both directions:

Formulas Degrees → radians: multiply by π/180. Radians → degrees: multiply by 180/π.
Worked Example Convert 45° to radians: 45 · (π/180) = π/4. Convert 3π/4 radians to degrees: (3π/4) · (180/π) = 135°.

2. Coterminal and reference angles

Two angles are coterminal if they share the same terminal side — which happens whenever they differ by a whole number of full rotations.

Formulas Coterminal angles: θ ± 360°·k (or θ ± 2π·k), for any whole number k. Reference angle: the acute angle between the terminal side and the (nearest) x-axis — always between 0° and 90° (or 0 and π/2 radians).
Worked Example 400° is coterminal with 400° − 360° = 40° — same terminal side, one full rotation apart. The reference angle of 200° is 200° − 180° = 20°, since 200° lands in the third quadrant, 20° past the negative x-axis.

Reference angles matter because every trigonometric function's value at any angle is determined (up to a sign) by its value at that angle's reference angle — Lesson 21 leans on this directly.

Check your recall

1. Converting 120° to radians gives:

120 · (π/180) reduces to 2π/3. Flipping the fraction, halving instead of the right reduction, or doubling the result all land on the three distractors.

2. An angle of 250° lies in which quadrant?

180° to 270° is the third quadrant, and 250° falls inside that range — past the negative x-axis but not yet down to the negative y-axis.

3. The reference angle of 340° is ___ degrees.

340° sits in the fourth quadrant, 360° − 340° = 20° short of a full rotation — so its terminal side is 20° from the (positive) x-axis.
