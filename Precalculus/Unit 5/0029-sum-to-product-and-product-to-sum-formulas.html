Sum-to-Product and Product-to-Sum Formulas

Two more families, both squeezed out of the same sum and difference formulas from Lesson 27 — just added or subtracted in a different order.

← Lesson 28 Lesson 30 → Contents Glossary →

Lesson 27 gave four formulas: sin(α+β), sin(α−β), cos(α+β), cos(α−β). Add or subtract pairs of them, and two entirely new toolkits fall out — one that turns products into sums, one that turns sums into products.

1. Product-to-sum formulas

Formula — Product-to-sum Adding the two cosine formulas gives cosα cosβ = ½[cos(α−β) + cos(α+β)]. Subtracting them gives sinα sinβ = ½[cos(α−β) − cos(α+β)]. Adding the two sine formulas gives sinα cosβ = ½[sin(α+β) + sin(α−β)].
Worked Example Express cos75° cos15° as a sum, then evaluate it. cos75°cos15° = ½[cos(75°−15°) + cos(75°+15°)] = ½[cos60° + cos90°] = ½[½ + 0] = 1/4 — no calculator needed, since 60° and 90° are both special angles.

2. Sum-to-product formulas

Formula — Sum-to-product Running the algebra in reverse (substituting u = α+β, v = α−β into the product-to-sum formulas and solving back for α, β) gives: sinα + sinβ = 2 sin((α+β)/2) cos((α−β)/2), sinα − sinβ = 2 cos((α+β)/2) sin((α−β)/2), cosα + cosβ = 2 cos((α+β)/2) cos((α−β)/2), and cosα − cosβ = −2 sin((α+β)/2) sin((α−β)/2).

Physically, this is the mathematics of beats — two sound waves at slightly different pitches don't just add; the sum-to-product form reveals a fast oscillation riding inside a slow one, which is exactly what a beating "wah-wah-wah" sound is.

1. cosα cosβ equals:

Adding cos(α−β) and cos(α+β) cancels the sine terms, leaving 2cosαcosβ — divide by 2 for the formula. The second option is actually sinα sinβ's formula instead; the sine-based options don't correspond to a cosine product at all.

2. sinα + sinβ = 2 sin((α+β)/2) ___((α−β)/2).

The sum-to-product formula for sinα + sinβ pairs a sine of the half-sum with a cosine of the half-difference — the missing function is cos.

3. cosα − cosβ equals:

cosα − cosβ is the one sum-to-product formula with a leading minus sign, paired with two sines. Dropping the minus, or swapping to cosines, both name a different one of the four formulas.
