Verifying Trigonometric Identities

The six trig functions are all secretly built from just two of them. Identities are the equations that prove it — and the toolkit for rewriting one messy expression as a simpler one.

← Lesson 25 Lesson 27 → Contents Glossary →

Unit 4 built six functions and graphed all of them. This unit asks what's true about them — equations that hold for every valid input, not just special angles. These identities are the algebra of trigonometry: the tools that let you simplify, rewrite, and eventually solve trig equations in Lesson 30.

1. The fundamental identities

Formula — Pythagorean identities sin²x + cos²x = 1, straight from the unit circle. Dividing through by cos²x gives 1 + tan²x = sec²x; dividing through by sin²x gives 1 + cot²x = csc²x. All three are really the same one fact, seen three ways.
Formula — Reciprocal, quotient, and even-odd identities Reciprocal: csc x = 1/sin x, sec x = 1/cos x, cot x = 1/tan x. Quotient: tan x = sin x/cos x, cot x = cos x/sin x. Even-odd: cos(−x) = cos x (cosine is even); sin(−x) = −sin x and tan(−x) = −tan x (sine and tangent are odd) — matching where each function's graph has mirror symmetry.

Every one of these is a definition or a one-line consequence of the unit circle — nothing here needs memorizing from scratch if Unit 4 is solid.

2. Verifying an identity

Worked Example Verify: (1 − cos²x)/sin x = sin x. Start from the more complicated side: 1 − cos²x = sin²x, by the Pythagorean identity. So the left side becomes sin²x/sin x = sin x, matching the right side exactly. Done — the strategy is always the same: rewrite the messier side using known identities until it matches the other.

Check your recall

1. 1 + tan²x equals:

Dividing sin²x + cos²x = 1 through by cos²x gives tan²x + 1 = sec²x. Dividing by sin²x instead gives the cot²x/csc²x version — a different, easily confused sibling identity, not this one.

2. Because sine is an odd function, sin(−x) = ___.

An odd function satisfies f(−x) = −f(x) — flip the input's sign, and the output's sign flips too. Sine's graph has this exact symmetry (180° rotational symmetry about the origin), so sin(−x) = −sin x.

3. Rewriting 1 − cos²x as sin²x uses the:

sin²x + cos²x = 1 rearranges directly to 1 − cos²x = sin²x — that rearrangement is the Pythagorean identity at work, not a ratio-based, flip-based, or symmetry-based identity.
