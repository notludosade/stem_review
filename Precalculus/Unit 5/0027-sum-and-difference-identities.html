Sum and Difference Identities

sin(α + β) is not sin α + sin β. These five formulas are what it actually equals — and they let you reach angles the unit circle never memorized directly.

← Lesson 26 Lesson 28 → Contents Glossary →

The unit circle gives exact values at a handful of special angles: 30°, 45°, 60°, and their relatives. These formulas break any angle into a sum or difference of two special angles, reaching exact values everywhere in between.

1. The formulas

Formula — Cosine and sine cos(α ± β) = cos α cos β ∓ sin α sin β (signs flip between the two versions). sin(α ± β) = sin α cos β ± cos α sin β (signs match here instead). Notice cosine's formula flips the sign, while sine's formula keeps it — the single easiest place to mix the two up.
Formula — Tangent tan(α ± β) = (tan α ± tan β) / (1 ∓ tan α tan β), built by dividing the sine sum/difference formula by the cosine one. Same flip-the-sign pattern as cosine shows up in the denominator.
Worked Example Find cos(75°) exactly, using 75° = 45° + 30°. cos(75°) = cos45°cos30° − sin45°sin30° = (√2/2)(√3/2) − (√2/2)(1/2) = (√6 − √2)/4 — an exact value the unit circle alone never lists, reached entirely from two angles it does.

Check your recall

1. cos(α + β) equals:

Cosine's sum/difference formula flips the sign: cos(α+β) uses minus, cos(α−β) uses plus. The other two options are sine's formulas instead, which keep the sign matching the ± on the left.

2. Using α = 45° and β = 30°: cos(α − β) = cos45°cos30° + ___.

cos(α−β) = cosαcosβ + sinαsinβ — the difference formula uses a plus sign, so the missing term is sin45°sin30°.

3. tan(α − β) equals:

Tangent's formula flips the sign in the denominator relative to the numerator: tan(α−β) pairs a minus on top with a plus on the bottom. The first distractor is tan(α+β); the other two mismatch the sign pattern entirely.
