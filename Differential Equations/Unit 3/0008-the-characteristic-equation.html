The Characteristic Equation — Real, Repeated, and Complex Roots

Lesson 7 solved the easy case. A quadratic's discriminant can also hit exactly zero, or go negative — and each case needs its own solution template, not a guess.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

1. Three cases, from one discriminant

Fact — Case 1: Real, Distinct Roots (b² − 4ac > 0) Two different real roots r₁ ≠ r₂ give two independent solutions. The general solution is y = C₁er₁x + C₂er₂x.
Fact — Case 2: Repeated Root (b² − 4ac = 0) A single root r = −b/(2a) only supplies one solution, erx — not enough for a second-order equation. A second, independent solution is xerx (found by reduction of order). The general solution is y = (C₁ + C₂x)erx — note the second term carries an extra factor of x, not just a second constant.
Fact — Case 3: Complex Conjugate Roots (b² − 4ac < 0) The roots come as a conjugate pair r = α ± βi. Using Euler's formula, the real-valued general solution is y = eαx(C₁cos(βx) + C₂sin(βx)) — an exponential envelope (from the real part α) multiplying an oscillation (from the imaginary part β), never a bare exponential in βi.
Worked Example — All Three Cases y″ − 5y′ + 6y = 0: r = 2, 3 (real, distinct) → y = C₁e2x + C₂e3x. y″ − 6y′ + 9y = 0: r = 3, 3 (repeated) → y = (C₁ + C₂x)e3x. y″ + 4y = 0: r = ±2i, so α = 0, β = 2 (complex) → y = C₁cos(2x) + C₂sin(2x).

Check your recall

1. If the characteristic equation has two distinct real roots r₁ and r₂, the general solution is:

Two distinct roots give two independent solutions, e^(r₁x) and e^(r₂x), combined with their own constants — dropping either term would lose a degree of freedom the second-order equation needs.

2. Fill in the blank: for a repeated root r, the general solution is y = (C₁ + C₂___)e^(rx)

The second independent solution is xe^(rx), not a second copy of e^(rx) — that extra factor of x is what keeps the two solutions independent.

3. If the characteristic equation has complex conjugate roots α ± βi, the real-valued general solution is:

The real part α of the root always builds the exponential envelope e^(αx); the imaginary part β always builds the oscillation cos(βx) and sin(βx) — mixing the two up (as the last option does) swaps envelope and frequency.

4. Fill in the blank: for y″ + 4y = 0, the roots are r = ±2i, so the general solution is y = C₁cos(___x) + C₂sin(___x)

Here α = 0 (no exponential envelope, since e^(0x) = 1) and β = 2, giving pure oscillation y = C₁cos(2x) + C₂sin(2x).

5. y″ − 6y′ + 9y = 0 has characteristic equation r² − 6r + 9 = (r − 3)² = 0 — a repeated root r = 3. The general solution is:

C₁e^(3x) + C₂e^(3x) collapses to a single constant times e^(3x) — not two independent solutions. The correct second solution is xe^(3x) (same root, extra factor of x), giving y = (C₁ + C₂x)e^(3x).
