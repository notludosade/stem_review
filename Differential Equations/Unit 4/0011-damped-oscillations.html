Damped Oscillations

Real springs have friction. Adding one term — a first derivative — reopens all three of Unit 3's root cases at once, and each one describes a physically distinct way of settling down.

← Lesson 10 Lesson 12 → Contents Glossary →

1. Adding friction to the model

Definition — Damped Spring-Mass System Adding a friction force proportional to velocity, −cx′, to Lesson 10's model gives mx″ + cx′ + kx = 0, usually rewritten x″ + 2βx′ + ω₀²x = 0 with β = c/(2m) (the damping rate) and ω₀ = √(k/m) (the undamped natural frequency from Lesson 10).
Fact — Three Damping Regimes The characteristic equation r² + 2βr + ω₀² = 0 reopens all three of Unit 3's root cases, now decided by comparing β and ω₀: underdamped (β < ω₀, complex roots) oscillates with a decaying envelope, x(t) = e−βt(C₁cos(ω_d t) + C₂sin(ω_d t)) with ω_d = √(ω₀² − β²); critically damped (β = ω₀, repeated root) returns to equilibrium the fastest of all three without ever oscillating, x(t) = (C₁ + C₂t)e−βt; overdamped (β > ω₀, real distinct roots) also never oscillates but returns to equilibrium more slowly than the critical case, x(t) = C₁er₁t + C₂er₂t with both r₁, r₂ < 0.
Worked Example ω₀ = 2 (so k/m = 4). With β = 1 (β < ω₀, underdamped): ω_d = √(4−1) = √3, and the motion oscillates inside a decaying envelope e−t. With β = 2 (β = ω₀, critically damped): x(t) = (C₁ + C₂t)e−2t, settling with no oscillation at all. With β = 3 (β > ω₀, overdamped): r = −3 ± √5, both negative, and x(t) is a sum of two decaying exponentials — settling even more sluggishly than critical damping.

Check your recall

1. Adding friction to Lesson 10's spring model gives the equation:

A friction force proportional to velocity adds a first-derivative term with a plus sign (friction opposes motion, sapping energy) — x″ + 2βx′ + ω₀²x = 0.

2. Underdamped motion (oscillation inside a decaying envelope) occurs when:

β < ω₀ makes the characteristic equation's discriminant negative, giving complex roots and therefore oscillation — damped just enough to still swing back and forth.

3. Critically damped motion occurs when:

β = ω₀ makes the discriminant exactly zero — a repeated root, the boundary case between oscillating (underdamped) and not (overdamped).

4. Fill in the blank: for underdamped motion, x(t) = e^(−___t)(C₁cos(ω_d t) + C₂sin(ω_d t)) — the amplitude envelope decays according to this rate.

The real part of the complex roots, −β, controls the exponential envelope; the imaginary part ω_d controls the oscillation frequency inside it.

5. Of the three damping regimes, which returns to equilibrium fastest without ever oscillating?

Underdamped motion oscillates past equilibrium before settling; overdamped motion decays too sluggishly (the smaller of its two decay rates dominates). Critical damping sits exactly at the boundary, settling fastest of the two non-oscillating cases — the reason it's the target for car shock absorbers and door closers.
