Simple Harmonic Motion

Unit 3 built the machinery for complex roots without saying why anyone would want them. A frictionless spring is the cleanest possible reason — its motion is exactly Lesson 8's pure-oscillation case.

1. From Hooke's Law to a differential equation

Definition — Spring-Mass System A mass m on a spring with spring constant k obeys Hooke's Law: the restoring force is F = −kx, where x is displacement from equilibrium. Newton's second law (F = ma = mx″) gives mx″ = −kx, or x″ + ω²x = 0, where ω = √(k/m) is the system's natural angular frequency.
Fact — The Solution x″ + ω²x = 0 has characteristic equation r² + ω² = 0, giving purely imaginary roots r = ±ωi — exactly Lesson 8's complex case with α = 0. The solution is x(t) = C₁cos(ωt) + C₂sin(ωt), equivalently written x(t) = A·cos(ωt − φ) with amplitude A = √(C₁² + C₂²) and phase φ. The motion never decays — with no friction in the model, it oscillates forever at constant amplitude.
Worked Example A 2 kg mass hangs on a spring with k = 8 N/m, pulled to x(0) = 3 m and released from rest (x′(0) = 0). ω = √(8/2) = 2 rad/s. Since x′(t) = −C₁ω sin(ωt) + C₂ω cos(ωt), x′(0) = C₂ω = 0 forces C₂ = 0, and x(0) = C₁ = 3. The motion is x(t) = 3cos(2t), with period T = 2π/ω = π seconds.

1. An undamped spring-mass system obeys the differential equation:

Newton's second law with Hooke's restoring force gives mx″ = −kx, or x″ + ω²x = 0 with ω = √(k/m) — a second-order equation with a plus sign, giving oscillation rather than exponential runaway.

2. Fill in the blank: for a spring with m = 3 kg and k = 27 N/m, the angular frequency ω = √(k/m) = ___ rad/s

ω = √(27/3) = √9 = 3.

3. The period of simple harmonic motion x(t) = A·cos(ωt − φ) is:

Cosine completes one full cycle every time its argument increases by 2π, so ωT = 2π gives T = 2π/ω — larger ω means a shorter period, a faster oscillation.

4. Fill in the blank: released from rest (x′(0) = 0), the general solution x(t) = C₁cos(ωt) + C₂sin(ωt) always reduces to a pure cosine, because x′(0) = C₂ω forces C₂ = ___

x′(t) = −C₁ω sin(ωt) + C₂ω cos(ωt), so x′(0) = C₂ω. Setting this to 0 (with ω ≠ 0) forces C₂ = 0, leaving x(t) = C₁cos(ωt) — motion released from rest always starts at its own amplitude.
