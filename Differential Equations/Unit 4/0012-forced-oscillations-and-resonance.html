Forced Oscillations and Resonance

Lesson 11 let a spring settle on its own. Push it rhythmically instead, and Unit 3's undetermined coefficients returns — with a driving frequency that can pump in energy faster than damping removes it.

← Lesson 11 Unit 5, Lesson 13 → Contents Glossary →

1. Driving the system

Definition — Forced Oscillation Adding a periodic external force F₀cos(ωt) to Lesson 11's model gives mx″ + cx′ + kx = F₀cos(ωt) — nonhomogeneous, with the driving frequency ω generally different from the system's own natural frequency ω₀ = √(k/m).
Fact — Undamped Resonance With no damping (c = 0), x″ + ω₀²x = f₀cos(ωt): when ω ≠ ω₀, undetermined coefficients (Unit 3, Lesson 9) gives xp(t) = [f₀/(ω₀² − ω²)]cos(ωt) — bounded, steady oscillation. But when ω = ω₀, the trial form resonates exactly as in Lesson 9's resonance case, forcing a factor of t into the guess: xp(t) = [f₀/(2ω₀)]·t·sin(ω₀t). The amplitude now grows without bound as t → ∞ — pushing a swing at exactly its own natural rhythm, forever.
Fact — Damped Forced Response With damping present, the steady-state response has amplitude M(ω) = f₀ / √[(ω₀² − ω²)² + (2βω)²]. Unlike the undamped case, M(ω) stays finite even when ω = ω₀ — but it still peaks sharply near ω₀ for small β, and that peak flattens as β grows. This finite-but-large peak is practical resonance.
Worked Example For x″ + 9x = 10cos(2t) (so ω₀ = 3, f₀ = 10, ω = 2 ≠ ω₀): xp(t) = [10/(9 − 4)]cos(2t) = 2cos(2t). Driving at ω = 3 instead would hit resonance, and the amplitude would grow linearly in t rather than settling at a fixed value.

2. Watching the resonance peak

With ω₀ = 2 and f₀ = 1 fixed, slide the damping β and the driving frequency ω, and watch how close to ω₀ the response amplitude peaks — and how tall that peak gets as damping shrinks.

1. A forced spring-mass system is governed by:

Forcing adds a nonzero, time-varying right-hand side to the damped equation — a periodic external push rather than a homogeneous, self-contained system.

2. Fill in the blank: pure resonance in the undamped case occurs when the driving frequency ω equals ___

When ω = ω₀, the forcing term cos(ωt) matches the homogeneous solution exactly, triggering the same resonance rule from Unit 3 — multiply the trial form by t.

3. Compared to the undamped case, adding damping to a forced oscillator at resonance (ω = ω₀):

M(ω) = f₀/√[(ω₀²−ω²)² + (2βω)²] stays finite at ω = ω₀ as long as β > 0, unlike the undamped case's unbounded linear growth.

4. Fill in the blank: for x″ + 9x = 10cos(2t), the steady-state response is x_p(t) = ___·cos(2t)

x_p(t) = [f₀/(ω₀²−ω²)]cos(ωt) = [10/(9−4)]cos(2t) = 2cos(2t).

5. Holding ω₀ and f₀ fixed, as the damping β increases, the peak of the amplitude response curve M(ω) generally:

More damping removes energy faster, flattening the resonance peak — the sharp, tall spike of light damping becomes a low, broad bump as β grows.
