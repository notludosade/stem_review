Graphs, Vertices, and Edges

Unit 5 leaves numbers behind and starts drawing pictures. A graph is one of the simplest structures in all of mathematics — points, and connections between some pairs of them — and one of the most widely reused, from road maps to social networks to the dependency chain between the lessons in this course.

1. Graphs, vertices, and edges

Definition — Graph A graph G = (V, E) consists of a set V of vertices (or nodes) and a set E of edges, where each edge is an unordered pair of vertices, {u, v}. This lesson works with simple graphs: no edge from a vertex to itself (no loops), and no two vertices connected by more than one edge. E is literally a set — Unit 2's set operations and notation apply to it without modification, since an edge set is just a set of 2-element subsets of V.
Worked example V = {A, B, C, D}, E = {{A,B}, {B,C}, {C,D}, {D,A}} describes a 4-cycle: A connects to B and D, B connects to A and C, and so on around a square.

2. Degree and the Handshake Theorem

Definition — Degree The degree of a vertex v, written deg(v), is the number of edges incident to it (the number of other vertices it's directly connected to).
Theorem — The Handshake Theorem Σ (v ∈ V) deg(v) = 2|E| — the sum of every vertex's degree equals exactly twice the number of edges. Each edge {u,v} contributes exactly 1 to deg(u) and 1 to deg(v), so it's counted twice in the sum, once from each endpoint. A direct consequence: the sum of all degrees in any graph is always even, and the number of vertices with odd degree is always even.
Why this looks familiar Counting each edge from both endpoints is exactly the same "counted from two directions, must agree" logic as Unit 3's symmetry identity C(n,r) = C(n,n−r), and the Handshake Theorem's proof is a one-line application of the addition principle from Lesson 7: summing degrees vertex by vertex is the same total as summing 2 for every edge, just grouped differently.

3. Directed graphs, briefly

Directed vs. Undirected Everything above describes an undirected graph — {u,v} connects u to v and v to u equally. A directed graph (digraph) instead uses ordered pairs (u,v), meaning an edge that only goes from u to v — think one-way streets or "follows" on a social network. This course's Unit 5 focuses on undirected graphs; directed graphs reuse every definition here with "unordered pair" swapped for "ordered pair."

1. In a simple graph, which of these is disallowed?

Simple graphs disallow loops and multiple edges between the same pair of vertices — a vertex with degree 0, a fully-connected vertex, and an empty edge set are all perfectly valid.

2. A graph has 5 edges. What must the sum of all vertex degrees equal?

By the Handshake Theorem, the sum of degrees is always exactly 2|E| = 2(5) = 10, regardless of how many vertices the graph has.

3. Can a simple graph have exactly one vertex of odd degree, with every other vertex having even degree?

Since the total degree sum is always even (2|E|), and an odd number of odd terms would make the sum odd, the count of odd-degree vertices must always be even — a direct consequence of the Handshake Theorem.

4. Fill in the blank: an ___ is an unordered pair of vertices, {u, v}, in an undirected simple graph.

An edge connects two vertices without regard to order — {u,v} and {v,u} name the same edge.

5. Fill in the blank: the number of edges incident to a vertex v is called its ___.

deg(v) counts how many edges touch v — how many other vertices v is directly connected to.
