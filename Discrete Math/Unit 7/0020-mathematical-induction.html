Mathematical Induction

Lesson 19 showed that a recursive definition reaches every case from a base case plus a rule. This lesson turns that exact shape into a proof technique — one capable of proving a claim about infinitely many integers at once, from checking only two things.

1. The principle of mathematical induction

Definition — Mathematical Induction To prove a statement P(n) holds for every integer n ≥ n₀: (1) Base case — prove P(n₀) directly. (2) Inductive step — assume P(k) holds for some arbitrary k ≥ n₀ (the inductive hypothesis), and use that assumption to prove P(k+1). Together, these two pieces prove P(n) for every n ≥ n₀ — the base case starts the chain, and the inductive step shows each case forces the next.
Why these two steps are enough to cover infinitely many cases This is exactly Lesson 19's recursive-definition shape, run in the proof direction instead of the definition direction. The base case proves P(n₀). The inductive step, applied to k = n₀, proves P(n₀+1). Applied again to k = n₀+1, it proves P(n₀+2). And so on — every single case gets proven, one domino knocking over the next, even though you only ever wrote down two arguments (not infinitely many).

2. A full worked proof

Worked example — the sum of the first n positive integers Claim: for all n ≥ 1, 1 + 2 + ... + n = n(n+1)/2.

Base case (n=1): the left side is just 1. The right side is 1(2)/2 = 1. They match.

Inductive step: assume 1 + 2 + ... + k = k(k+1)/2 for some k ≥ 1 (inductive hypothesis). Show it holds for k+1: 1 + 2 + ... + k + (k+1) = [1 + 2 + ... + k] + (k+1) = k(k+1)/2 + (k+1) [using the inductive hypothesis to replace the bracketed sum] = (k+1)(k/2 + 1) = (k+1)(k+2)/2 — which is exactly the claimed formula with n = k+1. ∎

Since both steps hold, the claim is true for every n ≥ 1.

3. Strong induction

Variant — Strong Induction Strong induction changes only the inductive hypothesis: instead of assuming just P(k), assume P(m) holds for every m with n₀ ≤ m ≤ k, then prove P(k+1). This is useful whenever the k+1 case needs to reach back further than just the immediately preceding case — Lesson 15's claim that every tree on n vertices has n−1 edges is exactly this shape, since removing one vertex from a larger tree doesn't always leave a tree of size exactly one smaller in an easily-isolated way without strong induction's full range of smaller cases available.

1. What does the inductive step actually prove?

The inductive step is an implication: assuming P(k) (the inductive hypothesis) lets you derive P(k+1). It never asserts P(k) is true on its own — that's the base case and the chain of implications together that establish it.

2. In the sum-of-integers proof, what role does the inductive hypothesis play?

The whole point of assuming P(k) is to use it as a known fact partway through deriving P(k+1) — here, substituting the assumed formula for 1+2+...+k directly into the sum for k+1.

3. When is strong induction preferred over ordinary induction?

Strong induction only changes what you're allowed to assume — the full range of smaller cases, not just the immediately preceding one — which matters exactly when the k+1 case needs to reach back further than one step.

4. Fill in the blank: skipping the ___ of an induction proof means the chain of implications has nothing to start from, even if every implication itself is correct.

Without the base case established, the inductive step's chain of "P(k) implies P(k+1)" implications is vacuous — none of them ever get triggered, exactly like the widget's middle-domino demonstration.

5. Fill in the blank: in the inductive step, after assuming P(k), the goal is to prove P(___).

The inductive step always targets the very next case, k+1, using P(k) as an assumed tool to get there.
