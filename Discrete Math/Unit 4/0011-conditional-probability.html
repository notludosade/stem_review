Conditional Probability

Lesson 10 computed probability against the whole sample space. This lesson asks a sharper question: given that you already know some event B happened, how does that change the odds of A? The answer is the single most misapplied formula in introductory probability, so this lesson leans hard on one worked example until the mechanics are automatic.

1. Conditional probability

Definition — Conditional Probability The conditional probability of A given B, written P(A | B), is P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), defined whenever P(B) > 0. Intuitively: once you know B happened, the sample space effectively shrinks to just B, and you ask what fraction of that shrunken space is also in A.

2. Worked example — a diagnostic test

P(Disease | Test +) = P(Disease ∩ Test+) / P(Test+) = (45/1000) / (140/1000) = 45/140 ≈ 0.321. A positive test only means a 32% chance of actually having the disease — far lower than most people's intuition, precisely because the disease is rare enough that false positives from the large healthy population outnumber true positives.

3. Independence

Definition — Independent Events A and B are independent if knowing B tells you nothing about A: P(A | B) = P(A). Equivalently (multiplying both sides by P(B)), P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B) — this is exactly Lesson 7's multiplication principle, restated for probabilities of independent events instead of counts of independent choices.
Why the diagnostic-test example is the one worth memorizing The base-rate mistake in that example — assuming a positive test mostly means "has the disease" — is the single most common conditional-probability error in practice, in medicine, spam filtering, and criminal justice alike. The fix is always the same: write down P(A ∩ B) and P(B) as raw counts (or fractions of the total population) before dividing, rather than trying to intuit P(A|B) directly. The contingency table is that fix, made visual.
Formula — Bayes' Theorem Rearranging the conditional probability definition both ways (P(A∩B) = P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A)) gives Bayes' Theorem: P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B). It's the same computation as the contingency table above, just solved algebraically instead of by counting — useful when you're given P(B|A) directly (like a test's true-positive rate) but want P(A|B) (the chance of disease given a positive test).

1. P(A | B) is defined as:

Conditional probability restricts attention to the overlap of A and B, as a fraction of B's own probability — P(A ∩ B) / P(B).

2. In the diagnostic-test example, why is P(Disease | Test+) so much lower than most people expect?

With 950 healthy people and only a 10% false-positive rate, 95 false positives still outnumber the 45 true positives from the much smaller group of 50 sick people — the base-rate effect.

3. A and B are independent exactly when:

Independence means knowing B doesn't change A's probability, which is algebraically equivalent to P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) — not the same as being mutually exclusive (A ∩ B = ∅), a common mix-up.

4. Fill in the blank: from the table (45 true positives, 140 total positives), P(Disease | Test+) = ___ (as a fraction or decimal).

P(Disease | Test+) = 45/140 ≈ 0.321 — true positives divided by all positives.

5. Fill in the blank: the formula P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A)/P(B) is called ___.

Bayes' Theorem rearranges the conditional probability definition to solve for P(A|B) when P(B|A) is what's directly known.
