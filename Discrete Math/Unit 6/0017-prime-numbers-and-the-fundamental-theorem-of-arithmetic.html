Prime Numbers and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Divisibility gave every integer its divisors. This lesson identifies the integers with the fewest possible divisors — primes — and proves they're the unique building blocks every other integer is assembled from, in exactly one way.

1. Prime and composite numbers

Definition — Prime and Composite An integer p > 1 is prime if its only positive divisors are 1 and p itself. An integer n > 1 that is not prime is composite — it has some divisor strictly between 1 and n. 1 is neither prime nor composite: the definition of prime explicitly requires p > 1, so 1 is excluded by definition, not by oversight.
Why 1 is not prime This trips people up because 1's only divisors technically are 1 and itself — it looks like it should qualify. It's excluded on purpose, because including it would break the Fundamental Theorem below: if 1 counted as prime, 12 = 2²·3 and 12 = 1·2²·3 and 12 = 1·1·2²·3 would all be "different" prime factorizations of the same number, destroying uniqueness entirely. Modern mathematics settles this by definition rather than by argument — p > 1 is simply part of what "prime" means — precisely so that factorization stays unique.
Worked example 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 are prime. 4 = 2×2, 6 = 2×3, 9 = 3×3 are composite. 2 is the only even prime — every other even number is divisible by 2 and by itself, so it has a third divisor and can't be prime.

2. The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Theorem — The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic Every integer n > 1 can be written as a product of primes, and this factorization is unique up to the order the primes are listed in. n = p₁ · p₂ · ... · pₖ, where each pᵢ is prime (repeats allowed), and no other multiset of primes multiplies to n.
Worked example 360 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 2³ · 3² · 5. No other combination of primes multiplies to 360 — you can rearrange the order (2·2·2·3·3·5 vs. 3·2·5·2·3·2), but the multiset {2,2,2,3,3,5} is fixed.
Why primes are called the "building blocks" of the integers Every integer greater than 1 is either prime itself, or breaks down into a uniquely-determined product of primes — there is exactly one way to build any given integer out of prime factors, the same way every molecule breaks down into a fixed, unique combination of atoms. This uniqueness is what later makes ideas like "greatest common divisor" and "least common multiple" well-defined in terms of shared or combined prime factorizations.

1. Is 1 a prime number?

1 is neither prime nor composite — it's excluded from "prime" specifically so the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic's uniqueness claim holds.

2. Why does mathematics exclude 1 from being prime, rather than allowing it?

If 1 counted as prime, you could insert arbitrarily many extra factors of 1 into any factorization without changing the product, so the Fundamental Theorem's uniqueness guarantee would fail entirely.

3. What is the prime factorization of 84?

84 = 4 × 21 = 2² × 3 × 7 — every factor must itself be broken down until only primes remain, ruling out 14, 4, and 21 as final factors.

4. Fill in the blank: the only even prime number is ___.

Every other even number is divisible by 2 and by itself — a third divisor — so 2 is the only even number that stays prime.

5. Fill in the blank: the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic guarantees every integer > 1 has a ___ prime factorization, up to reordering.

Uniqueness (up to order) is the theorem's central claim — no integer has two genuinely different multisets of prime factors.
