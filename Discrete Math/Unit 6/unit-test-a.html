1. Does 6 divide 102?

102 = 6 × 17 exactly, with no remainder, so 6 | 102.

2. Applying the Division Algorithm to a = 58, b = 7, what are q and r?

58 = 7(8) + 2, and r = 2 satisfies 0 ≤ r < 7.

3. Is 1 a prime number?

1 is neither prime nor composite — the p > 1 requirement is a deliberate part of the definition, specifically to preserve the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic's uniqueness claim.

4. Fill in the blank: the prime factorization of 60 is 2² · 3 · ___.

60 = 4 × 15 = 2² × 3 × 5.

5. What is 29 mod 6?

29 = 6(4) + 5, so 29 mod 6 = 5.

6. Suppose 5 | a and 5 | b. Answer both parts:

(a) Which of these must also be true?

(b) Fill in the blank: this holds because a = 5k and b = 5m for integers k, m, so a + b = 5(k + ___).

(a) If a and b are both multiples of 5, so is their sum. (b) a + b = 5k + 5m = 5(k + m), so 5 | (a+b).

7. Applying the Division Algorithm to a = −30, b = 4. Answer both parts:

(a) What are q and r?

(b) Fill in the blank: since b = 4 here, r must satisfy 0 ≤ r < ___.

(a) −30 = 4(−8) + 2, and r = 2 is the unique remainder satisfying 0 ≤ r < 4. (b) The Division Algorithm requires 0 ≤ r < b, and here b = 4.

8. Consider prime factorization. Answer both parts:

(a) Why is uniqueness of prime factorization considered "fundamental"?

(b) Fill in the blank: the prime factorization of 98 is 2 · 7² = 2 · ___.

(a) Uniqueness is the theorem's central claim — no integer has two genuinely different multisets of prime factors. (b) 7² = 49, and 2 × 49 = 98.

9. Consider 41 and 17 modulo 8. Answer both parts:

(a) Is 41 ≡ 17 (mod 8)?

(b) Fill in the blank: both 41 and 17 leave remainder ___ when divided by 8.

(a) 8 | 24, confirming the congruence. (b) 41 = 8(5) + 1 and 17 = 8(2) + 1 — both leave remainder 1.

10. Consider arithmetic modulo 7. Answer both parts:

(a) What is (4 + 6) mod 7?

(b) Fill in the blank: (−3) mod 7 = ___.

(a) 4 + 6 = 10, and 10 mod 7 = 3. (b) −3 = 7(−1) + 4, so (−3) mod 7 = 4, since the remainder must stay in [0, 7).
