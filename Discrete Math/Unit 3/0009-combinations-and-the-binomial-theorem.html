Combinations and the Binomial Theorem

Lesson 8 closed on a question: what happens to the count once order stops mattering? This lesson answers it, then shows the resulting numbers arranged into a triangle you may have already seen — and proves it isn't a coincidence.

← Lesson 8 Lesson 10 → Contents Glossary →

1. Combinations

Definition — Combination, C(n, r) A combination of r items chosen from n distinct items, where order does not matter, is counted by C(n, r) = n! / (r!(n − r)!), also written nCr or (ⁿᵣ) ("n choose r"). It equals P(n,r) divided by r! — the permutation count, with the r! orderings of each chosen group collapsed into a single outcome.
Worked example A committee of 3 is chosen from 8 people, with no distinct roles (unlike Lesson 8's podium, where 1st/2nd/3rd were different positions). C(8,3) = 8!/(3!·5!) = (8·7·6)/(3·2·1) = 336/6 = 56 — exactly P(8,3) = 336 divided by 3! = 6, since every group of 3 people can be internally ordered 3! = 6 different ways, all counting as the same committee.

2. Symmetry and Pascal's identity

Two Identities Symmetry: C(n, r) = C(n, n−r) — choosing r items to include is the same count as choosing n−r items to exclude. Pascal's identity: C(n, r) = C(n−1, r−1) + C(n−1, r) — to choose r from n items, either a fixed item is included (choose the remaining r−1 from the other n−1) or it isn't (choose all r from the other n−1); the addition principle from Lesson 7 combines these two mutually-exclusive cases.

3. Pascal's Triangle

Definition — Pascal's Triangle Arranging C(n, 0), C(n, 1), ..., C(n, n) as row n of a triangle (starting with row 0) produces Pascal's Triangle: each row starts and ends in 1, is symmetric left to right (Pascal's identity's symmetry fact), and every interior entry is the sum of the two entries diagonally above it (Pascal's identity itself, applied directly).

4. The Binomial Theorem

Formula — Binomial Theorem (x + y)ⁿ = Σ (k=0 to n) C(n,k) · x(n−k) · yk. Expanding (x+y)ⁿ by hand for large n means tracking, for each term, how many of the n factors of (x+y) contributed a y instead of an x — and "how many of n slots have y" is exactly a combination count, C(n,k), with no ordering involved.
Worked example Row 4 of Pascal's triangle is 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 — exactly C(4,0) through C(4,4). So (x+y)⁴ = x⁴ + 4x³y + 6x²y² + 4xy³ + y⁴, with no multiplying-it-out required once the row is known.

1. A pizza shop lets you pick any 3 of 7 available toppings, with no ranking among them. How many topping combinations are possible?

Order doesn't matter for toppings, so this is a combination: C(7,3) = 7!/(3!·4!) = (7·6·5)/(3·2·1) = 35.

2. Why is C(n, r) always equal to C(n, n−r)?

Every selection of r items to keep simultaneously determines a selection of n−r items to exclude, and vice versa — the two counts describe the same set of outcomes from opposite directions.

3. Row 3 of Pascal's triangle is 1, 3, 3, 1. What is (x + y)³?

The Binomial Theorem pairs row 3's coefficients (1,3,3,1) with descending powers of x and ascending powers of y: x³ + 3x²y + 3xy² + y³.

4. Fill in the blank: C(4,2) = ___.

C(4,2) = 4!/(2!·2!) = 24/4 = 6.

5. Fill in the blank: the identity C(n,r) = C(n−1,r−1) + C(n−1,r), which produces each entry of Pascal's triangle as the sum of the two above it, is called ___.

Pascal's identity is the addition-principle argument that splits "choose r from n" into "fixed item included" plus "fixed item excluded" cases.
