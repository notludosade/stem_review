Permutations

Lesson 7's multiplication principle counted independent choices. This lesson applies it to a specific, extremely common shape of problem: arranging things in order, where each choice slightly shrinks the options left for the next one.

← Lesson 7 Lesson 9 → Contents Glossary →

1. Factorial notation

Definition — Factorial (n!) For a positive integer n, n! = n · (n−1) · (n−2) · ... · 2 · 1 — the product of every positive integer up to n. By convention, 0! = 1 (an empty product — there is exactly one way to arrange zero things: do nothing).

2. Arranging all n items

Rule — Arranging n Distinct Items The number of ways to arrange all n distinct items in a sequence is n!. By the multiplication principle: n choices for the first position, n−1 remaining for the second (since one is used), n−2 for the third, and so on down to 1 choice for the last position.
Worked example How many ways can 5 distinct books be arranged on a shelf? 5! = 5·4·3·2·1 = 120.

3. Arranging r of n items

Definition — Permutation, P(n, r) A permutation of r items chosen from n distinct items, where order matters, is counted by P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)! = n · (n−1) · ... · (n−r+1) — the first r factors of n!, stopping after r terms instead of running all the way down to 1.
Worked example 8 runners race; only 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place are recorded (order matters, and not everyone gets a spot). P(8,3) = 8!/(8−3)! = 8!/5! = 8 · 7 · 6 = 336. Notice this is just the multiplication principle directly: 8 choices for 1st, 7 remaining for 2nd, 6 remaining for 3rd — the n!/(n−r)! formula is a shortcut for exactly that shrinking-choices product, and P(n,n) = n!/0! = n! recovers the "arrange everything" case from before.
Why order matters here, and why that's about to change Every example this lesson used cares which item lands in which position — 1st place is a different outcome from 3rd place, even with the same three runners. Lesson 9 asks the opposite question: what if you only care which r items were chosen, not the order they came in? That single change in what counts as "the same outcome" is the entire difference between a permutation and a combination, and it shows up as dividing out the r! orderings that a combination doesn't distinguish.

1. What is 0! by convention, and why?

0! = 1 by convention, treating it as an empty product — there is exactly one way to arrange nothing (do nothing), which also keeps formulas like P(n,n) = n!/0! = n! working correctly.

2. How many ways can 6 distinct trophies be arranged in a row on a shelf?

Arranging all n distinct items uses n!: 6! = 6·5·4·3·2·1 = 720.

3. 10 sprinters run a race; gold, silver, and bronze medals go to 1st, 2nd, and 3rd place. How many different medal outcomes are possible?

P(10,3) = 10!/(10−3)! = 10 · 9 · 8 = 720 — order matters, since gold, silver, and bronze are distinct positions.

4. Fill in the blank: P(4,4) = 4!/0! = ___.

4! = 24 and 0! = 1, so P(4,4) = 24/1 = 24 — arranging all 4 of 4 items is just 4!.

5. Fill in the blank: a permutation counts arrangements where ___ matters, unlike a combination.

Order is exactly the distinguishing feature: a permutation treats "A then B" as different from "B then A," which is what Lesson 9's combinations will stop caring about.
