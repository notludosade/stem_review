The Counting Principles

Combinatorics is the art of counting a set of outcomes without listing every single one. This lesson gives you the two foundational rules — one for "and," one for "or" — that every other counting formula in this unit is built from.

← Lesson 6 Lesson 8 → Contents Glossary →

1. The multiplication principle

Rule — Multiplication Principle If a task consists of a sequence of k independent choices, and choice i has nᵢ options regardless of the earlier choices, then the total number of ways to complete the whole task is n₁ · n₂ · ... · nₖ. "Independent" is doing the work here: the count of options for each choice can't depend on what was chosen earlier.
Worked example A café offers 3 sizes and 4 flavors, chosen independently. Total distinct orders: 3 · 4 = 12. Extend it: add 2 milk options (independent of size and flavor), and the total becomes 3 · 4 · 2 = 24.
Why this is already familiar Lesson 1 noted that a truth table for n independent statements needs 2ⁿ rows without proving it — this is the proof. Each statement is an independent "choice" between 2 truth values, so by the multiplication principle, n of them produce 2 · 2 · ... · 2 (n times) = 2ⁿ total combinations. The same rule will reappear as the reason a power set has 2ⁿ elements, later in this unit.

2. The addition principle

Rule — Addition Principle If a task can be completed by exactly one of k mutually exclusive methods, and method i has nᵢ ways to do it, the total number of ways is n₁ + n₂ + ... + nₖ. "Mutually exclusive" is the required condition: no outcome should be reachable by two different methods at once, or it gets double-counted.
Worked example A student picks one elective: 5 art classes or 3 music classes (no class counts as both). Total choices: 5 + 3 = 8. If it's "or" and the options overlap, the addition principle as stated over-counts — that correction (inclusion-exclusion) is a standard extension covered in the source reading below.
Telling them apart The fastest test: does the task involve doing all the steps in sequence (multiply), or picking exactly one path out of several alternatives (add)? "Shirt and pants" is multiplication. "Bus or train" is addition. This mirrors Unit 1 exactly — multiplication principle is to ∧ what addition principle is to a mutually-exclusive ∨.

1. A restaurant offers 4 appetizers, 6 entrées, and 3 desserts, chosen independently as a fixed 3-course meal. How many distinct meals are possible?

Each course is chosen independently, so the multiplication principle applies: 4 · 6 · 3 = 72.

2. A student can get to campus by 1 of 2 bus routes or 1 of 3 train routes (no route is both a bus and a train route). How many ways can they get to campus?

These are mutually-exclusive alternatives (a route is a bus route or a train route, never both), so the addition principle applies: 2 + 3 = 5.

3. Which principle applies to a task described as "do step 1, then step 2, then step 3," where each step's options don't depend on earlier choices?

A sequence of independent steps, all of which must happen, is exactly the multiplication principle's shape — "and" across steps means multiply.

4. Fill in the blank: a truth table for 5 independent statements needs ___ rows.

Each statement independently contributes a factor of 2 (T or F), so 5 statements give 2⁵ = 32 rows by the multiplication principle.

5. Fill in the blank: the addition principle requires the alternative methods to be ___ — no outcome reachable by more than one of them.

Without mutual exclusivity, an outcome reachable by two different methods gets counted twice, overstating the true total — this is exactly the gap inclusion-exclusion later patches.
